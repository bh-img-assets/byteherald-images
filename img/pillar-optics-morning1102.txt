<p>Marvell's $115 billion agreement to acquire Lumentum, announced on November 2, 2026, prices a physical shift that has been building for years: AI clusters are moving from copper wires between chips to light over fiber, and from plug-in optical modules at the edge of a switch to optical engines built into the switch package itself. The deal is the news. The reason it makes sense is measured in watts per port, and that math, laid out in one table below, is the citable core of this article: at 800 gigabits per second, vendor figures fall from about 16 watts for a pluggable port to about 3.5 watts for the newest co-packaged design, and at cluster scale that gap is the difference between a power bill and a power problem.</p>

<h2>What pluggable means, and what CPO changes</h2>
<p>CPO stands for co-packaged optics. A pluggable optical module is a hot-swappable component that sits at the front edge of a switch: electrical signals travel over copper traces from the switching chip to the module, the module converts them to light, and fiber carries them onward. The module can be swapped when it fails or when speeds rise, which is why the industry used pluggable modules for decades. Co-packaged optics moves the optical engine into the same package as the switching chip, next to the silicon, so the electrical run shrinks from centimeters of lossy copper to a short, efficient path. The fiber still leaves the package, but the conversion from electricity to light happens where the data is, not at the edge of the box.</p>
<p>That move is why the watt figures fall. Copper traces waste power and lose signal integrity as speeds rise, and the digital signal processor inside a pluggable module adds its own power to clean up the signal. Removing the long copper run and much of that cleanup removes watts at every port. The trade is serviceability: a failed pluggable module is swapped in minutes, while optics inside a package are repaired with the package, not beside it. Any buyer weighing CPO must weigh watts saved against that loss of easy replacement, and the table below gives the watts side of that trade with its sources attached.</p>
<h2>The watt table, with sources attached</h2>
<p>Every figure in this table is a vendor figure, not an independent measurement, and the generations differ, so the table reads as a direction of travel across generations, not as a head-to-head test of same-year products. <a href="https://www.electronicdesign.com/technologies/embedded/article/21248744/electronic-design-broadcoms-first-switch-with-co-packaged-optics-is-cloud-bound">Broadcom's Humboldt switch</a>, its first co-packaged design, was described by Broadcom's vice president of optical systems marketing, Manish Mehta, as using about 7 watts for the same 800 gigabit per second bandwidth that a pluggable module with a 7-nanometer digital signal processor ran at about 16 watts. Broadcom's later Bailly switch, <a href="https://www.nextplatform.com/connect/2025/10/17/the-third-time-will-be-the-charm-for-broadcom-switch-co-packaged-optics/1635588">reported by Next Platform</a>, put an 800 gigabit per second port at 5.5 watts. Broadcom's Davisson design, also reported by Next Platform, put an 800 gigabit per second port at about 3.5 watts, more than 70 percent below pluggable optics at the same bandwidth by Broadcom's account. Next Platform also published a five-year power-cost illustration for 100,000 accelerator units, putting pluggable optics at $1.44 billion against $315 million for co-packaged optics; that illustration is theirs, built on vendor inputs, not ByteHerald's measurement, and it is cited here as their illustration.</p>

<p>The 10,000-port column is ByteHerald arithmetic, nothing more: each watt figure multiplied by 10,000 ports and expressed in kilowatts. It is included because cluster buyers think in ports, not in single-port watts, and the arithmetic makes the scale visible. Moving from 16 watts to 7 watts saves 90 kilowatts per 10,000 ports; moving to 3.5 watts saves 125 kilowatts per 10,000 ports. At 100,000 ports, the same arithmetic gives 1,600 kilowatts against 350 kilowatts. Those totals count only the port optics, not the switch chips, the accelerators, or the cooling, and they should not be quoted as total cluster power. They are the optics slice, and the optics slice is the part CPO changes.</p>
<h2>Who leads, and what Marvell is buying into</h2>
<p>Broadcom leads the co-packaged switch story in public reporting, with Humboldt in deployment, Bailly shipping in volume to hyperscalers, and Davisson as the next design. NVIDIA is the other named leader, with its Spectrum-X co-packaged switches shipping to select partners, and with published claims of 3.5 times to 5 times better optical-interconnect power efficiency, 4 times fewer lasers, and 10 times resiliency, all vendor claims, not third-party measurements. Marvell's deal with Lumentum, announced by <a href="https://investor.marvell.com/news-events/press-releases/detail/1040/marvell-and-lumentum-to-combine-creating-a-category-leader-in-ai-connectivity">Marvell on November 2, 2026</a>, buys the other half of the chain: Lumentum's lasers, transceivers, and optical circuit switching, plus its compound semiconductor manufacturing. Marvell already designs the electrical side; Lumentum supplies the light side. The combined company's stated $550 billion total addressable market is a company estimate, and its promised synergies are expectations, but the strategic logic does not depend on those numbers: whoever controls both sides of the signal path can co-design the package, and co-design is where the watts are saved.</p>
<p>That logic connects to the other bottleneck in AI hardware. Memory decides what a model can hold, and ByteHerald's <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/the-memory-wall-how-hbm-stacks-decide.html">memory wall pillar</a> showed how high-bandwidth memory stacks decide what AI can do. Interconnect decides what a cluster can move, and at the scale of tens of thousands of accelerators, movement is the binding constraint. A cluster with fast memory and slow links waits; a cluster with fast links and slow memory starves. The two pillars are two halves of the same machine, and buyers who track only one will misread both.</p>
<h2>What CPO does not fix</h2>
<p>Three limits should stay attached to every CPO watt claim. First, the figures are vendor figures, measured on vendor hardware under vendor conditions, and independent head-to-head measurements at the same bandwidth, on the same test, are not the basis of this table. Second, the bottleneck moves rather than disappears: optical engines, silicon photonics chips, and advanced packaging capacity decide how fast co-packaged switches ship, and a design that saves watts but cannot be manufactured at volume does not save a cluster. Third, the power saved at the port is real but partial: it does not cut the power of the accelerators themselves, the memory, or the cooling, and ByteHerald's <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/ais-power-and-water-bill-datacenter.html">datacenter resource ledger</a> showed how large those remaining loads are. CPO is a watts-per-port story, not a watts-per-cluster solution, and any claim that treats it as the latter is overselling it.</p>
<h2>The decision guide, by situation</h2>
<p>For an existing fleet running at 400 gigabits per second or below, pluggable modules still win on serviceability and on the fact that the hardware is already paid for; swapping a working fleet for co-packaged switches to save port watts would cost more in hardware than it saves in power. For a new build at 800 gigabits per second and above, where ports number in the tens of thousands and power is the constraint on how many accelerators a site can host, co-packaged optics is the design to evaluate first, with the watt table above as the opening question, not the answer: ask the vendor for the same-bandwidth figure on its current product, in writing, before comparing. For a build that must be serviceable by a small team without package-level repair, wait for a second source and for field failure data, because the first generation of any packaged optics design carries repair risk that a pluggable fleet does not. The Marvell deal does not change those rules; it prices them. A $115 billion bet says the industry believes the watts table will keep moving in CPO's direction, and that the company that owns both sides of the signal path will set the pace. The table above lets a reader check that belief, port by port, rather than take it on faith.</p>
