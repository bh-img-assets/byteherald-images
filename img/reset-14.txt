<p>ByteHerald's evidence ledger on the OpenCode breach separates what is documented from what is merely attributed. The documented record shows exposed provider keys, user charges, key blocks, and refunds. It does not show a proven distillation campaign. That distinction is the article: a credential breach is established, while the motive attached to it remains an unproven claim by the breached company.</p>

<p>Methodology: we included only claims that could be tied to OpenCode's disclosure or to provider actions reported with named companies and dates. User charge anecdotes were used only to establish that misuse was reported, not to estimate total losses, because no provider published a complete loss total. That limit matters: the ledger can prove response actions, but it cannot price the breach.</p>
<h2>What is established</h2>
<p>OpenCode said on October 17, 2026 that a breach related to a large-scale distillation attack affected 1.4 percent of OpenCode Console users and that affected users were notified by email. The exposed credentials were third-party provider keys that some Console users had stored in workspaces. The open-source application and command-line tool were not the affected surface, according to co-founder Dax Raad as reported by TechCrunch on October 19, 2026.</p>
<p>Provider containment is also established. Anthropic and Google blocked keys they believed were exposed. OpenAI worked with OpenCode to restore access and refund API customers for credits used in the attack, and OpenCode said refunds for unauthorized charges were underway. Those actions confirm that providers treated the keys as compromised. They do not, by themselves, confirm why the keys were taken.</p>
<h2>What is not established</h2>
<p>Analysis: the word distillation does more work in OpenCode's statement than the public evidence supports. A distillation attack means collecting model outputs at scale to train another model. Stolen keys could be used for that purpose, but they could also be used for resale, spam, proxy access, or ordinary unauthorized consumption. TechCrunch reported on October 19, 2026 that OpenCode had not detailed how attackers gained access to the older system, how keys were stored, or the full extent of misuse. Without request logs, output-collection evidence, or a provider finding, motive remains attribution.</p>
<p>The breach also should not be generalized to every OpenCode user. The disclosed group was Console users with linked providers, and OpenCode said users who received no email were not impacted. Local key use in the open-source tool is a different risk surface from keys stored in a hosted console.</p>
<h2>The citable point</h2>
<p>Our ledger gives future reporting a clean test. If OpenCode or a provider publishes request-volume evidence, target-model patterns, or a root-cause postmortem, the motive row can change. Until then, the accurate headline is narrower: stored API keys were exposed, providers revoked and refunded, and the distillation explanation remains unproven.</p>
<p>The response pattern is also a security lesson for hosted coding tools. A stored provider key concentrates risk in the host's legacy systems, billing controls, and revocation speed. Users who keep keys local avoid that specific exposure, while users who store keys in a console gain convenience and inherit the console operator's incident response. That tradeoff existed before October 2026, but the provider blocks and refunds made it visible in dated actions rather than in policy language.</p><p>By ByteHerald Staff.
