<p>ByteHerald's price-status ledger finds only one purchasable fact in the Nvidia agent software story: the published AI Enterprise list prices. The roughly $2,000 per GPU per year figure discussed at GTC Berlin on October 22, 2026 was presented as an example of how licensing would work, and Nvidia has not announced which products it covers, which GPUs it applies to, or when it would take effect. Against the published one-year AI Enterprise list price of $4,500 per GPU, the discussed figure is 44.4 percent of list, by our division. That comparison measures distance between a remark and a price list. It does not establish a discount, because the two figures may not cover the same software.</p>
<p>The method separates three evidence classes. Published list prices come from Nvidia's AI Enterprise pricing documentation. Event remarks come from reporting of the GTC Berlin session. Product context comes from the OpenShell Reference Architecture announcement of October 21, 2026. The ledger does not merge those classes into a single price claim. Illustrative fleet arithmetic is labeled as illustration and uses only the two per-GPU figures already in the table.</p>

<p>The context explains why the remark drew attention. Nvidia announced the OpenShell Reference Architecture on October 21, 2026, combining Vera Rubin infrastructure, Vera CPUs and BlueField-4 data processing units running Nvidia Sentry to build, contain, observe and enforce AI agents. The licensing discussion the next day concerned software that runs agents on AI factories, in the wording reported from the session. Analysis: the strategic signal is that agent management software is being framed as a recurring per-GPU charge. The commercial signal is weaker, because no orderable agent licence with that scope and price has been published in the sources used here.</p>
<p>The published ladder also complicates any simple claim that $2,000 is cheap. Nvidia lists AI Enterprise at $4,500 per GPU for one year, $13,500 for three years and $18,000 for five years, with the five-year term priced as four years. Our division puts the five-year term at $3,600 per GPU per year. The perpetual licence lists at $22,500 per GPU with five years of support. Qualified education and Inception pricing lists at $1,125 per GPU for one year, below the discussed figure, but only for qualified institutions and program members. A buyer comparing the $2,000 remark with list prices therefore needs the missing scope before any saving can be calculated.</p>
<p>The fleet illustration shows the size of the unanswered question. At 1,000 GPUs, $2,000 per GPU per year equals $2.0 million per year, while $4,500 equals $4.5 million per year, a difference of $2.5 million per year by our multiplication and subtraction. That illustration assumes the same GPU count and a full year of licensing. It does not assume the same software bundle, support level or licence rights, because Nvidia has not published those terms for the discussed fee. If the eventual agent licence excludes components inside AI Enterprise, the comparison changes. If it replaces part of AI Enterprise, the comparison changes again.</p>
<p>What the evidence does not establish should stay in the article. It does not establish a launch date, a product name for the discussed fee, eligibility by GPU model, or whether existing AI Enterprise customers would pay twice for overlapping rights. It also does not establish that the OpenShell runtime itself carries the discussed charge; OpenShell was announced as a reference architecture with an open source runtime in reporting of the launch. The citable conclusion is narrower and stronger: Nvidia has published per-GPU software prices for AI Enterprise, and separately discussed an approximate agent software figure whose commercial terms remain undisclosed.</p>
