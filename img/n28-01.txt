<p>ByteHerald finding: three September 2026 filings already disclose $2,284.471 billion in selected future commitments that sit outside the balance sheet lines most readers check first. Alphabet accounts for $1,054.9 billion of that filing subtotal, Meta for $810.25 billion, and Amazon for $419.321 billion. The result is not a replacement for the five-company $3.2 trillion tally. It is a narrower filing ledger showing why the headline should be read as unlike promises, not one block of debt.</p><p>The distinction matters because a signed lease that has not started, a purchase commitment for technical infrastructure, and a non-cancelable cloud capacity contract do not constrain a company in the same way. A lease becomes a balance-sheet liability when the asset is available for use. A purchase commitment can bind cash even if demand disappoints. A contingent cloud purchase can shrink if the provider resells capacity. Treating all three as identical debt overstates precision, while ignoring them understates the cash already promised to the AI buildout.</p><h2>ByteHerald filing extraction ledger</h2><p>Methodology: ByteHerald extracted only rows that the filings describe as not reflected on the balance sheet, not yet commenced, non-cancelable, or unconditional purchase obligations. Alphabet is 46.2 percent of the three-company subtotal, Meta is 35.5 percent, and Amazon is 18.4 percent, by ByteHerald arithmetic. Alphabet also has the most purchase-heavy mix in this extraction: $924.4 billion of its $1,054.9 billion subtotal is material purchase commitments, or 87.6 percent. Meta is more balanced between leases not yet commenced and contractual commitments, at 43.9 percent and 56.1 percent. Amazon selected rows are 39.3 percent leases not yet commenced, 38.1 percent property and equipment, and 22.6 percent unconditional purchase obligations.</p><p>Analysis: the risk question is not whether the commitments are real. The filings say they are. The risk question is how fixed each promise is when demand, chip supply, power timing, or model economics change. Alphabet says $213.4 billion of its material purchase commitments is due within 12 months, and that the significant majority will be fulfilled through 2031. Meta says $45.85 billion of its non-cancelable contractual commitments is due in 2026 and $121.41 billion in 2027. Amazon gives a year-by-year schedule for leases not yet commenced, property and equipment, and unconditional purchase obligations. Those timing rows are more useful than a single trillion-dollar headline because they show when cash must leave.</p><p>The evidence does not establish that every dollar in these rows is AI-only. Alphabet includes technical infrastructure and inventory, energy take-or-pay contracts, and content licenses inside the $802.1 billion portion of material purchase commitments. Meta includes consumer hardware products in Reality Labs inside non-cancelable contractual commitments. Amazon unconditional purchase obligations include energy, digital media content, and software. That is why ByteHerald labels this a filing extraction ledger for future commitments tied to the buildout, not a pure AI debt total. Nikkei Asia reported the five-company AI-related total at about $3.2 trillion on November 4, 2026, with about 60 percent purchase commitments and 40 percent lease obligations yet to begin. Our three-company subtotal equals 71.4 percent of that rounded five-company figure, but the definitions differ, so the percentage is context, not reconciliation.</p><p>For investors and competitors, the citable point is narrower and stronger than the headline. By September 30, 2026, Alphabet, Meta, and Amazon had already disclosed $2,284.471 billion in selected off-balance-sheet or not-yet-commenced commitments in the rows extracted here. Any claim that the AI buildout is only current capex misses those signed future payments. Any claim that the full amount is immediate debt also misses the filing language. The next useful disclosure will not be a bigger total. It will be a company-by-company split by start date, cancellation right, and variable pricing, because those three facts decide whether a commitment is a fixed bill or an expensive option.</p><p>By ByteHerald Staff.
