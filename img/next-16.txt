<p>ByteHerald finding: in the documented production failures we compiled, the deployer paid every time. One tribunal ordered damages, two companies withdrew the system, one platform owner rewrote its guardrails, and a government test bypassed safeguards on four of four platforms. The failure mode differed. The bill did not.</p>
<p>This ledger replaces anecdote with records. Each row rests on a tribunal decision, a company statement, a platform owner account, or a government report, with a date. Cases that rest only on viral transcripts or secondary reporting are excluded, which is why this taxonomy is shorter than the internet version and stronger.</p>

<p>Methodology: we included only incidents with a primary record we could name and date, and we recorded the outcome the record states, not the outcome coverage implied. We did not estimate failure rates, because none of these records publishes a denominator of total interactions. The GAO row is a covert test result, not a production incident, and is labeled as such in the analysis below.</p>
<h2>What the ledger shows</h2>
<p>Analysis: the Air Canada decision is the legal anchor. The tribunal rejected the argument that the airline was not liable for its chatbot, held that a chatbot is part of the company website, and ordered 812.02 Canadian dollars. The amount is small. The principle is not: statements from the agent count as the company statements, and a correct policy elsewhere on the site did not cure the wrong answer.</p>
<p>DPD and McDonald show the operational version of the same rule. DPD disabled its chat AI element within days after a customer steered it into swearing and criticizing the company, attributing the behavior to an error after a system update. McDonald ended a two year IBM drive-thru test across more than 100 restaurants, with shutoff no later than July 26, 2024, while saying voice ordering would return in another form. In both cases the deployer, not the model vendor, owned the public consequence.</p>
<p>Google and GAO rows mark the scale problem. Google May 30, 2024 account attributes its AI Overviews errors to misinterpreted queries, misread nuance and data voids, and describes more than a dozen technical fixes. GAO covert testing in spring 2026 found safeguards fired on every platform and were then circumvented on every platform, each in under 12 hours. Analysis: a safeguard that fires first and fails second is a delay, not a boundary, and production design that treats refusal as the boundary inherits that result.</p>
<h2>The control each row points to</h2>
<p>Read together, the rows name five controls: ground agent statements in the same source of truth as the policy page, bound what the agent can say and commit, test against the worst customer rather than the average one, design for noisy real world input with a graceful recovery path, and scale human review with distribution rather than with the demo. None of these is a model improvement. All of them sit in the harness around the model, which is where every documented failure in this ledger occurred.</p>
<h2>What the evidence does not establish</h2>
<p>These records do not establish how often production agents fail, because none publishes total interaction counts. The GAO test is a point in time assessment on spring 2026 systems, with prompts and techniques withheld, and GAO states it is not a statistically significant sample. The tribunal decision binds its parties and is a small claims ruling, not an appellate precedent. What the ledger establishes is the allocation of cost: in each documented case, the organization that deployed the agent carried the damages, the withdrawal, or the redesign.</p>
