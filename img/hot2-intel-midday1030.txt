<p>Intel reported a strong third quarter on October 29, 2026, with revenue of 16.5 billion dollars, up 21 percent from a year earlier, and data center and AI revenue of 7.3 billion dollars, up 78 percent. Then it guided fourth quarter revenue to 15 to 16 billion dollars, well below the 17.1 billion dollars Wall Street expected, and the stock fell more than 20 percent in after-hours trading and more than 25 percent in pre-market trading the next morning. The quarter was not the problem. The guide was.</p>

<h2>Beat versus guide, side by side</h2>
<p>The contrast is stark enough to state plainly, because it is the whole story. On the third quarter itself, Intel beat expectations: revenue of 16.51 billion dollars against a consensus of 16.44 billion dollars, and adjusted earnings of 52 cents a share against a 39-cent consensus, according to <a href="https://www.zacks.com/stock/news/3019377/intel-surpasses-q3-earnings-estimates-on-robust-data-center-demand">Zacks</a>. Data center and AI revenue rose to 7.32 billion dollars from 4.12 billion dollars a year earlier, and Intel called it the segment's strongest growth in 15 years. On the fourth quarter, Intel guided to revenue of 15 to 16 billion dollars and adjusted earnings of 25 cents a share, against expectations of 17.1 billion dollars and 43 cents. The midpoint of the revenue guide, 15.5 billion dollars, sits 1.6 billion dollars, or about 9 percent, below what Wall Street expected, and the earnings guide sits 42 percent below the 43-cent expectation.</p>

<h2>Two businesses moving in opposite directions</h2>
<div class="separator" style="clear: both; text-align: center;"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bh-img-assets/byteherald-images/main/img/hot2-intel-midday1030-chart.png" alt="Bar chart: Intel third quarter revenue change by segment, data center and AI up 78 percent, Foundry up 58 percent, total up 21 percent, client down 5 percent" /></div>
<p>Intel's quarter split in two. Data center and AI, the server business, surged on demand for Xeon processors from hyperscale, enterprise, and neo-cloud customers, with Granite Rapids becoming the largest server product family and Clearwater Forest, the first Intel 18A server product, beginning to generate revenue. Foundry revenue climbed 58 percent to 6.7 billion dollars, although the foundry business still reported an operating loss of 1.85 billion dollars. Client computing and physical AI, the PC business, fell 5 percent to 8.1 billion dollars, down 9 percent sequentially, as memory shortages, higher component prices, and constrained IT budgets weakened PC demand and customers cut purchases to manage inventories.</p>
<p>That split is the mechanism behind the selloff, and it is worth spelling out because it reverses the usual AI story. The same AI buildout that lifted Intel's server business is squeezing its PC business. Memory chips made by Samsung, SK hynix, and Micron are in short supply, memory prices have risen sharply, and that has pushed up the price of laptops and desktops and reduced demand for the Intel chips inside them. Counterpoint Research figures cited in coverage put global PC shipments down 4 percent in the second quarter. Intel's chief financial officer, David Zinsner, said the company sees the PC slowdown continuing at least through the first half of next year, and chief executive Lip-Bu Tan projected a 12 percent contraction in the PC-chip market in 2027. Those are management projections, not established outcomes, but they are the numbers the market traded on.</p>
<h2>Supply problems Intel could not sell through</h2>
<p>Intel also could not fully capitalise on server demand, because supply was constrained. Management cited shortages of some raw materials and a shutdown at its fab in Israel, where an explosion earlier in October killed two workers and seriously injured a third. The incident is expected to cost about 600 million dollars in revenue over two quarters, according to Reuters reporting, with about 300 million dollars of that effect and a 200-basis-point gross margin effect falling in the fourth quarter. Tan said expanding Xeon supply is an immediate priority, which is an unusually blunt admission: demand is not the binding constraint on the server side right now, supply is.</p>
<p>The fourth quarter guide makes that constraint concrete. Management expects data center and AI revenue to grow by a mid-teens percentage sequentially in the fourth quarter, with purpose-built silicon growing faster than the segment overall, while client computing revenue is expected to decline by more than 20 percent sequentially as customers reduce inventory. In other words, the guide does not predict a server collapse. It predicts a PC decline steep enough to drag the total down even as servers grow. That is a very different claim from "AI demand is fading," and it is the claim investors should test against the next two quarters.</p>
<h2>Was the market right?</h2>
<p>A fair verdict needs a falsification test, not just a reaction. If the market was right, the fourth quarter should show client revenue falling more than 20 percent sequentially, the PC slowdown persisting into the first half of 2027, and memory prices staying high enough to keep PC demand soft. If the market was wrong, the fourth quarter should show a shallower client decline, server growth at or above the mid-teens sequential rate management guided to, and Xeon supply easing faster than management suggested. The stock had already fallen about 23 percent in October before the earnings report, on worries about the scale of AI infrastructure investment, even as it remained up sharply for the year. That pre-report fall matters: part of this reaction was a crowded trade unwinding, not a fresh discovery that Intel has problems.</p>
<p>There is also a longer counterweight that the guide does not erase. Tan estimated that agentic workloads could expand the server CPU market from about 40 billion dollars to more than 200 billion dollars over five years. That is a management estimate, not company guidance, and it should be read that way. Intel maintained its 2026 gross capital expenditure forecast of 20 billion dollars and expects spending to increase strongly in 2027, with management warning that 2027 will likely be an investment year without positive free cash flow. A reader who wants the wider version of this pattern, where the market punished guidance rather than the quarter, should read our report on <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/servicenows-worst-day-ever-beat-that.html">ServiceNow's worst day ever</a>, where a beat also crashed the stock on a weaker outlook. And for the spending side of the same AI cycle, our earlier report on <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/gartner-35-trillion-of-ai-spending-may.html">Gartner's AI spending forecast</a> sets out how large the industry-wide bill has become. Intel's story sits inside both: a company caught between a booming server market it cannot fully supply and a PC market being squeezed by the same boom.</p>
