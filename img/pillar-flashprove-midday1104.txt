<p>Anthropic published research on November 2, 2026, that answers a question most AI users never think to ask: how do you know the model that answered you is the model you asked for? Its system, called FlashProve, produces a cryptographic proof that a reply came from a specific set of committed model weights, without revealing those weights. The proof costs about 70 to 95 times the GPU time of running the model normally, which is why this is a research prototype and not a product, and also why the cost table matters more than the headline. A proof that is too expensive to use is a demonstration, and a proof that is cheap enough to check is a new kind of evidence.</p>

<h2>The problem FlashProve solves, and the one it does not</h2>
<p>Today, a user querying a model has almost no way to check that the response came from the specific model a developer claims is responding, rather than a cheaper model substituted by a routing service for cost reasons, or a token stream tampered with by an attacker. Judging from output quality alone is unreliable, Anthropic argues, because a substituted model can still produce high-quality responses. The same gap matters for evaluators, who need to know the model they tested is the model being served, and for clients paying a routing service for tokens from a named model. FlashProve addresses that gap with a zero-knowledge succinct argument, a zkSNARK: the provider first publishes a short commitment, a fingerprint that pins down one exact choice of weights while keeping the weights hidden, and then, for each request, produces a proof that the committed model, run on the given prompt, produces the given output tokens.</p>
<p>What a proof does not do is just as important, and Anthropic states it plainly. A FlashProve proof tells you which weights produced a reply. It says nothing about whether that reply is correct, safe, or unbiased. A provider could prove, perfectly, that a harmful or false answer came from the claimed model. Knowing which agent acted is a different problem from stopping it, which is why <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/ai-agents-need-id-agent-identity-stack.html">the agent identity stack, compared across vendors</a>, answers who an agent is, while FlashProve answers which weights ran. The two are complements, not substitutes: identity without proof can be claimed, and proof without identity can still leave you unable to say which agent, under which permissions, invoked the model.</p>
<h2>The proof-cost table: what Anthropic actually measured</h2>
<p>Anthropic measured FlashProve on Llama 4 Maverick, a 400-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts model with about 17 billion parameters active per token, using publicly available 4-bit weights converted to an integer model so results are bit-exact across machines. The prover ran on a single H100 GPU with 24 CPU cores, and the verifier ran on a CPU with no GPU. Those conditions are the method, and any comparison that drops them is not comparing the same thing.</p>

<p>The overhead range needs reading carefully, because Anthropic reports it two ways. The headline range, 70 to 95 times, summarizes the GPU-time cost of proving against running the model on vLLM, a common open-source inference library. The detailed comparison ran vLLM with the FP8 release of the same model on eight H100 GPUs, and found proving a batch of 24 requests took about 72 times longer per GPU than vLLM processing the same requests at once, with 66 to 77 across runs, and about 94 times longer when vLLM ran at the highest load tried. A reader who quotes only 70 times, or only 94 times, without the batch and hardware conditions, is quoting a number without its method. The honest summary is that proving costs roughly two orders of magnitude more GPU time than serving, on Anthropic's own benchmark, and that no independent reproduction has yet confirmed it.</p>

<h2>Where a proof would actually be used</h2>
<p>Anthropic names four uses, and each one tests a different weakness in the current system. First, pacing: a regulator that requires hardware to be devoted to inference, rather than training a new model, could ask for certificates covering requests served in a period, showing the claimed model really served them. Anthropic adds the caveat that this verifies inference, not that the hardware is not training on the side. Second, tamper detection: a provider could require a valid proof on every response, so a compromised server, or a stronger model substituting itself for a weaker one to dodge monitoring, cannot hide behind a plausible reply. Third, text provenance: a developer could claim a frontier model scanned a library for vulnerabilities, or approved a business plan, and a proof would let a third party check that claim without rerunning the model. Anthropic even sketches a proof-of-failure, showing a model spent at least a stated number of tokens trying, and did not find a bug in a patch. Fourth, model approval: a regulator that requires only approved models to be served could check proofs instead of trusting a provider's word.</p>
<p>Those uses share a pattern worth naming. A proof is most valuable where the person checking cannot rerun the work, either because they lack the hardware, lack access to the weights, or lack clearance. That is also why a watermark is not a substitute. A watermark tries to detect AI output after the fact, by a signal hidden in the text, and detection can fail or be stripped. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/how-ai-watermarking-actually-works-and.html">How watermarking actually works, and where it breaks</a>, showed that detection depends on the signal surviving editing and on a detector that can read it. A cryptographic proof, by contrast, is checked against a published commitment before anyone has to trust the text, but it only works if the provider publishes that commitment and produces proofs in the first place. The choice is therefore not proof versus watermark in the abstract. It is a checkable commitment from the provider versus a detectable signal in the output, and a serious system may eventually need both.</p>
<h2>How much faster is this than what came before</h2>
<p>Anthropic's prior-work section makes the progress concrete, and it also shows why cross-system comparisons need care. The only earlier proof of the same model, Llama 4 Maverick, that Anthropic cites is VerInf, at about 0.02 tokens per second, with 14.3 hours for 1,000 tokens on a DGX Spark, a 93.6 gigabyte proof, and almost a day to verify. FlashProve, on the same model, reports 110 tokens per second on a single H100, with about 19 megabyte proofs verified in seconds. That is a genuine step change on the same model, not a cherry-picked comparison across model sizes. By contrast, Attestable, a commercial effort Anthropic cites, reports 53 to 77 tokens per second for a dense 31-billion-parameter Gemma model on a single H100, with no open-source library released at the time of writing. That figure is not comparable to FlashProve's 110 tokens per second on a 400-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts model, because model size, architecture, and proof coverage differ, and Anthropic does not present it as a race. A reader who ranks FlashProve above Attestable, or below it, from those two numbers alone is making the exact error Anthropic's prior-work section warns against.</p>
<p>Formal verification is the second half of the claim, and it has its own limits. Anthropic used the Lean proof assistant to specify the protocol and prove three top-level properties, completeness, soundness, and zero knowledge, in a machine-checkable form, with an interactive visualizer to help audit the theorems. The fast C++ verifier that users would run day to day carries no formal guarantee itself, though Anthropic says it agrees with the Lean verifier on every proof in its tests. And the formal statements still have to be audited by hand to ensure they match their intent, while the executable code has to be audited to ensure it matches the formally defined protocol. Formal verification narrows the trust gap, from trusting all the code to trusting the verifier and the audited statements, but it does not close it, and Anthropic does not claim otherwise.</p>
<h2>The five-question test for any verified-AI claim</h2>
<p>That is the citable tool this pillar leaves behind. When any vendor, auditor, or regulator claims an AI reply is verified, ask these five questions, in order, and do not accept the claim until all five have answers. First, which exact weights were committed, and where is the commitment published? Second, what token window does the proof cover, and is it the full reply or a sample? Third, is the proof for greedy decoding, the model's single most likely next token, or for sampled decoding, where randomness can make a reply depart from the proved computation? Fourth, what does the verifier need, in hardware, downloads, and time, to check the proof independently? Fifth, what is explicitly not proved, including correctness, safety, bias, and anything outside the proved window? FlashProve, on Anthropic's own reporting, answers the first four with a published commitment, a 1,024-token window, greedy decoding on an integer model, and a CPU verifier needing about 6.5 megabytes plus the proof, and it answers the fifth honestly: correctness, safety, and the served model's architecture are not part of the guarantee, side-channel leaks from proof timing are out of scope, and contexts beyond 1,024 tokens are not yet covered. A future system that claims verification without answering those five questions is selling the word, not the property.</p>
<p>The limitations, stated naturally because they decide whether this research becomes a product, are the following. The proof covers an integer version of the published weights under greedy decoding, so a reply from a floating-point implementation, or from sampled decoding, could depart at some position and no longer exactly match the proved computation. The architecture is not hidden, so a provider that wants to keep its mixture-of-experts structure secret gets no help here. Side-channel attacks, such as measuring proof timing to infer weights, are out of scope for this release. Contexts are capped at 1,024 tokens, against chat contexts that regularly run to hundreds of thousands or millions of tokens. And Anthropic describes the release as intermediate progress, not a completed research agenda, with code, Lean proofs, and documentation under an Apache 2.0 license, released so others can improve it. Those are not footnotes to the breakthrough. They are the conditions under which the breakthrough is true.</p>
