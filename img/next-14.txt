<p>ByteHerald finding: the citable part of this story is not the six hours. It is the verification package. Our ledger shows a solve that can be re-run and checked: a published transcription, a key, a regeneration script, and an independent database maintainer who marked the entry solved. The model run is the least checkable number in the story.</p>
<p>The document is a coded military briefing sent on Napoleon orders through Eugene de Beauharnais to General Auguste de Marmont in March 1809. It survived as a low resolution scan in a 1969 French army journal and sat on Cryptiana list of unsolved historical ciphers for decades. SentinelOne engineer Carter Church gave GPT-6 Astra one scanned image and one goal in September 2026.</p>

<h2>What the ledger shows</h2>
<p>Analysis: the partial key changes the shape of the claim. The model did not start from zero. A partial key catalogued by cryptography historian Daniel Tant already covered 33 letter values, about a fifth of the distinct signs, and the model contribution included finding that key and connecting it to this letter. The remaining work, about 122 sign types by our arithmetic, was solved by a simulated annealing search scored against French language statistics. That is still a substantial automated workflow. It is not a cold break of an unknown system.</p>
<p>The ambiguity row is the honest edge of the solve. Five signs appear only once, so their values cannot be confirmed by repetition. Church also reran the analysis with Napoleon and Marmont material stripped from the reference texts and reported the same reading, a check against the model recalling the answer from surrounding sources. The decoded text lists French and allied troop positions and completes a sentence that breaks off in Napoleon 1865 memoir, which is historical consistency evidence, though the letter is a paraphrase of orders rather than Napoleon exact words.</p>
<h2>Why verification carries the claim</h2>
<p>A wrong key cannot produce coherent, historically consistent French across 1,300 units, and the published script lets anyone regenerate the reading and test it against the ciphertext. Tomokiyo review and the Cryptiana status change give the solve an external check by the maintainer of the list where the cipher sat unsolved. Nature reported the same sequence in October 2026 and noted Church own point that the message could have been solved without AI, with the model reducing time and effort.</p>
<p>Analysis: that distinction matters for every AI discovery claim that follows. Transcription, literature search, solver construction and historical cross checking ran as one workflow, which is the real capability on display. But capability claims age badly without artifacts. Here the artifacts exist, so the claim survives independent of how the six hours are interpreted.</p>
<h2>What the evidence does not establish</h2>
<p>The record does not establish that the original French codebook has been recovered, and the five single occurrence signs remain uncertain. No peer reviewed journal assessment is reported in the sources we compiled. The six hour figure excludes Church own evenings of work around the run. What is established is narrower and stronger: a mechanically testable key and plaintext, published for re-running, and accepted as solved by the database that listed the cipher as unsolved.</p>
