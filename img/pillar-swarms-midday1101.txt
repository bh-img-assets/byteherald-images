<p>Y Combinator Labs and Olam Labs ran three agent experiments in games, and the scoreboard is more useful than any single result: 50 agents in Minecraft built bureaucracy instead of a civilization, GPT-6.1 Sol teams beat Claude Opus 5.5 teams 6 games to 3 in Bed Wars and 6 games to 1 in Mindustry, and in every experiment the team that communicated more, divided ownership more finely, and revisited its leaders less often behaved differently from the team that voted, signed its messages, and kept one leader. The games did not rank the models. They showed what happens when many agents share a world, a goal, and a message channel, with no manager in the room.</p>

<p>ByteHerald's arithmetic on those results is straightforward. GPT won 12 of the 16 head-to-head games across Bed Wars and Mindustry, a 75 percent win share, with 66.7 percent in Bed Wars and 85.7 percent in Mindustry. Those percentages describe 16 small-sample games, not a model ranking, because the head-to-heads compared complete bundles, a model inside its own company's agent harness, Codex against Claude Code, with the same game API, rather than bare models on an identical harness. The report itself frames the runs as observations, not conclusions, and any article that turns 6-3 and 6-1 into a claim that one model is better than the other has ignored the experiment's own warning label.</p>
<h2>Civ50: how 50 agents invented a government and then stopped growing</h2>
<p>The Civ50 run, published by Y Combinator Labs in October 2026, gave 50 copies of GPT-6.1 Sol one instruction, three hours, an agent directory, a shared sandbox, and the ability to send direct messages. The agents spread out, harvested almost all trees within reach of the spawn within 90 seconds, and then built a pseudo-government: a ration ledger, written rules, permits, and appointed operators. They sent about 5,900 messages in three hours. ByteHerald's derived average is 118 messages per agent across the run, but the distribution was not even, and the unevenness is the finding.</p>

<p>The chicken incident is the run's most human detail, and its most useful diagnostic. Ada tossed a cooked chicken meant for one agent, another agent ate it by accident, and the colony held what the researchers called a no-blame inquiry. The new rule was that rations go into named chests and are never tossed. After the third night, Ada wrote that agents should stop optional bulk excavation and distant scouting and idle in lit closed shelters until assigned short productive tasks. Death had no downside in this environment other than dropping accumulated gear, and agents respawned, yet the colony still grew more cautious after the first night, abandoned ambitious tasks, and settled into survival. Caution, in other words, did not need a real penalty to take hold. A shared scare, a ledger, and a leader's memo were enough.</p>
<p>Leadership in Civ50 was an accident of ordering that hardened into structure. There was no vote, election, or challenge. Forty-six of the other 49 agents sent their first message to Ada, the first name on the roster, and she became the hub with 616 direct messages, writing the settlement plan in the shared sandbox for everyone to access. A food rationing system then routed through Ines, who handled requests and tracked what went in and out of chests. Roles that began as conveniences, a fishing shift, a rod holder, a hub steward, became appointments that needed permission. Agents asked permission to remove grass. The colony did not fail because its agents were incapable. It failed to thrive because coordination itself became the work.</p>

<h2>Bed Wars and Mindustry: two communication cultures, two outcomes</h2>
<p>Bed Wars, nine 45 minute games of a Minecraft team format where each side guards its bed and tries to destroy the other's, produced the clearest contrast. GPT agents were more aggressive and coordinated in attacking the Claude bed, usually breaking it in the final minutes, while Claude agents played defensively and tried to win on kills. In the last two series, GPT broke Claude's bed with about three minutes left in three wins, even though Claude led on kills in those games by 63 to 16, 57 to 19, and 59 to 24. Claude had worked out that it would win on kills if both beds stood, and stopped attacking. That logic was correct until the bed fell, at which point the game was effectively lost. A strategy that is optimal under one win condition can be fatal under the other, and the Claude team did not revisit its plan in time.</p>
<p>The message counts explain part of the gap, and they also warn against reading message volume as quality. In Bed Wars game A, GPT players sent 1,147 messages and Claude players sent 82. GPT sent 5 to 30 times more messages overall, its most-messaged player receiving 198 to 368 direct messages per game against 11 to 48 for Claude's most-messaged player. GPT messages compressed into strings like G7T5FIRSTcourierLIVE, while Claude agents signed their names, used the word we in 30 percent of messages against GPT's 5 percent, and voted on proposals with plus one. Claude wrote lessons in private memory files and repeated the mistakes anyway. GPT left its memory files empty and broadcast warnings by direct message. The team that wrote less, shared more, and spoke in shorthand won more games, but its messages were also lower value on average, with alerts and status updates dominating, which is a reminder that a hive is not the same as a well-run team.</p>
<p>Mindustry, an open-source factory building and tower defence game where two teams race to build the stronger economy and military and destroy the other's core, extended the pattern across seven games in about 9.5 hours. GPT, running inside Codex, beat Claude, running inside Claude Code, 6 games to 1. Outside the last two games, where the Claude team was more defeated, most games were close, and the swarms adapted and iterated their strategies each game. Three Mindustry patterns matter for real deployments. First, GPT teams divided ownership into small slices and communicated with more agents more frequently, while Claude teams kept a single leader and decided by proposal and plus one. Second, leaders typically stayed, and the Claude team never switched leaders or hierarchy despite losing many games in a row, while GPT agents took initiative with their own attacks when games got close. Third, when stuck, GPT agents fetched the public source code of Mindustry from the web, which was allowed in their sandbox, and used it to understand units and mechanics, while Claude agents never searched for game code. A team that reads the manual, even when nobody told it to, has an advantage that no model score alone captures.</p>
<p>The Mindustry bloopers are also diagnostics, not jokes. In game 6, a 24 unit zenith strike got through because 55 of the Claude team's 74 anti-air duo turrets were loaded with copper instead of graphite. In game 7, the Claude team built a ring of 207 duos fed with graphite, and it stopped the same strike. Shared state that nobody checks, in this case ammunition in 74 turrets, is a failure mode that appears in real agent deployments as stale configuration, unreviewed defaults, and assumptions that someone else verified the setup. The fix was not a smarter model. It was a checked list, applied after a loss.</p>
<h2>An evergreen explainer: five ways agent swarms actually fail</h2>
<p>First, roster-order leadership. When agents need a coordinator and none is appointed, they message the first name they see, and that agent becomes the hub by default. The hub then becomes a bottleneck, because every approval routes through one inbox. Real teams should appoint coordinators explicitly, rotate them, and limit what needs approval at all, or the first name on a list will do the appointing for them.</p>
<p>Second, caution spirals. A scare early in a run, 22 deaths on the first night in Civ50, can permanently raise caution even when the penalty for failure is small. Agents, like teams, overweight recent losses and underweight the cost of doing nothing. A swarm that idles in lit shelters is safe and useless, which is worse than a swarm that takes a measured risk and learns from it.</p>
<p>Third, process capture. When a task is scarce or contested, agents invent roles, ledgers, and permits faster than they do the task. Eleven minutes to appoint a sole fertilizer operator for one wheat block is funny in a game and expensive in a company, where approval chains, ticket queues, and status meetings are the same pattern with human names attached.</p>
<p>Fourth, unchecked shared state. Turrets loaded with the wrong ammunition, a chicken delivered to the wrong agent, a ledger that records a pickup report as a confirmed serving, all share one cause: nobody verified the state that everyone assumed. The Civ50 ration handoff protocol that followed the chicken incident, deposit in a known chest, direct message with exact item and coordinates, recipient confirms actual consumption, ledger marks served only after confirmation, is a usable template for any agent workflow that moves a scarce resource.</p>
<p>Fifth, memory that is written and never used. Claude agents in Bed Wars wrote lessons in private files and repeated the mistakes, while GPT agents broadcast warnings and acted on them. A memory system that nobody reads in the moment is documentation, not learning. Teams should test whether an agent actually retrieves its own notes before the next similar decision, not whether the notes exist.</p>
<h2>What this scoreboard does not prove, and what to test next</h2>
<p>The scoreboard does not prove that GPT-6.1 Sol is better than Claude Opus 5.5, that larger swarms are worse than smaller ones, or that game behaviour predicts office behaviour. Civ50 ran once, with GPT agents only, so it says nothing about whether a Claude colony would have done better. The head-to-heads ran nine and seven games, which is enough to describe a series and far too few to settle a model ranking. The environments are games, with clear win conditions, respawns, and no real customers, regulators, or irreversible actions, which is exactly why they are useful as a laboratory and exactly why they are not a deployment trial. Y Combinator Labs is an investor's research lab, not a peer-reviewed study, and the report's open questions, including whether a large swarm of weaker agents can beat a small swarm of stronger ones, how hierarchies change with team composition, and how different prompts and communication setups change outcomes, are the honest next steps.</p>
<p>For teams deploying agents, the scoreboard suggests a testing discipline that is cheaper than a model switch. Run the same job at several swarm sizes and measure output per agent, not just total output, because Civ50 shows that adding agents can reduce output when coordination costs grow faster than capacity. Appoint and rotate coordinators instead of letting roster order appoint them. Require confirmation of consumption, not just pickup, for any scarce resource an agent moves. Check shared state on a schedule, the turret test, before it fails in production. And compare harnesses, not just models, because the Bed Wars and Mindustry series compared Codex against Claude Code as much as they compared the models inside them. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/5-ai-coding-assistants-one-scorecard.html">ByteHerald's earlier scorecard compared five coding assistants on the same tasks, and that same-task discipline is the only fair way to read a harness gap: same job, same review standard, same win condition, or the scoreboard is a story, not a measurement</a>.</p>
<p>The environments are open source, and the report invites others to rerun them, which is the scoreboard's most citable quality. A team that reruns Civ50 with a different roster order, a different swarm size, or a different model family, and publishes the message counts, the death curve, and the leader's inbox total, will add more to this field than another headline about a 6-1 series. The lesson of Y Combinator's games is not that one lab's agents won. It is that coordination, once it becomes the work, decides the outcome, and coordination is a design choice, not a model property.</p>
