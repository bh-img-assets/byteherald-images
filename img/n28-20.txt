<p>ByteHerald graded six AI shutdown designs against one question: can anyone actually stop a deployed frontier model, and how fast? The answer from the primary texts is zero. None of the six designs in force or proposed gives a government standing authority to take a dangerous model offline quickly. The fastest real mechanism is a lab choosing to stop its own model under a voluntary pledge made in May 2024.</p>

<h2>Three different switches</h2>
<p>The word kill switch covers three mechanisms. A release block stops a model from shipping. An API shutdown pulls a model already serving users. Weight deletion or compute seizure removes the artifact itself. The Anthropic draft, as reported, is a release and use suspension ordered by a judge after an evaluator process and a 72-hour notice to Commerce. SB 53 requires safety frameworks, transparency reports and incident reporting, with civil penalties up to $1 million per violation, and contains no shutdown authority at all. The EU AI Act is a product-safety law: authorities can require corrective action and restrict, withdraw or recall non-compliant systems, which is market removal through process, not an emergency halt of a model mid-crisis.</p>
<p>The Seoul Frontier AI Safety Commitments are the only text that promises a halt, and the promise runs to the company itself: signatories commit not to develop or deploy a model if mitigations cannot keep risks below their own thresholds. That is fast because it needs no court. It is also voluntary, self-graded, and unenforceable by any regulator.</p>
<h2>What the ledger does not establish</h2>
<p>Analysis: this grading measures legal authority, not technical capability. Labs can shut down their own hosted models today; nothing here disputes that. The gap is authority over a lab that refuses, or over weights already distributed outside a lab's control, which no design in the ledger reaches. The Anthropic draft's details come from reporting on an unpublished text, so its grading carries more uncertainty than the enacted laws.</p>
<p>The November 18, 2026 California recommendations would close part of the gap with a state emergency order effective while courts review. They are recommendations, not law, and their speed claim is untested. Until a legislature enacts something like them, the honest summary of the ledger stands: the switch everyone describes does not exist in law. That distinction is the citable point of this map, because public debate keeps treating proposals, pledges and disclosure statutes as if they were the same instrument. They are not, and the difference decides who acts in the first 72 hours of a crisis.</p>
