<p>ByteHerald's timeline of the Meta terms dispute reaches a narrower conclusion than the October headlines did: the crawling clause is paused, not withdrawn, and the legal fight has moved from contract wording to competition law. Our ledger below is compiled from Meta's own notices, the UK regulator's statement, and court reporting dated between October 15 and November 5, 2026. It shows a proposal that changed three times in three weeks and then stopped without a new date.</p>

<p>Methodology: we counted only dated public records that changed the legal status of the clause: a preview, a wording revision, a date change, a court order, a regulator opening, and a pause notice. Commentary and publisher statements were excluded unless they carried a dated Meta or regulator action. That rule is why the table has six rows rather than every reaction published during the dispute.</p>
<h2>What the timeline establishes</h2>
<p>First, the October 30 deadline never arrived. Meta moved the date on October 23, 2026, then paused the updates on November 5, 2026. The pause covers four documents: the Commercial Terms, Product Catalog Terms, Self-Serve Ad Terms, and Ad Creative Generative AI Terms, according to Meta's Business Help Center notice of November 5, 2026. Existing terms remain in effect.</p>
<p>Second, the dispute changed legal category. The Oslo injunction of October 28, 2026 treated the terms as a legality question for Norwegian media companies. The CMA investigation opened October 30, 2026 asks a different question: whether requiring businesses to accept the terms could amount to an unlawful imposition of unfair trading conditions under Chapter II of the Competition Act 1998. That is an abuse-of-dominance frame, not only a copyright frame.</p>
<p>Third, the wording fight did not settle the consent fight. Removing the word train on October 17, 2026 narrowed one sentence, but publishers still faced a contract tied to business accounts. The later court and regulator actions were aimed at that tie: whether a business can refuse automated access without losing Meta business services.</p>
<h2>What the evidence does not establish</h2>
<p>Analysis: the pause does not prove Meta abandoned the clause. Meta's November 5, 2026 notice gives no replacement date and does not say a future version will drop the crawling language. It also does not settle how Meta uses content it already accesses through other products. Those are separate questions, and the timeline should not be read as a final publisher victory.</p>
<p>The CMA investigation also has made no finding of wrongdoing. Its October 30, 2026 statement opens an inquiry; it is not a ruling. The Oslo order is temporary and Norwegian in scope. A reader who treats either action as a global ban on Meta crawling would be going beyond the record.</p>
<h2>Why this ledger matters</h2>
<p>For publishers, the practical question is no longer what happens on a fixed October date. It is what Meta files next, and whether regulators require a real refusal option. Our timeline gives that next step a baseline: any revised clause can be compared against the October 17 wording, the October 23 delay, the October 28 injunction, the October 30 CMA opening, and the November 5 pause. If a future version returns without a publisher choice over crawling, the competition-law issue identified by the CMA remains live.</p>
