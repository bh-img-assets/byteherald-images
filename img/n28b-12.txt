<p>ByteHerald's structure ledger finds that the Brown power map proves the shape of AI's labor chain, not its size. The map links 13 data-labeling companies to 141 corporate customers across 31 countries with worker sites. Our arithmetic puts that at about 10.8 customers per firm and 2.4 countries per firm, with the 13 firms forming just 7.0 percent of the 185 mapped nodes.</p>
<p>That concentration is the finding. A thin intermediary layer sits between a large customer base and a dispersed workforce. The map does not count workers, wages, or hours, and its author presents it as illustrative rather than a census.</p>
<h2>The ByteHerald structure ledger</h2>
<p>Brown senior Cunyan Ma built the map from existing databases of labeling firms and customers plus her own database of delivery centers, used as a proxy for worker location. We computed only ratios of the three published counts. A ratio describes the drawing's structure; it does not describe any firm's headcount.</p>

<p>The proxy limit is decisive. A delivery center marks where a firm operates a site; it does not establish how many people work there, what they are paid, or which customer's tasks they perform. A mapped link between a customer and a firm documents a relationship in the underlying databases, not a volume of work.</p>
<p>The map's value is that it makes an opaque chain inspectable. Before it, the chain from a corporate customer to a labeling firm to a worker site was mostly asserted in individual accounts. The drawing connects those accounts to a structure that buyers, journalists and workers can question node by node.</p>
<h2>What the evidence does not establish</h2>
<p>Analysis: the counts do not establish the global size of the data workforce, conditions at any specific site, or the share of any customer's AI labor handled by a mapped firm. The project is a public education tool built through Brown's Center for Technological Responsibility, Reimagination and Redesign, and its coverage will change as databases grow.</p>
<p>The citable result is structural: 13 firms, 141 customers, 31 countries, and a documented method with a stated proxy. Claims about scale or conditions need payroll, contract, or site-level evidence the map does not contain.</p>
<p>Ma describes the extraction of difficult, low-paid data work from the Global South for Western profit as AI colonialism. We record that as the project author's framing, attributed to her, rather than as a finding of the counts.</p>
<p>The work itself spans transcription, chatbot response rating, medical scan labeling, and filtering disturbing content, according to Brown's report. That range matters for reading the map: a single node can represent routine annotation and psychologically harmful moderation at once, and the structure alone cannot distinguish the risk carried by each task type.</p>
<p>The customer count needs the same care as the firm count. One hundred forty-one corporate customers means 141 relationships recorded in the underlying databases, not 141 equal buyers. A customer with one small labeling contract and a customer outsourcing continuous moderation both appear as one node. The map can therefore rank connectedness, but it cannot rank dependence, spending, or responsibility for conditions at a site.</p>
<p>Ma's stated next step, extending power mapping to semiconductor labor, would test whether the same intermediary pattern appears in a second AI supply chain. That extension matters methodologically: if a different industry with different firms produces a similar thin intermediary layer, the structure is a feature of how AI production is organized, not an artifact of one database.</p>
