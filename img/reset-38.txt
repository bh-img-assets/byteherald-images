<p>ByteHerald's power-and-water ledger finds the November update raised the power line and gave the water line a denominator. The International Energy Agency's World Energy Outlook 2026, published November 19, 2026, puts data centre electricity use at about 1,000 terawatt-hours in 2030 under its Stated Policies Scenario, with capacity built for AI driving about three-quarters of the growth and associated emissions on track to rise by 70 percent by 2030. The same outlook puts water linked to data centres at 885 billion litres in 2025, of which 240 billion litres was used inside the buildings.</p>
<p>The asset is a revision ledger: the April 2026 projection this article originally used, set against the November 19, 2026 outlook, with ByteHerald arithmetic only where two published figures are divided or subtracted. It keeps scenario projections labeled as projections. The IEA states that its scenarios are not forecasts, and the Stated Policies Scenario counts policies governments have put forward even if they are not yet law.</p>

<p>Methodology: ByteHerald did not model electricity demand or water use. We placed two IEA publications side by side and performed two arithmetic operations: 1,000 minus 950 terawatt-hours for the revision, and 240 divided by 885 billion litres for the direct water share. Every other figure is the IEA's. The 2025 electricity range is stated as a range because the April update and November outlook round the same year differently; presenting one decimal would invent precision neither publication claims.</p>
<p>The water split changes how the local argument should be read. Most of the 885 billion litres sits outside the server hall, in electricity generation and supply chains, while the direct share is about 27.1 percent by our arithmetic. A community fight over a specific facility is therefore about the direct share and about siting: the IEA puts about 30 percent of existing and planned capacity in high water stress areas. A global total cannot settle a local permit, and a local permit cannot settle the global total.</p>
<p>What this does not establish: it does not prove that 1,000 terawatt-hours will occur, because a scenario is not a forecast. It does not allocate the total to any single company, model or query. It does not show that efficiency gains fail; it shows that, in the IEA's stated-policies path, demand growth outruns the efficiency improvements embedded in that scenario. Company-level disclosure remains uneven, so the ledger cannot rank operators by water or power intensity from public data alone.</p>
<p>Analysis: the November revision tightens the original conclusion rather than reversing it. The power projection moved up by about 5.3 percent, the emissions path rose with it, and water acquired a global denominator that separates direct use from value-chain use. The citable ByteHerald contribution is the revision and the split: about 50 terawatt-hours added between IEA publications, and about 27.1 percent of linked water used inside the buildings.</p>
