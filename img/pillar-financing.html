AI infrastructure is usually reported as spending: so many billions in capital expenditure, so many gigawatts, so many chips. The financing behind it is reported deal by deal, and nobody puts the instruments side by side. This ledger does that. It compares five financing deals announced between October 14 and November 2, 2026, on the only questions that matter to anyone who has to judge them: what instrument, how much, what it costs, and what happens if it fails. The answer, in one line, is that there is no single way AI is being paid for. There are four, and they fail in different ways.

ByteHerald's own sums, labeled as such: the three cash-like financings in this ledger, Microsoft's 25 billion dollars, Oracle's 20 billion dollars, and SpaceX's 40 billion dollar lease principal, total 85 billion dollars announced in ten days, from October 24 to November 2. Marvell's 115 billion dollars is deliberately excluded from that total, because an enterprise value in stock is not cash raised, and adding it would overstate the money actually funding construction. CoreWeave's 1.25 billion dollar bond is also excluded from the new money total, because it was issued in June; its October repricing is a warning signal, not a new raise.

Four instruments, four different risks

Bonds are the cheapest money for the strongest borrowers, and the most punishing for the weakest. Microsoft, rated AAA, priced 25 billion dollars across ten tranches with coupons from 4.85 percent to 6.35 percent, the longest maturing in 2066, and reported orders above 100 billion dollars, more than four times the offering. That is a borrower being paid to take its time. CoreWeave is the same instrument at the other end of the market: a 9.625 percent bond issued at par in June traded below 80 cents by mid October, pushing its yield above 15 percent, while five year credit default swaps reached about 1,200 basis points, a market implied default probability near 60 percent over five years. The coupon on CoreWeave's bond did not change, and the company does not suddenly owe 15 percent on old debt, but any new borrowing it attempts will be priced off that distressed level. Same instrument, opposite verdict, decided by the balance sheet behind it.

Equity is the instrument for borrowers who cannot afford more debt. Oracle already carried 125.34 billion dollars in borrowings at August 31, 2026, against 67.36 billion dollars of fiscal 2026 revenue, so half of its 20 billion dollar raise is new shares. The cost is dilution, about 76.5 million shares at the October 30 reference price of 130.67 dollars, and the benefit is cash with no interest bill and no maturity date. Marvell's deal is equity taken further: no cash changes hands at all, and Lumentum holders take 4.500 Marvell shares per share instead. The risk moves with it. Because the ratio is fixed, a 20 percent fall in Marvell's price before the expected first half 2028 close cuts the value Lumentum holders receive by 20 percent, while the 67 to 33 ownership split stays stable. Termination fees, 5.8 billion dollars payable by Marvell and 2.9 billion by Lumentum in specified cases, plus a 3.2 billion dollar regulatory fee for Marvell in specified antitrust failures, put a price on walking away, but they do not fix the dollar value.

Leases are the newest instrument in this set, and the least understood. SpaceX did not borrow 40 billion dollars in the ordinary sense. An Apollo managed entity buys Nvidia Vera Rubin chips and related equipment, then leases them to SpaceX for about five to five and a quarter years per delivered tranche, with quarterly payments covering principal plus a rate based amount that had not been set at announcement. SpaceX chief financial officer Bret Johnsen said the lease diversifies funding sources and pointed to payback periods under one year on AI infrastructure. If that payback holds, the lease is self liquidating: the hardware earns its rent before the term ends. If it does not, SpaceX is paying rent on chips whose next generation has already arrived, which is the same obsolescence risk as owning them, without the residual value. The distinction that matters for readers is that 40 billion dollars is expected aggregate principal across the lease schedules, not the full amount payable, which will be higher once the rate based amount is added.

What the totals hide

PitchBook reported on October 30, 2026, that the traditional hyperscalers, Amazon, Alphabet, Meta, Microsoft, and Oracle, have issued 182 billion dollars of senior notes in United States markets in 2026, up from 93.3 billion dollars in 2025 and under 17 billion dollars in 2024. That context reframes every deal in this ledger. Microsoft's 25 billion dollars and Oracle's 20 billion dollars are not outliers; they are two prints in a year when the largest borrowers nearly doubled their bond issuance. The same reporting notes that 10 of the 21 largest deals ever printed, sized 25 billion to 49 billion dollars, were placed in 2026. The bond market is not rationing AI borrowers at the top end. It is rationing them at the bottom, where CoreWeave's repricing shows what happens when contracted backlog, reported above 100 billion dollars, meets 35.6 billion dollars of debt principal and maturities that arrive before the contracts pay out.

That split is the judgment this ledger supports. When a journalist, investor, or supplier asks whether AI's buildout is affordable, the honest answer is that it depends on which borrower you mean. A AAA borrower can borrow for 40 years at 6.35 percent. A junk rated neocloud cannot refinance at any price the market currently offers without distress. A company with a leverage problem can sell stock instead, and dilute its way through. A company with hardware that pays back in under a year can lease it, and let someone else own the obsolescence. ByteHerald's capex ledger tallied what five companies plan to spend in 2026; this ledger tallies how the spending is raised, and the two ledgers together are the full picture. Spending without financing is a wish list. Financing without spending is a balance sheet. The buildout is both, and the risk sits in the gap between them.

How to use this ledger

For any future AI financing announcement, ask the same five questions this table asks. What instrument is it, and does the headline number mean cash, stock, enterprise value, or lease principal? What does it cost, in coupon, dilution, or rent, and is that cost fixed or floating? When does it come due, and does the hardware or revenue it funds last that long? What breaks first if the plan slips, a maturity wall, a share price fall, or a lease term that outlives the chip? And is the deal new money, or a repricing of old money, like CoreWeave's October bond move? A deal that answers all five clearly is financeable. A deal that hides one of them, usually the maturity or the full payable amount, is where the next surprise will come from.
