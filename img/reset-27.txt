<p>ByteHerald's litigation ledger counts eleven states suing OpenAI over ChatGPT and children, and separates the remedy that matters most from the damages headline. Oregon seeks damages of not less than $2.035 billion. Texas asks a court to bar ChatGPT for minors in Texas and to require court-approved evaluation of new models. The eight states that filed on October 14, 2026 seek penalties, data deletion and practice changes. Every claim in the ledger is an allegation in a pending civil case. No court has ruled on the child-safety claims in the record we reviewed.</p>

<h2>How to read the remedies</h2>
<p>The ledger separates three kinds of remedy. Money remedies set a floor or a per-violation price: Oregon pleads not less than $2.035 billion in its negligence and public-nuisance counts, and Montana seeks $10,000 per consumer-protection violation and $7,500 per privacy violation. Conduct remedies change the product: data deletion, practice changes and injunctions. Access remedies change who may use the product and under what approval: Texas asks the court to bar ChatGPT for minors in Texas and to require court-approved evaluation of new models.</p>
<p>Analysis: the Texas request is the remedy with industry-wide reach, because court-approved evaluation of new models would move a state court into the release process. Oregon's figure is larger as a headline, but it is a damages floor to be proved at trial, not an award. The complaint states the amount in the negligence and public-nuisance counts as not less than $2,035,000,000. That wording matters: the number is a pleading floor, not a judgment.</p>
<p>The eight October filings allege that ChatGPT's agreeable design harms vulnerable users and that safety marketing misled parents. OpenAI disputes the claims. The ledger records the allegations and the remedies sought; it does not treat either as proven. The metric that would change the analysis is a court order: an injunction, a model-evaluation requirement, or a ruling on liability. As of the filings reviewed, that metric remains unmet, while the number of courts that could issue such an order has grown from eight coordinated filings to eleven child-safety states.</p>
<p>What the ledger does not establish is harm, causation, or liability. Those questions belong to the courts. It also does not merge California's cyber-incident case into the child-safety count, because the legal theory differs. Keeping the theories separate is the point of the ledger: eleven states on child safety, one separate California cyber case, and no ruling yet on the claims that would turn a chatbot lawsuit into a development injunction.</p>
