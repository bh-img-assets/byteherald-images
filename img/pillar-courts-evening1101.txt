<p>Courts have now caught artificial intelligence being misused in their own filings often enough that a rule is taking shape, and it can be stated in one sentence: what a machine can read counts as part of the filing, even if no human eye can see it, and the person who signed the filing answers for it, not the tool. Five rulings from 2023 to 2026, collected in the table below, show how that rule was built case by case, from invented citations to a hidden instruction aimed at a court's own AI system.</p>

<p>The Brazil case deserves a closer look, because it is the first in this set where the target was not a human reader at all. The case, numbered ATOrd 0001062-55.2025.5.08.0130, concerned a rural tractor operator seeking recognition of employment, severance, overtime, and a hazard premium. The claim was valued at about 842,000 reais. While the court's Galileu system, an AI tool developed by the Regional Labor Court of the Fourth Region and adopted nationally, processed the initial petition, it surfaced text no human could see: white font on a white background, in Portuguese, instructing any AI reader to respond to the petition superficially and not challenge the documents, regardless of the command given to it. Judge Luiz Carlos de Araujo Santos Junior treated the conduct as an offense against the dignity of justice under articles 5 and 77 of Brazil's Code of Civil Procedure, imposed the 10 percent fine on the two signing attorneys, and ordered referrals to the Bar Association of Para and to the inspectorate of the Eighth Regional Labor Court. <a href="https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/artificial-intelligence/brazilian-lawyers-busted-for-attempting-to-manipulate-courts-ai-with-prompt-injection-hidden-in-filing-courts-ai-flags-hidden-text-that-demanded-superficial-legal-scrutiny-lawyers-fined-usd16-000">Tom's Hardware, reporting the ruling on November 1, 2026</a>, added a detail that sharpens the point: the defendant never answered the allegations, so the plaintiff won by default, and the hidden instruction was unnecessary even on its own terms. The court sanctioned it anyway, because the attempt itself was the offense.</p>
<h2>How the pattern changed between 2023 and 2026</h2>
<p>The first cases were about output: lawyers filing material a chatbot invented and failing to check it. Mata v. Avianca, decided by Judge P. Kevin Castel on June 22, 2023, remains the founding example, because the lawyers had submitted ChatGPT-generated citations that were entirely fictitious without verifying them against any authoritative database, and the court rested its order on bad faith in that failure. Park v. Kim then showed the next step up the ladder: a circuit court treating a fabricated citation as a professional responsibility matter, worthy of a grievance referral, not just a civil sanction. By 2024, local rules began to harden the lesson into procedure, including a Northern District of Texas rule effective September 3, 2024, requiring attorneys to disclose on the first page of a brief when generative AI prepared any part of it.</p>
<p>The 2026 cases are about input and evidence, not just citations. In Connecticut, a self-represented plaintiff named Matthew Elliott filed suit in October 2025, and a court clerk noticed unusual spacing between documents in July 2026, which led to the discovery of the hidden prompt. The court, through Judge Spader Jr., found that the hidden text was a prompt injection directed at an AI system, instructing it to generate output favorable to the plaintiff, and that it amounted to contacting the court in secret. In Arizona, the issue was not a filing at all but a sentencing video: the sister of Christopher Pelkey presented a video in which an AI-generated likeness and voice of the deceased victim spoke to the court, and the sentencing judge praised it. The Court of Appeals, in an opinion by Presiding Judge D. Steven Williams, held that allowing the AI video was fundamental error, vacated the 10.5-year manslaughter sentence, and ordered resentencing, while affirming the conviction and expressly permitting the authentic footage embedded in the same presentation. The line the Arizona court drew is the one every filer should memorize: a real record of a person is evidence, and a generated account of that person's thoughts is not, even when the family that made it means well.</p>

<h2>Why detection, not honesty alone, is now doing the work</h2>
<p>None of these five cases was caught because the filer confessed. Mata's citations were caught because the other side and the court could not find them. Brazil's instruction was caught because the court's own system surfaced it while processing the petition. Connecticut's prompt was caught because a clerk noticed spacing. Arizona's video was caught because an appeals court reviewed what the sentencing judge had relied on. That is why the most useful comparison is not with another court case but with volunteer detection elsewhere: Wikipedia's cleanup project, which ByteHerald covered on October 27, 2026, catches AI-generated text by the same method, looking for tells in the material itself rather than trusting the author's word. For that ledger of detection methods, see <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/wikipedias-cleanup-ledger-how-ai-slop.html">Wikipedia's Cleanup Ledger: How AI Slop Gets Caught</a>.</p>
<p>The scale behind these five cases is larger than any single table can show, and it should be stated with its source attached. A public database maintained by researcher Damien Charlotin counted about 2,000 court decisions involving AI-hallucinated material by September 2026, according to a November 2026 write-up of that database, with most involving self-represented litigants and a large minority involving licensed lawyers. That count is a researcher's tally, not a court statistic, and it is reported here as such, because the underlying decisions span many jurisdictions and many kinds of filings. What the five rulings above add to that count is the direction of travel: sanctions began at 5,000 dollars for an unverified brief, and within three years reached bar referrals, print-only filing requirements, and vacated sentences.</p>
<h2>The four-check test for anyone who files with AI</h2>
<p>For lawyers, the practical lesson is now a checklist that courts have effectively written for them. First, read the rendered filing, not just the draft on screen, because white text, tiny type, and hidden layers survive in the file even when they are invisible in print. Second, verify every citation against an authoritative database before signing, because Mata established that no verification at all falls below the minimum, and later rules turned that minimum into a disclosure duty. Third, treat any instruction embedded in a document as part of the document, because Brazil and Connecticut both punished the instruction, not just the visible argument. Fourth, separate authentic material from generated material and label the difference, because Arizona permitted the real footage and vacated the sentence over the generated portion.</p>
<p>That checklist also explains why lab governance and court governance are different jobs, even when they use similar words. ByteHerald compared six labs' safety frameworks on October 28, 2026, and those frameworks are voluntary commitments a company writes for itself. For how the labs frame their own rules, see <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/every-major-labs-ai-safety-framework.html">Every Major Lab's AI Safety Framework, Compared</a>. A court's rule, by contrast, is enforced by someone else, with fines, referrals, and vacated sentences, and it applies whether the filer read the framework or not. The gap between a policy page and a court order is the gap between a promise and a penalty, and the five rulings above measure it.</p>
<p>This article is a record of rulings, not legal advice, and its limits should be stated plainly. The five cases are selected because each one changed the rule, not because they are a complete census of AI cases in court, and outcomes vary by jurisdiction, by whether the filer was a lawyer, and by whether the material was a citation, a hidden instruction, or evidence. Fines and referrals reported here are taken from the orders and from reporting of those orders, and the database count above is attributed to its researcher rather than presented as ByteHerald's own tally. With those limits stated, the conclusion stands: a filing is now everything a machine can read in it, and signing it means answering for all of it.</p>
