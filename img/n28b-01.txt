<p>ByteHerald's exposure ledger finds that Google's human review of Gemini Connected Apps data covers five data categories, and in one of them the line between a summary and the source document disappears. Google states that in some cases the summary a reviewer sees may just be the email or the file itself, according to Google's Gemini Apps Help page Answer 16836988, current as of November 28, 2026. That single sentence changes how the 2023 privacy promise should be read.</p>
<h2>The ByteHerald exposure ledger</h2>
<p>We extracted every data class Google names for Connected Apps review and scored each one on three questions: what a human reviewer can see, whether the whole source document can appear, and whether the material is used for training. The ledger below is our own extraction from Google's help documentation, not a reproduction of Google's page layout.</p>

<h2>What the ledger shows</h2>
<p>Three findings stand out. First, the reviewed subset is broader than chat transcripts. Google says it includes interactions with Gemini, summaries, excerpts, and inferences drawn from relevant emails and files, plus Wallet data and model information. Second, the summary exception matters. A summary of a long contract is a reduced copy, but Google's own wording allows the summary to be the file itself, which means a short sensitive document can reach a reviewer in full. Third, the training boundary is narrower than the review boundary. Google says Gemini does not train directly on a Gmail inbox, Drive files, Contacts, Calendar, Wallet, or Photos imagery and audio, but does train on summaries, excerpts, generated media, and inferences when Gemini Apps History is on.</p>
<p>Google also warns users directly: do not connect apps containing confidential information that a reviewer should not see. That warning sits in tension with the September 19, 2023 blog post that introduced these integrations with a promise that personal data would not be used to train models, a post Google updated on October 16, 2026. The promise and the warning can both be true, because they describe different things. Training use is restricted. Human review is not.</p>
<h2>Methodology and limits</h2>
<p>Methodology: we read Google's Gemini Apps Help Answer 16836988 in full on November 28, 2026, and coded each named data class against reviewer visibility, whole-source possibility, and training use. We cross-checked the training boundary against Google's September 19, 2023 blog post and its October 16, 2026 update note. Limits: Google does not publish what share of interactions enters the reviewed subset, how long reviewed material is retained, or how reviewers are vetted beyond describing them as including trained service providers. Our ledger therefore measures stated exposure, not observed frequency. The evidence does not establish that any specific user's file was read by a reviewer.</p>
<h2>Analysis</h2>
<p>Analysis: the practical risk is concentrated in short documents. A two-page term sheet, a medical letter, or a password memo stored in Drive is exactly the kind of file where a summary and the source converge. Users who connect Drive or Gmail for convenience should treat short sensitive files as reviewer-visible in full, because Google's own text permits that outcome. The disconnect control also deserves attention: disconnecting an app stops future access but leaves existing Gemini History in place until the user deletes it separately.</p>
