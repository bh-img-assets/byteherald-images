<p>ByteHerald's access ledger finds four distinct ways websites now treat AI agents, not one blocklist. The October 17, 2026 reporting named Yelp, Delta Air Lines, JPMorgan Chase and Charles Schwab as blocking or restricting agents. Eleven days later, Shopify's Fall '26 developer release dated October 28, 2026 moved in the opposite direction: WebMCP tools on every Liquid and Hydrogen storefront and checkout let browser agents search products, update the cart and complete checkout once the buyer approves, with the same validation as the checkout page.</p>
<p>The asset is a dated access-model ledger. It separates companies that block agents, companies that route agents through paid licensing, companies that restrict transactions, and platforms that admit agents through structured tools. The distinction matters because an agent reading a page, an agent using licensed data, and an agent completing a checkout create different risks and different evidence. Treating all four as one refusal hides the actual policy choice.</p>

<p>Methodology: ByteHerald classified each dated company position by the action the agent is permitted to take, not by whether the company sounds friendly or hostile to AI. A licensing gate permits data use under contract. A transaction block permits information access while withholding the act that changes a booking. An account restriction protects authenticated sessions. Structured checkout permits a transaction only inside platform validation and buyer approval. The categories are ours; the company positions are sourced in each row.</p>
<p>Shopify's release also describes Shop.dev and shop.md as a single connection to Shopify Catalog, sign-in with Shop, cart, checkout and Shop Pay, with shop.md able to search more than a billion products by Shopify's count. That count is a company claim, not a ByteHerald measurement. The verifiable product fact is narrower: Shopify says the tools exist on its storefronts and checkout, and that checkout completes after buyer approval. Whether merchants keep the default, and whether agents complete purchases at scale, are separate questions the release does not answer.</p>
<p>What this does not establish: it does not prove that structured checkout is safe for banks, airlines or ticketing, where the transaction changes an account, a seat or a legal obligation. It does not prove that blocked sites lost sales, or that open sites gained them. It does not measure how many agents were actually stopped, because the October 17, 2026 reporting relies on company statements and user reports rather than a public block-rate dataset.</p>
<p>Analysis: the split is no longer open versus closed. It is unstructured browsing versus structured access. A site can plausibly treat screenshot-driven clicking as scraping while still admitting an agent through tools it can meter, validate and revoke. That is the citable distinction: by October 28, 2026, at least one major commerce platform had moved agent access from tolerated browsing to structured checkout, while travel, banking and local-data companies were restricting the acts that carry money or identity risk.</p>
