<p>Oracle filed on November 2, 2026, to raise 20 billion dollars, split evenly between 10 billion dollars of new common stock and 10 billion dollars of senior notes, to help pay for its artificial intelligence and cloud data center buildout. The filing landed less than a week after Oracle's October 28 investor day, where the company said it would raise no more than 20 billion dollars in additional capital market financing for its fiscal 2027 to 2030 investment cycle. In other words, this filing uses up the whole cap in one move, and the market noticed: Oracle shares fell roughly 5 to 6 percent in Monday trading, extending a decline of about 33 percent for the year through Friday, October 30.</p>

<p>Two details in that table deserve emphasis. First, the stock and bond deals are independent, so Oracle can take the equity even if bond buyers balk, or borrow even if shareholders hate the dilution. Second, the prospectus language is deliberately broad: "general corporate purposes" covers capital spending, but it also covers debt repayment, acquisitions, dividends, and buybacks, so not every dollar of this raise is earmarked for data centers. The AI buildout is the reason investors are being asked, not a legal restriction on how the cash is spent.</p>
<h2>Why Oracle needs the money now</h2>
<p>Oracle told investors at its October 28 investor day that capital spending would peak in the current fiscal year and the next, then step down in fiscal 2029, when it expects to start funding its own growth, with positive free cash flow expected in the second half of fiscal 2028. That timeline is the whole bet. Between now and then, Oracle must build data centers and buy chips faster than its revenue arrives, and the gap has to come from somewhere. The company already carries 125.34 billion dollars in borrowings against 67.36 billion dollars of fiscal 2026 revenue, a ratio that explains why half of this raise is equity rather than all debt: more bonds alone would push leverage further, while new shares dilute existing holders but add cash without interest payments.</p>
<p>The market reaction shows both sides of that trade. Existing shareholders lose a slice of the company, roughly 76.5 million new shares at the reference price, and bondholders see more debt ahead, which is why Oracle's existing 6.85 percent bonds due in 2066 fell more than 1.2 cents on the dollar on the day, according to Tradeweb data reported by <a href="https://srnnews.com/oracle-plans-to-raise-20-billion-through-bonds-and-stock-sales/">Reuters</a>. The question for readers is not whether Oracle needs funding, it plainly does, but whether the fiscal 2029 self funding date holds. If spending peaks as promised and revenue triples toward the 225 billion dollar fiscal 2030 target, this raise looks like a bridge. If spending keeps rising past the peak, Oracle will be back asking again, and the 20 billion dollar cap it set a week ago will be remembered as a promise it broke, not a limit it kept.</p>
<h2>How to judge this raise</h2>
<p>Judge it on three observable tests, not on the size of the headline. First, pricing: when the stock price and bond rates are set, compare them with the reference levels, 130.67 dollars per share and the existing 2066 bond's move, to see what dilution and interest actually cost. Second, the fiscal 2028 free cash flow turn: chief financial officer Hilary Maxson said free cash flow turns positive in the second half of fiscal 2028, and any slip in that date is a louder signal than any single quarter's spending. Third, whether Oracle files anything further before fiscal 2029: the company has now used its stated 20 billion dollar cap, so a second raise before then would mean the cap was a first installment, not a ceiling. For the wider pattern of how AI infrastructure gets paid for, ByteHerald's <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/five-companies-828-billion-2026-ai.html">capex ledger</a> tallied what five companies plan to spend; this filing is the other side of that ledger, where the money comes from.</p>
