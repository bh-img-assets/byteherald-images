<p>ByteHerald re-ran the Convovo ballot counts with refusals left in the denominator, and the headline survives in a weaker form. Convovo's October 2026 paper reports 1,437 replies per model across 479 Democrat-versus-Republican races. Of GPT-6.1 Sol replies naming a major-party candidate, 1,082 of 1,111 named the Democrat. Of Grok 4.7 replies naming a major-party candidate, 952 of 1,038 named the Republican. Those are the 97 percent and 92 percent figures. They exclude 313 GPT refusals and 375 Grok refusals.</p>
<p>Our asset is a refusal-inclusive recount of the same published totals. It does not add new races and it does not change Convovo's race verdicts. It answers a narrower question that the headline percentages hide: when every reply counts, how often did each model actually name the party it is said to favor? The answer matters because a refusal is a real product behavior. A voter who receives no name has not received the recommendation implied by a forced-choice headline.</p>

<p>Methodology: ByteHerald divided each published reply count by 1,437, the paper's total replies per model in the 479-race panel. We did not treat refusals as votes for either party, and we did not recompute race-level winners. Convovo's own race verdict treats a refusal as an abstention and takes the majority among replies naming a candidate. That is a defensible verdict rule, but it is not the same measure as share of all replies.</p>
<p>The search-off panel is the mechanism test. Convovo's October 2026 paper reports that with search off and the original prompt, GPT named the Democrat in 287 answers and the Republican in 115. When the prompt named the nominees, GPT named the Democrat in 816 answers and the Republican in 91. Grok declined most races without search, and its picks tended to name the party holding the seat. In analysis, search strengthens GPT's lean and produces Grok's Republican lean. That distinction is more informative than a single bias label for either model.</p>
<p>What this does not establish: it does not measure what the consumer apps do for a normal user, because Convovo reports that the apps decline the ballot prompt. It does not measure persuasion, turnout, or vote switching. It does not show that either model would give the same answer to a voter who supplied policy priorities, because the prompt supplied none. It also does not separate model preference from search retrieval, because the search-on condition bundles the model with its own search results and, for Grok, hidden instructions the researchers could not inspect.</p>
<p>The citable finding is therefore precise, and it is narrower than the headline that traveled. A share of named picks describes direction among decided replies. A share of all replies describes what a user of the API actually received across the full panel, including the replies that declined to name anyone at all. Under a forced-choice API prompt with search on, GPT-6.1 Sol named the Democrat in 75.3 percent of all replies and Grok 4.7 named the Republican in 66.2 percent of all replies, by ByteHerald's recount of Convovo's October 2026 totals. The partisan direction is large. The certainty implied by 97 percent and 92 percent belongs only to the subset of replies that named a major-party candidate.</p>
