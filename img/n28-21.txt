<p>ByteHerald rebuilt the Houston Advanced Research Center's Texas data center model into a scenario ledger, and the spread is the story. The same data center buildout produces nitrogen oxide emissions up 136 percent by 2031 on the projected grid mix, up about 35 percent when paired with on-site wind and solar, and up nearly 400 percent on on-site gas engines. Power choice moves the outcome more than the size of the buildout itself: HARC finds combustion supply can emit 2 to 10 times the NOx of grid supply paired with renewables, depending on the demand forecast.</p>

<h2>The baseline being reversed</h2>
<p>The ledger's force comes from the starting point. Between 2002 and 2024, Texas power generation cut NOx by about 67 percent, sulfur dioxide by about 80 percent and PM2.5 by 60 percent, while population and the economy grew. The grid-mix scenario returns NOx to roughly its 2004 volume and PM2.5 to its 2006 volume by 2031. In plain terms, five years of data center power demand can undo two decades of power-sector cleanup, but only on some power paths. The renewables pairing keeps most of the gain; the gas path erases it and goes further.</p>
<p>Carbon dioxide follows the same pattern. HARC projects power-sector CO2 up about 90 percent by 2031 on the grid mix, roughly 251 million metric tons even under the most conservative demand forecast, and about 900 million metric tons under the highest, more than Texas emitted from all sources in 2024.</p>
<h2>What the model does not establish</h2>
<p>Analysis: these are scenarios, not a forecast of what will be built. HARC assumes data centers run around the clock at 80 percent utilization, and its demand forecasts span a wide range because many announced projects will be delayed, downsized or cancelled. The model projects statewide emission volumes; it does not estimate pollution concentrations in specific communities or count health outcomes such as asthma attacks or hospital visits. The worst path requires both the full buildout and the dirtiest power choices, a combination that is possible but not inevitable.</p>
<p>What the ledger does establish is the decision structure. Interconnection queues and turbine orders made in the next few years select among these rows. By the time monitors confirm which row Texas is on, the choices that produced it will be a decade old.</p>
<p>The demand side explains why the choice cannot wait. HARC notes that Texas data centers are expected to add more electricity demand in the next six years than the entire grid added in the last twenty, and the Texas Tribune counted at least 288 planned projects statewide. Grid connection waits of three to five years push developers toward on-site generation they control, which is exactly the row with the worst air outcome. The policy question the ledger frames is therefore concrete: whether permitting and interconnection rules reward the renewables pairing, which still adds emissions but preserves most of the two-decade gain, or leave the fastest private option, on-site gas, as the default.</p>
