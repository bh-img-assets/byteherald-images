<p>ByteHerald's two-speed ledger finds that Apollo's October 24, 2026 note documents a measurable split, not a proven cause. Inflation-adjusted revenue per employee for the Magnificent Seven rose from about $550,000 in 2022 to about $800,000 in 2026, roughly 45 percent higher by our arithmetic on the chart readings. The other 493 S&P 500 companies stayed near $550,000. The 2026 gap is about $250,000 per employee.</p>
<p>Apollo chief economist Torsten Slok reads that split as zero financial payoff from AI outside tech. The charts support the split. They do not, by themselves, prove that AI caused the Magnificent Seven gains or that AI spending caused the flat line elsewhere.</p>

<h2>What the evidence does not establish</h2>
<p>Analysis: two aggregate lines cannot separate AI effects from pricing power, layoffs, mix shift toward software revenue, or capital intensity. The Magnificent Seven sell much of the AI infrastructure the other 493 buy, so one group's revenue can be the other group's cost. That transfer alone can widen the split without any productivity change.</p>
<p>The defensible finding is concentration: by October 2026, the documented financial gains sat with seven companies, and the remaining 493 showed flat revenue per employee and flat margins. Whether AI later broadens that payoff is a forecast, not a result in these charts.</p>
<p>The five year warning attached to the note is a valuation argument, not a measurement. Slok argues markets priced rapid earnings growth while cash flow reality moves slower. Our ledger takes no position on that pricing claim, because the three documented measures describe company outcomes, not whether equity prices already reflect them.</p>
<p>The composition of the two groups limits every inference drawn from them. The Magnificent Seven are Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Meta, Microsoft, Nvidia and Tesla, a set dominated by firms selling cloud, chips, advertising, and platforms. The S&P 493 mixes banks, manufacturers, retailers, energy firms, and health companies. A flat aggregate for that mixture can hide gains in one industry and losses in another, so the ledger cannot say AI paid off nowhere outside tech, only that no payoff is visible in the combined lines.</p>
<p>Token spending behavior, the behavioral leg of Apollo's argument, cuts both ways. Slok reads optimization and routing as buyers treating tokens as a cost center. The same behavior is also what mature adoption looks like when unit prices fall and workloads move from pilots to production budgets. The charts document the financial split; the motive behind buyer behavior is interpretation, and we label it as such.</p>
