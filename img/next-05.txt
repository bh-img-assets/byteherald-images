<p>ByteHerald's liability ledger separates four questions that are often collapsed into one: who accessed the system, who authorized the target, who controlled the agent, and who can prove loss. The clearest court record in the set, Amazon v. Perplexity, answers only the first question for one architecture. The Ninth Circuit held on August 4, 2026, that Amazon was unlikely to succeed on its Computer Fraud and Abuse Act and California analogue claims because the user accessed Amazon computers with the Assistant as a tool. That is a narrow access holding, not a general agent-liability regime.</p>

<h2>Analysis</h2>
<p>Analysis: the Amazon opinion turns on architecture and statutory language. The court emphasized that Perplexity servers did not directly access Amazon servers on the record presented; screenshots moved from the user's browser to Perplexity, and instructions returned to the user's device. The court also treated the Assistant as a tool, not a person, for the access analysis. That reasoning helps a user-directed shopping agent under anti-hacking statutes. It does not decide whether a platform can enforce its terms, whether a user breached a contract, whether a developer negligently designed a harmful agent, or whether a different architecture with direct server contact would be treated the same way.</p>
<p>The Railway incident sits at the opposite end of the evidence ladder. There is an admission that agents were run, an apology, and a promised policy change. There is also a dispute about scope and intent: the engineer described signup and agentic testing that went too far, while Primcloud's founder described probing that his platform forbids and said no breach occurred. That record shows which controls were absent: authorization, testing rules, monitoring, and an independent stop. It is weak as legal precedent because no court has tested intent, authorization, damage, or loss.</p>
<p>Scale is the third guardrail. Uber's disclosure puts agent transactions at about 504 in a week of about 320 million trips and orders. That is about 1 in 634,921 by ByteHerald arithmetic, so the liability question is arriving before agent volume dominates transactions.</p>
<h2>What the evidence does not establish</h2>
<p>The evidence does not establish that AI developers are immune when agents cause harm, that users always bear liability for agent actions, or that anti-hacking statutes are the right tool for every agent dispute. It does not establish the full technical scope of the Railway incident, because logs and independent forensic findings are not in the public record used here. It also does not establish how courts will treat agents that contact servers directly, conceal automation, exceed user instructions, or act across many accounts at once.</p>
<p>What to watch next is the district court record after remand in Amazon v. Perplexity, any written Railway external-testing policy, and platform disclosures that separate agent transactions by authorization type. The decisive records will be permission, instruction scope, server-contact path, logs, stop latency, and loss.</p>
