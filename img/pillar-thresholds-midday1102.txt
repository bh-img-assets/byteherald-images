<p>There is no single frontier line for AI. There are at least five, drawn by different governments for different purposes, and a model can cross one, miss another, and still be caught by a third. The <a href="https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/high-level-summary/?ref=flyaps.com">European Union draws its line</a> at 10 to the power of 25 floating-point operations. China, in its <a href="https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/zwgk/zcfb/art/2026/art_0a31a3c9ac404f7a9b0e917ce3412997.html">October 2026 draft</a>, draws its model line at 10 to the power of 26, or 2 trillion parameters, or a global top-10 ranking. The United States drew its federal line at 10 to the power of 26 and then revoked it, while <a href="https://www.aei.org/research-products/report/regulating-the-frontier-how-california-new-york-and-illinois-are-governing-the-most-powerful-ai-models/">California, New York, and Illinois have enacted their own frontier laws</a> around the same 10 to the power of 26 figure. This pillar puts every line in one table, with its trigger, its consequence, and its status, because that table is the thing a builder, a buyer, or a journalist actually needs, and no single government document contains it.</p>

<p>Three readings fall out of that table immediately. First, the European line is 10 times lower than the American and Chinese model lines, by ByteHerald division: 10 to the power of 26 divided by 10 to the power of 25 is 10. A training run can therefore be an ordinary model in Washington's former framework and a systemic-risk model in Brussels. Second, China is the only regime in the table that adds parameter count and ranking as independent triggers, so a model that is efficient enough to stay below the compute line can still be caught by size or by success. Third, the United States no longer has a federal compute line at all, which means the live American lines are state laws, lab frameworks, and sector rules, not a single federal number. ByteHerald's <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/the-great-decoupling-every-ai-export.html">export-control map</a> traced the two-sided escalation that produced this patchwork, and this table is the numeric companion to that map: it gives the thresholds the map described in prose.</p>
<h2>Why compute was chosen, and why it is weakening</h2>
<p>Compute was chosen because it is measurable before a model exists in the world. A regulator can count floating-point operations in a training plan, while capability can only be measured after the model is built and tested. That is why every regime in the table starts from compute, even the ones that add other tests. But compute is a proxy, not a measurement of danger, and the proxy is weakening in both directions. Efficiency gains mean the same capability can be reached with less compute over time, so a fixed line captures fewer capable models each year. And scaffolding, fine-tuning, data quality, and agent architectures can turn a below-threshold model into a consequential system, which is exactly why China's draft controls agents by behavior and why the European Act lets the Commission designate a model below its compute line on capabilities, scale, market reach, and access to tools.</p>
<p>The forecast numbers show how fast the net is widening. A <a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2504.16138v1">2026 arXiv forecasting study</a> estimated that by the end of 2028, between 103 and 306 foundation models would exceed the European 10 to the power of 25 line, with a median estimate of 165, and between 45 and 148 would exceed the 10 to the power of 26 line, with a median estimate of 81. In April 2025, the same study's estimates put about 30 publicly available models above the European line and only 2 above the higher line. The gap between those counts is the regulatory story in one statistic: the lower line already catches an order of magnitude more models, and both counts are set to grow faster than linearly as training scales. A threshold that was drawn to catch only the frontier will, if left fixed, eventually catch the mainstream, and that is why the European Act gives the Commission power to amend its threshold by delegated act as hardware efficiency changes.</p>
<h2>The capability test that sits beside the numbers</h2>
<p>No table of numbers is complete without the capability test, because every regime in it eventually appeals to what a model can do. The European Act presumes systemic risk above its compute line but lets a provider argue, with evidence, that its model does not present systemic risk despite crossing it, and lets the Commission designate a below-line model on capability grounds. China's global top-10 test is a capability proxy by another name: ranking and call volume measure demonstrated performance and reach, not training cost. Lab frameworks make the capability test explicit: OpenAI's Preparedness Framework, as described in state-law analysis, uses capability thresholds to decide when stronger safeguards are required, rather than compute alone. ByteHerald's <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/every-major-labs-ai-safety-framework.html">six-lab safety framework comparison</a> maps those lab thresholds in detail, and readers who want to know what a lab counts as dangerous, as opposed to what a government counts as regulated, should start there.</p>
<p>The practical test for any team, then, is three questions in order. First, does the model cross a numeric line in any jurisdiction where it will be trained, exported, or deployed, counting compute, parameters, and ranking separately, because China counts all three? Second, does it meet a capability or designation test even if it misses every numeric line, because Brussels and the lab frameworks both allow that route? Third, does the way it will be delivered change the answer, because custody, not access, is what China's draft controls, and a model served by API in one jurisdiction can become an export in another the moment its weights move? A team that can answer those three questions has done the compliance work; a team that can only quote one threshold has not.</p>
<h2>What to watch, and what would change this table</h2>
<p>Four events would rewrite this table, and each has a date or a trigger to watch. China's comment window closes November 15, 2026, after which the draft thresholds, the undefined word "lightweight," and the automatic-licensing procedures may narrow, broaden, or shift. The European Commission can amend its 10 to the power of 25 line by delegated act, and any such amendment would move the most consequential live line in the world by definition. United States state laws will be tested by their first incident reports, and analysis of those laws already warns that their incident definitions are narrow enough that some consequential failures, including containment failures, might not trigger a report at all. And lab capability frameworks will keep moving independently of governments, which means the most restrictive line a model faces may be its own provider's policy, not any law. This table is therefore a snapshot dated November 2026, not a permanent map, and ByteHerald will update it when any of those four events moves a line.</p>
