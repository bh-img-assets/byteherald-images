<p>ByteHerald finding: the forum decides the appeal. In the records we compiled, drivers who appealed to the company that deactivated them almost never got back in, while drivers who appealed with representation under a due process ordinance usually did. The gap between the two paths is at least 70 percentage points.</p>
<p>That finding reframes the recent stories of gig workers using chatbots to fight deactivations. A chatbot can draft a letter and organize evidence. The ledger below suggests the harder problem is where that letter is heard, and whether anyone independent reads it.</p>

<p>Methodology: we compiled only published counts with a named source and date, and we did not average across cities because the populations, years and procedures differ. Percentages taken from the sources are reported as published. The Chicago reinstatement rate of 3.5% is our arithmetic, 1,085 divided by 30,881. The Seattle figure of 80% applies to drivers who qualified for and received representation, not to every deactivated driver in the 1,420 recorded cases.</p>
<h2>What the ledger shows</h2>
<p>Analysis: the New York survey and the Chicago baseline describe the same structure from opposite ends. AALDEF found that more than 90% of surveyed drivers who appealed stayed locked out, and most had received no prior notice of a complaint. Chicago recorded reinstatement for only about one in 29 deactivated drivers across seven and a half years. Seattle is the outlier, and its difference is procedural: a city ordinance, a driver resolution center, representation, and a just cause standard. Under that system, 80% of represented drivers had their deactivations overturned.</p>
<p>Human Rights Watch adds the error signal. In its Texas survey, 40 of 127 workers had been deactivated at least once, and nearly half of those workers said the platform later cleared them of wrongdoing. That does not prove every deactivation was wrong. It does show that a large share of automated or opaque terminations did not survive later review, which is exactly the kind of record an appeal is supposed to test.</p>
<h2>Where the chatbot fits</h2>
<p>Business Insider reported on November 8, 2026 that some deactivated workers are using ChatGPT to draft arbitration letters, evidence summaries and wage loss estimates, and that one Tennessee driver regained account access about a week after sending such a letter. That reporting is anecdotal, and it does not establish that the chatbot caused the reinstatement. What it establishes is narrower: workers who previously faced the paperwork alone now have a free drafting tool.</p>
<p>Analysis: drafting help attacks only one part of the gap. A better letter cannot supply notice that was never given, evidence the company will not disclose, or a neutral decision maker. The Seattle result came from representation plus a legal standard the company had to meet. A chatbot supplies neither. It can make an appeal clearer and faster to file, and for a worker with no lawyer that is real value. It does not change who judges the appeal.</p>
<h2>What the evidence does not establish</h2>
<p>These records do not measure chatbot assisted appeals as a category, and no source in the ledger compares outcomes for AI drafted appeals against human drafted ones. The New York survey is self reported and drawn from deactivated drivers, so it cannot estimate deactivation rates among all drivers. The Seattle study covers one city and one ordinance period. The Chicago figures count reinstatements, not appeals filed, so the 3.5% rate mixes drivers who appealed with drivers who never did.</p>
<p>The durable conclusion is procedural, not technological. Where the company is investigator, prosecutor and judge, reinstatement is rare. Where an independent process with representation exists, most challenged deactivations in the Seattle record were overturned. Until that structure changes, a chatbot is best understood as free paperwork help inside a system whose outcomes the ledger shows are decided elsewhere.</p>
