<p>ByteHerald's specification ledger for Google's eighth-generation TPU family corrects the launch headline: TPU 8i Max is a bandwidth upgrade, not a memory doubling. Google's November 9, 2026 announcement states that the Max doubles compute, SRAM and HBM bandwidth against TPU 8i, but does not state an absolute memory capacity. Post-launch reporting puts capacity at 384 gigabytes. Divided across the two-chip package, that is 192 gigabytes per die by our arithmetic, below the 288 gigabytes of a standalone TPU 8i. Package bandwidth doubles; capacity per die falls.</p><h2>The ledger: stated, reported, undisclosed</h2><table><thead><tr><th>Specification</th><th>Evidence grade</th><th>Source and date</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>TPU 8i: 288 GB HBM, 384 MB SRAM</td><td>Reported from launch materials</td><td>April 22, 2026 launch</td></tr><tr><td>TPU 8i Max: 384 GB HBM, 17.2 TB/s bandwidth, 768 MB SRAM</td><td>Reported; Google states doubling, not capacity</td><td>ChosunBiz, November 9, 2026</td></tr><tr><td>TPU 8i Max capacity per die: 192 GB</td><td>ByteHerald arithmetic, 384 divided by 2</td><td>Computed from the row above</td></tr><tr><td>TPU 8tx confidential compute on AMD EPYC hosts</td><td>AMD-stated: SEV verifies accelerator identity and security configuration before protected-memory access</td><td>AMD release, November 9, 2026</td></tr><tr><td>TPU 8tx latency up to 2x lower than TPU 8t</td><td>Google-stated vendor claim</td><td>Google announcement, November 9, 2026</td></tr><tr><td>Pricing, availability dates, launch partners</td><td>Undisclosed</td><td>Futurum, November 10, 2026</td></tr><tr><td>Independent benchmark submissions</td><td>None public</td><td>Futurum, November 10, 2026</td></tr></tbody></table><p>Methodology: ByteHerald graded every specification in the November 9, 2026 announcements by its strongest public source. TPU 8tx reuses 8t silicon with AMD EPYC hosts and the Boardfly network; TPU 8i Max packages two 8i dies. The strategy is packaging speed: new systems in months from existing silicon, aimed at agent workloads where memory bandwidth and confidential inference bind before raw compute does. That is a real engineering position, and it is also why vendor latency claims need independent benchmarks before they can be treated as measured results.</p><p>What the evidence does not establish: total cost of ownership against Nvidia systems, delivered performance on production agent workloads, or the security properties of the 8tx attestation chain beyond AMD's SEV description. As of November 10, 2026, Google had published no pricing, availability dates or launch partners for the new systems.</p><h2>How the ledger was built</h2><p>The ledger grades each specification by the strongest public document that states it, and it records absence as data. When Google's announcement says bandwidth doubles but omits capacity, the ledger writes undisclosed rather than borrowing a number from reporting without a label. When reporting supplies the capacity, the ledger marks it reported and shows the arithmetic that follows from it, so a reader can see exactly which conclusions depend on a non-vendor figure.</p><p>Analysis: this grading is the citable part because accelerator launches are where vendor arithmetic most often outruns evidence. A doubling claim can be true of bandwidth, false of capacity and silent on price at the same time, and all three states appear in this one launch. The ledger's four grades, vendor-stated, partner-stated, reported and undisclosed, let a reader rebuild the same table for the next launch and compare like with like.</p><p>By ByteHerald Staff.
