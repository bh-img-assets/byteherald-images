<p>On October 27, 2026, Artificial Analysis rebuilt the benchmark that ranks the world's leading AI models, and in doing so it reset every score on its leaderboard. Intelligence Index v5, described by its maker as the biggest upgrade since v4 launched in January 2026, puts 60 percent of the index on private test sets or held-out answers, replaces three benchmarks, adds two more, and weights four categories equally at 25 percent each. The first consequence is the one most coverage skipped: a v4 score and a v5 score are not comparable, so any story that says a model rose or fell by comparing across versions is wrong on its face, and the only honest comparison is inside v5 itself.</p>

<h2>What changed, in one table</h2>
<p>Artificial Analysis publishes the change list in its announcement, and it is worth reproducing in full, because the leaderboard only makes sense once the test itself is understood. Two benchmarks are new, three are upgraded, four stay, and three are removed. The weights below are taken from the same announcement, with subweights adding to each category's 25 percent.</p>

<p>The design choice behind the list is contamination control. Public test sets leak into training data, and once a model has seen the test, its score measures memory as much as ability. Private tests and held-out answers cost reproducibility, because outsiders cannot rerun the exact test, but they protect the measurement from that leak. Artificial Analysis also launched a separate Progress Index v1 alongside v5, which places models released since November 2022 on one continuous scale for tracking capability over time, while the Intelligence Index remains its primary measure of current model intelligence. Readers should use the two for different questions: Progress Index for how far capability has come, Intelligence Index for which model leads today, and never mix a score from one with a score from the other.</p>
<h2>The new leaderboard, and the gap that widened</h2>
<p>At v5 launch, Claude Opus 5.5 led at 58, ahead of GPT-6 Astra at 57 and GPT-6.1 Sol at 55, with Gemini 4 Argon next at 50. A day later, on October 28, 2026, Artificial Analysis reported that Google's Gemini 4 Carbon scored 59, taking first place, driven by leading performance in complex reasoning, knowledge, and scientific tasks, and leading 4 of the 9 index evaluations: HLE-Diamond, Terminal-Bench-Science, AA-Omniscience, and AutomationBench-AA. Carbon is not leading in coding or knowledge work, and it is second only to Claude Opus 5.5 on the separate Coding Agent Index, at 59 against 62. Readers who want the launch story should read ByteHerald's <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/gemini-4-carbon-leads-index-but-you.html">report on Gemini 4 Carbon leading the index</a>, which also notes that Carbon was not yet publicly available at announcement, going first to a subset of cyber defenders; this pillar is the methodology audit behind that headline, not a second launch report.</p>

<p>The most debated consequence of v5 is the open weights gap. Artificial Analysis states that the gap widened from 3 points in v4.3 to 12 points in v5, a widening of 9 points by ByteHerald's subtraction from the announcement's own figures. Kimi K3.1 Pro, whose weights were not yet released at v5 launch, is the top open weights model at 46, while among released weights, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 and GLM-5.3 lead at 32, which is 26 points behind the leader at v5 launch and below Claude Haiku 5.5 at 36. Reporting on the change states that Kimi scored 55 on the old index at launch and 46 on v5, a fall of 9 points, and fell from fourth place to ninth; those two figures are reported, not taken from Artificial Analysis's own announcement, and they must not be read as a like-for-like fall, because the test itself changed. The gap is widest where v5 is newest: on HLE-Diamond and Terminal-Bench-Science, Kimi scores 37 percent and 34 percent, against 68 percent for Claude Opus 5.5 and 63 percent for GPT-6 Astra. One reading, offered by physicist Stephen Hsu in coverage of the change, is that Chinese labs have not prioritized math and science performance to the same degree; another reading is that private science and coding tests favor the labs whose training already matches those tasks. The data in the announcement supports the size of the gap, not a single explanation for it, and this article does not choose between those readings.</p>
<p>Two further v5 findings deserve more attention than they received. First, no single model leads all nine evaluations: Claude Opus 5.5 leads three, and five other models lead the remaining six, which means a buyer who picks by the headline number alone is buying a composite, not a specialist. Second, cost varies by more than 13 times among leading models: GPT-6.1 Terra is cheapest among the ten highest-scoring models shown, at 0.96 dollars per index task, against 13.04 dollars for Claude Fable 5.1, while GPT-6.1 Sol scores within 4 points of Claude Opus 5.5 at about an eighth of the cost per task. Effort settings matter more in v5 than in v4.3: Claude Opus 5.5 scores 35 at low effort and 58 at max, at ten times the cost per task, 0.98 dollars against 10.04 dollars, so any v5 score quoted without its effort setting is incomplete. For readers comparing coding tools rather than models, ByteHerald's <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/5-ai-coding-assistants-one-scorecard.html">scorecard of five AI coding assistants</a> uses a different method, published benchmarks plus pricing plus a fit matrix, and the two should not be merged into one ranking, because a model benchmark and an assistant scorecard measure different things.</p>
<h2>How to read the next leaderboard change</h2>
<p>Leaderboards will change again, and the next change will tempt the same error: comparing a new score with an old one across a methodology break. The test below, built from the v5 announcement itself, is the check to run before quoting any future movement. It asks only questions the benchmark maker can answer from its own announcement, and if any answer is missing, the comparison is not ready to publish.</p>
<table>
<tr><th>Question to ask</th><th>Why it matters</th><th>v5 answer</th></tr>
<tr><td>Did the version change?</td><td>Scores across versions are not comparable</td><td>Yes: v4 to v5, with benchmarks added, upgraded, and removed</td></tr>
<tr><td>Is the effort setting the same?</td><td>Opus 5.5 spans 35 to 58 by effort alone</td><td>Stated per model in the announcement (max, high, and others)</td></tr>
<tr><td>What share of tests is private?</td><td>Private tests limit independent reruns</td><td>60 percent of v5, up from the v4 share</td></tr>
<tr><td>Is cost quoted on the same price basis?</td><td>Carbon's launch discount changes its cost per task</td><td>Artificial Analysis uses standard pricing, not the 50 percent launch discount</td></tr>
<tr><td>Does one model lead every evaluation?</td><td>A composite lead hides specialist weakness</td><td>No: six models lead across the nine evaluations</td></tr>
</table>
<p>What v5 does not prove should be stated as plainly as what it does. Private tests make the index harder to game and harder to verify, and that trade is real, not a footnote. Carbon's coding tests were run in Google's own Antigravity command line tool, according to reporting on the Carbon results, and at least one developer has said he plans to retest in other agent tools, so the coding portion of Carbon's score carries a harness caveat the headline number does not show. Carbon's hallucination rate on AA-Omniscience rose from 4 percent for Argon to 11 percent, even as accuracy rose 10 points to 58 percent, and while 11 percent is still the second lowest of any model, against 50 percent for both Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra on the same measure, a rising hallucination rate alongside a rising score is a trade a buyer should see, not a detail to bury. And Kimi's position remains provisional in one specific sense: its weights were promised by November 4, 2026, after v5 launch, so the open weights ranking at launch describes a model whose weights readers could not yet inspect. None of this makes v5 useless. It makes v5 a measuring instrument with stated limits, and the journalists and buyers who copy its numbers owe readers those limits alongside the ranking.</p>
