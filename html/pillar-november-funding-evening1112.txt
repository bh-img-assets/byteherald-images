<p>Twelve verified artificial intelligence funding rounds were announced between November 1 and November 12, 2026, and they total $17.96 billion. OpenAI alone accounts for $15 billion of that, about 84 percent, which leaves eleven companies sharing the remaining $2.96 billion. The month's story is not that AI funding is booming or busting. It is that the money has concentrated into one round at the top, while the rest of the market raises rounds a hundred times smaller, and the gap between the two tells you more about where AI investment stands than any single valuation does. This ledger ranks all twelve rounds, with the valuation, the lead investors, and the date for each, and it states what it counts and what it leaves out, because a funding table is only useful if the reader can see its edges.</p>

<h2>The concentration math</h2>
<p>The totals are where the ledger earns its keep, because no single-round announcement states them. The twelve rounds add to $17.955 billion, by our arithmetic. OpenAI's $15 billion is 83.5 percent of that, about 84 percent. The top two rounds, OpenAI and Fal, add to $16.3 billion, or 90.8 percent of the month. The top three, adding Fractile, reach $17.02 billion, or 94.8 percent. The other nine rounds combined total $935 million, or 5.2 percent of the month, which is less than Fal's $1.3 billion round alone. The median round is $82.5 million, the midpoint between Mount Thor's $45 million and Traversal's $120 million, while the mean is $1.496 billion, and the gap between the median and the mean is the concentration in one number: the typical November AI round is under $100 million, and the average is fifteen times higher only because of the top of the table.</p>
<p>The comparison with October makes the concentration sharper. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/six-ai-raises-added-up-232m-for-ai-that.html">Our October funding ledger</a> totaled $232.3 million across six verified rounds, so November's twelve rounds total 77.3 times October's, by our arithmetic, and OpenAI's single reported round is 64.6 times the entire October ledger. The market did not grow 77 times in a month; what changed is that the biggest companies came to market in the same twelve days, while October's rounds were all small. The wider market context points the other way: PitchBook data reported by the Wall Street Journal shows global AI venture funding passed $500 billion in the first nine months of 2026, but third-quarter deal value fell 43.4 percent from the second quarter to $94.3 billion, so November's $17.96 billion in twelve days is about 19 percent of the entire third quarter's global total, by our arithmetic, and almost all of it sits in one company. A funding market that is slowing overall and concentrating at the top is a different market from one that is booming everywhere, and the ledger is the evidence for which one November is.</p>
<h2>What the money is buying</h2>
<p>The twelve rounds split into three themes, and the split shows what investors are paying for now. The first theme is infrastructure to run AI: Fal sells the computing to run image, video, and audio models, and its chief executive told Bloomberg the company had to rate-limit customers in the summer because demand outran its compute, which is why a $1.3 billion round exists at all; Applied Compute builds an AI cloud for training and running custom models and reports a $100 million revenue run rate in under 18 months; Mount Thor rents Apple silicon at data center scale for agents that need macOS; Fractile builds AI chips, and its $720 million Series C at about $7 billion values a chip that has not shipped, with first delivery expected in 2027, according to <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/the-nvidia-challenger-ledger-10-chip.html">our ledger of the ten chip startups trying to break Nvidia's grip</a>. The second theme is agents and the tools to control them: LangChain sells the software layer between models and the business tasks agents carry out, and its valuation rose 2.4 times in a year, from $1.25 billion in October 2025 to $3 billion now; Kindo, WhiteMatter, Nebuly, and 4Minds all sell control, governance, measurement, or data readiness for enterprise agents, which is the work that has to happen before agents can be trusted with real systems. The third theme is a single health bet: Orakl Oncology builds virtual cancer patients from real tumors to predict how drugs will perform in trials, and its $34 million Series A is the only life sciences round in the table.</p>
<p>Two details in the table deserve a closer look because they show how valuations are actually set. Fal's $16 billion valuation is double its $8 billion valuation from April, and Bloomberg reported annualized revenue above $800 million, which puts the valuation at under 20 times annualized revenue, by our arithmetic, a multiple that only makes sense if the revenue keeps growing at the current rate. LangChain's $3 billion valuation, by contrast, comes with no revenue figure disclosed at all, and the company plans to nearly double its headcount to 850 by the end of 2027, which means investors are pricing the agent tooling layer on its position, not its current sales. The difference is the point: in the same month, one company is valued on revenue and another on position, and the ledger records both without pretending they are the same kind of number.</p>
<h2>What this ledger counts, and what it does not</h2>
<p>The scope is the twelve largest artificial intelligence funding rounds announced between November 1 and November 12, 2026, verified from company announcements, wire releases, tier-one reporting, or specialist funding datasets, and the cutoff is the date of this article. The ledger covers AI-first companies, meaning firms whose product is AI software, models, or AI infrastructure, and it counts announced round sizes, not money already received. Amounts are in US dollars; Orakl's round was announced as 30 million euros, about $34 million, and the table uses the dollar figure the company and the reporting gave.</p>
<p>Five categories are outside the table, and each is excluded for a stated reason rather than silently dropped. Zipline raised $1.5 billion at a $21.5 billion valuation in the same week, but it is a drone delivery and logistics company, not an AI-first firm, so it is out of scope even though it would rank second by size. CesiumAstro raised $730 million and Northwood Space $375 million, but both are space and defense companies. Nooks took $500 million from General Catalyst's Customer Value Fund, but that is non-dilutive financing that shares in returns on sales and marketing spend instead of taking an ownership stake, so it is not an equity round. Nebius announced $40 billion in planned spending and Norwest raised a $4 billion fund, but a spending plan and a fund are not rounds. Anthropic's listing is a public offering, not a private round, and it is covered in <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/anthropics-644b-ipo-file-every-number.html">our audit of its IPO filing</a>. Fal's $350 million Series E, led by Sequoia in April, was disclosed alongside the Series F but is April money, so it is excluded from the $1.3 billion figure and from the total. Smaller rounds below $15 million, military drone and biotech enzyme rounds, and rounds whose amount was not disclosed are outside this table, and a later round announced after November 12 belongs in a later edition, not this one.</p>
<h2>How to read a funding round</h2>
<p>A funding table is easy to misread, so the four distinctions that matter are worth stating once. First, pre-money and post-money are different numbers: OpenAI's $1.25 trillion is pre-money, the value before the new cash, while its March $852 billion was post-money, the value including the cash just raised, so comparing them directly overstates the step-up unless the $122 billion from March is subtracted first. Second, reported and announced are different claims: eleven rows in this table are company announcements, and one, OpenAI, is a report from CNBC citing unnamed sources, with the round not formally closed, so the $15 billion is the least certain number in the table and the article says so. Third, new money and total raised are different numbers: Kindo raised $30 million in November and $57.6 million in total, and WhiteMatter raised $20 million in November and $24 million in total, and only the November money belongs in a November total. Fourth, a valuation is not revenue: Fal reports annualized revenue above $800 million against a $16 billion valuation, while LangChain discloses no revenue against a $3 billion valuation, and neither number tells you whether the company makes money, only what investors will pay for a share of it. Read with those four distinctions in mind, the table says something simple: November's AI funding is real, it is large, and almost all of it went to one company.</p>
