<p>In a single week in late October 2026, the question of who an AI agent is stopped being theoretical and became a purchasing problem. Home Depot's chief information officer said her company will welcome shopping agents only if they show identification. Bank of America told customers never to hand their banking passwords to an AI assistant. Socure launched a product that detects agents, scores them, and ties each one to a verified person. Infisical raised 40 million dollars to give agents access without ever giving them credentials. And Meta and Sierra's draft Personal Agent Protocol, published weeks earlier, suddenly looked less like a white paper and more like a job description. An AI agent needs four things before a business should trust it: a verifiable identity, a named person behind it, a stated permission, and an audit trail. No single product on the market today supplies all four, which is why the identity stack is now a stack, not a product.</p>

<h2>From blocking agents to identifying them</h2>
<p>The identity debate is the second act of a story ByteHerald has been tracking all month. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/the-great-agent-lockout-who-blocks-ai.html">The Great Agent Lockout: Who Blocks AI Agents, and the Cost</a> documented the blocking wave: publishers, retailers, and banks shutting agents out because they could not tell a legitimate agent from a scraper, and in several cases because the agent signed in as the customer and clicked through pages built for people. Blocking works, but it costs the business the sale and costs the customer the convenience, and it fails the moment a best customer arrives via an agent anyway. Socure's chief executive put the point bluntly in the company's launch release: most companies still treat agents like bots and block them, and that strategy stops working the moment their best customers try to engage through Muse or Instinct. Identification is the alternative to blocking, and the five approaches below are the market's first serious attempts at it. They differ in who runs them, what the business actually learns, and what they leave unsolved, which is why the comparison matters more than any single launch.</p>

<h2>The four questions every agent ID must answer</h2>
<p>Strip away the vendor language and every approach above is trying to answer the same four questions. First, which agent is this: a named software actor with a public identity a business can look up, or an anonymous browser session that merely looks human. The Personal Agent Protocol answers this with a public identity address that serves the agent's name, logo, and signing keys, so a business can verify signatures against keys it fetched itself. Second, whose behalf is it acting on: the person behind the agent must be identified separately from the agent, because an agent without a principal is just a bot with better manners. The protocol gives each person an opaque identifier that differs per business and is never derived from personal information, and Socure's approach binds the agent to a verified person or business in its identity graph. Third, what permission was granted: read only, or permission to change things, and for which transaction. Fourth, what trail remains: a record the business can audit when something goes wrong. A credential that answers only the first question is a name tag, not an identity system, and the diagram below is the test to apply to any vendor claim in this space.</p>

<h2>The bank warning is the liability story</h2>
<p>The sharpest document in this whole stack is not a product launch but a customer email. Bank of America emailed customers in October 2026 under the title Protecting your finances in the AI World, telling them never to share a username and password with another person or an outside service, including an AI-enabled tool or agent that offers to act on their behalf, and warning that customers are responsible for activity resulting from access they shared. TechCrunch reported the email on October 27, 2026, and a bank spokesperson confirmed it was authentic. The warning matters because it exposes the architecture underneath most consumer agents today: the agent logs in as the customer, with the customer's password, which means the bank cannot distinguish the agent from the customer, cannot scope what the agent may do, and falls back on the oldest rule in banking, which is that sharing your credentials is your problem. Reporting around the warning adds that Morgan Stanley has begun blocking third party agents while JPMorgan, Bank of America, and Citi restrict them, with most institutions moving toward read-only access of the kind already granted to budgeting apps. Read-only access is a holding pattern, not a solution: it lets an agent look but not act, which protects the bank while stranding the use case customers actually want, which is an agent that pays, books, and files on their behalf. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/googles-universal-cart-ai-shopping-wars.html">Google's Universal Cart: The AI Shopping Wars, Tracked</a> shows where that use case is heading in retail: a checkout layer built for agents needs an identity layer underneath it, because a payment authorization from an unidentified agent is a fraud department's worst day, not a feature.</p>

<h2>What to buy, what to pilot, and what to wait for</h2>
<p>A buyer assembling an agent identity stack in late 2026 should treat the five approaches as layers, not rivals. Detection is the buy now layer: if a business cannot see agent traffic, it cannot govern it, and Socure's detection layer is generally available today, with the caveat that its 99.99 percent accuracy figure for identifying named consumer agents such as Muse and Instinct comes from controlled lab tests across 700 telemetry variables, not from production traffic at large. Vaulted access is the pilot layer: Infisical's model of applying credentials only at request time, so the agent never holds a password or key, directly fixes the exact failure Bank of America warned about, and its preview status means a pilot should test scope granularity before any production rollout. The Personal Agent Protocol is the watch layer: its design is sound, its launch coalition is serious, and its draft status plus the differing participation reports (later coverage lists 35 design partners including OpenAI, while early reporting said OpenAI and Anthropic were not on board at launch) mean no buyer should build solely on it yet. The retailer and bank postures are the constraint layer: Home Depot's demand for identification plus proof of a real customer, and the banks' move to read-only access, together define what any agent must present to be allowed in at all. Home Depot's second concern deserves emphasis because it is the one vendors rarely mention: the retailer does not want to lose contact with its customer, particularly contractors in its Pro Rewards program, which means agent identity is also a customer relationship question, not just a security control. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/every-major-labs-ai-safety-framework.html">Every Major Lab's AI Safety Framework, Compared</a> shows the same gap from the lab side: frameworks promise oversight, triggers, and gates, but none of them answers the question a retailer or a bank actually asks when an agent arrives, which is who are you, who sent you, and what exactly may you do. The identity stack is the mechanism those promises have been missing, and October 2026 is the month it stopped being optional.</p>
