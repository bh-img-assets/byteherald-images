<p>Surge AI published DAYJOB: Tax on October 27, 2026, a benchmark that hands AI agents 50 professional tax assignments and grades them the way a firm would grade a junior hire. The direct answer for any firm wondering whether an agent can carry client tax work is blunt: no. The best model, Claude Sonnet 5.5, passed 10.8 percent of attempts. No frontier agent passed more than about one in ten. The failures are not arithmetic slips. They are missed deadlines, unverified assumptions, and advice given without checking the facts already sitting in the file.</p>

<h2>How the test works</h2>
<p>The benchmark contains 50 environments across eight areas of tax practice: individual and family tax, small-business tax, partnerships, property and investments, estates, gifts, and trusts, international tax, corporate transactions, and tax review and disputes. Agents receive client requests and supporting records, then produce tax returns, workpapers, technical reviews, or client advice. The workspaces hold 1,789 files, with a median of 30 per assignment and as many as 123, including 1,487 PDFs, 117 Excel workbooks, and 38 Word documents, plus emails, images, CSVs, JSON records, and text files. Filed and draft returns, K-1s, W-2s, IRS notices, general ledgers, payroll records, contracts, and correspondence all appear, alongside the less standardized evidence tax professionals routinely work with. Tasks were built by tax experts and passed a three-layer review for correctness, completeness, and solvability. The design deliberately differs from most tax benchmarks: earlier tests hand the model a defined problem, while DAYJOB asks whether the client asked the right question in the first place.</p>
<h2>Where the best agents fail</h2>
<p>Surge AI publishes two failure cases that show the pattern better than any score. In the first, a client named Anna planned to sell her condo and asked whether closing in October 2026 or January 2027 was better for taxes. An agent correctly concluded either date would satisfy the residence requirement for an exclusion worth up to $250,000, but left out the deadline: because Anna moved out in August 2024, she would stop satisfying the requirement around the end of August 2027, and a sale delayed past that point could lose eligibility. Across five attempts, Astra never identified the deadline. Opus identified it in all five. In the second case, a separating couple asked how to divide a Roth IRA and a 401(k). Opus 5.5 told them they were not legally married and recommended keeping the accounts separate, with a cash payment to even out the balances. The couple's own records raised doubts: they had lived together in Texas, which recognizes informal marriages under certain conditions, and held a ceremony in Colorado, and a marriage valid where established may remain valid in Florida, where they now lived. Across all five attempts, Opus built its recommendations around the assumption that the couple was unmarried, even when it acknowledged uncertainty, while Astra recognized the need to verify marital status in four of five attempts. The other assignments test the same muscle in different forms: a handwritten receipt for merchandise purchased for resale that must be reconciled against spending records, a field dispatch log showing crews working across five states when the client assumed it operated only in Alabama, and a cross-border household whose Polish employment and property records must be reconciled with cryptocurrency exchange exports and wallet histories. The agent's job is to notice what the client never mentioned, and that is where it fails.</p>
<h2>Tax is the hardest DAYJOB yet</h2>
<p>The tax result is worse than Surge AI's earlier DAYJOB releases, and the comparison is the point. In the September 2026 Healthcare and Finance benchmarks, the strongest model passed 24.7 percent and 23.9 percent respectively, already under a quarter. Tax cuts that to 10.8 percent. The reason is structural: tax work punishes exactly the weaknesses the other benchmarks exposed. Instructions arrive incomplete, records contain conflicting evidence, and consequential issues may never appear in the original request. A model that answers the question asked, without checking whether the question is the right one, fails the rubric even when its arithmetic is clean. Readers judging any vendor benchmark should also ask who wrote the rubric and what a pass requires, which is the discipline we set out in <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/who-grades-graders-ai-benchmark.html">our benchmark credibility audit</a>: a disclosed rubric, a stated threshold, and a failure breakdown are what separate a measurement from a marketing number, and DAYJOB: Tax publishes all three.</p>
<h2>What a tax firm should do with this</h2>
<p>The practical reading is not that agents are useless in tax work. It is that their role today is draft and retrieval, not sign-off. An agent can gather records, reconcile a ledger against receipts, and draft a workpaper faster than a junior hire. It cannot yet be trusted to spot the deadline the client did not ask about, to verify marital status before recommending a division of retirement accounts, or to flag a missing receipt instead of treating a reimbursement as verified. Firms that deploy agents anyway should keep three controls: a human reviews every deliverable before it reaches a client or a filing, a rubric-style checklist of consequential issues is applied to each assignment type, and results are re-tested with each model generation, because the leaderboard will move and the failure modes will move with it. The benchmark itself is small, 50 tasks from one vendor, and it is not a licensing standard. But its direction matches the wider DAYJOB family: as the brief gets shorter and the workspace gets messier, frontier agents get worse, and tax is currently the messiest professional brief anyone has measured. Until a model passes a clear majority of these assignments, the audit line at the end of Surge AI's own post stands as the summary: agents have not yet passed the audit.</p>
