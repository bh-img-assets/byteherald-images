<p>The grid connection queue is now the binding constraint on the AI buildout, and the labs have stopped waiting for it. Anthropic reportedly spent about 3.4 billion dollars on 1.7 gigawatts of gas generators in a late-September deal reported on November 8. Google bought a Wyoming campus in November and is putting 702 megawatts of Bloom fuel cells on it. xAI is running converted jet engines in Mississippi. The pattern is the same in every case: a data center that waits for the grid energizes hardware that is already behind, so the biggest compute builders have started acting like utilities.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/anthropics-34b-generators-it-bought.html">Anthropic's generator purchase</a>, reported November 8, was described by <a href="https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/09/anthropic-joins-ais-natural-gas-frenzy-buys-3-4b-worth-of-turbines/">the Financial Times</a> as the largest known single power-equipment purchase by an AI lab, and it is not an outlier. The rest of the ledger is quieter: turbine orders, fuel-cell deployments, and pipeline deals that never make a press release.</p>

<p>The ledger understates the shift, because most of it never gets announced. A tracker counted 59 behind-the-meter data center projects in mid-2026, totaling about 90 gigawatts announced against about 2 gigawatts actually operating, according to <a href="https://cleanview.co/reports/behind-the-meter-data-centers">Cleanview</a>. More than 25 percent of planned US data center capacity is now behind the meter, and 92 percent of the projects were announced since the start of 2025. The equipment makers see it in their order books: <a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1996810/000199681026000147/gev2q2026form8-k.pdf">GE Vernova</a>'s gas equipment backlog plus slot reservations rose from 100 gigawatts to 116 gigawatts in the second quarter of 2026, with a firm backlog of 53 gigawatts and reservations of 63 gigawatts.</p>

<h2>The two clocks</h2>
<p>The reason is arithmetic, not ideology. A frontier GPU generation stays commercially competitive for roughly two or three years. A data center shell takes roughly one to two years to build. A grid connection for a large new load often takes many years: the United States interconnection queues hold more than 2,600 gigawatts, with typical waits of five to seven years in PJM, according to Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory queue data. Connecting 100 megawatts in Northern Virginia takes about seven years, according to <a href="https://www.iea.org/reports/powering-artificial-intelligence">the International Energy Agency</a>. A facility that sits in the queue until the end of the decade will energize hardware that is already behind. Converted jet-engine units can have a data center running in roughly 24 months, according to Rystad Energy, and on-site gas generation takes months to a couple of years, permit-dependent. The delays are already forcing business decisions: Microsoft withdrew as anchor customer for Nscale's data center in Loughton, England, after the local utility said it could not deliver enough power until the early-to-mid 2030s.</p>

<h2>What behind the meter means</h2>
<p>Power made on the customer's side of the grid connection point is called behind the meter. For a data center, it means generating its own electricity next to the building, using gas turbines, gas engines, or fuel cells, instead of waiting for the utility to deliver it. There are four common structures. On-site thermal generation puts gas turbines or reciprocating engines at the campus, sometimes as bridge power until the grid arrives, then kept as backup. Fuel cells arrive in modular units, usually gas-fed, and deploy fast but cost more per megawatt-hour. Co-location puts the data center next to an existing power plant, often nuclear, and takes the output before it reaches the grid, which raises regulatory questions about grid cost allocation and reliability. A grid-delivered power purchase agreement, plus firming with storage or backup, is the slowest route because it still needs interconnection. Most 2026 campuses mix several of these: on-site gas as bridge power, a power purchase agreement for the rest. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/ais-power-and-water-bill-datacenter.html">We tallied what AI consumes in our October power and water ledger; this is the grid's answer to that demand</a>.</p>
<h2>The catches</h2>
<p>Buying your own power has its own queues. Turbine models are effectively sold out through 2031, and permitting, fuel supply, and maintenance vary widely by location. Converted jet engines carry real compromises: they are less fuel-efficient than turbines designed for stationary power plants and produce higher emissions per megawatt. State permits for xAI's Mississippi units allow carbon monoxide emissions at twice the rate of other turbines at the site, and a 25 percent higher limit for volatile organic compounds. In Vineland, New Jersey, state regulators fined data center operator DataOne roughly 1.07 million dollars after a drone survey found 62 gas generators running without air permits at a facility reportedly powering Microsoft Copilot workloads. Oracle filed a force majeure notice on its 2.45 gigawatt Project Jupiter campus in New Mexico after a natural gas pipeline serving the site fell behind schedule, an admission that even a developer building its own gas supply chain can get stuck waiting on upstream infrastructure it does not control. And not every lab is buying in: Google told <a href="https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/03/data-centers-are-turning-to-old-jet-engines-as-the-wait-for-power-grows/">the New York Times</a> it has opted to wait for grid-connected power rather than use converted engines. The emissions question is open: the International Energy Agency puts electricity-related emissions from data centers at around 150 million tonnes of carbon dioxide today, rising to about 350 million tonnes by 2035 under its central scenario.</p>
<h2>What to watch</h2>
<p>Whether the grid adapts will decide whether private power is a bridge or a permanent feature. The International Energy Agency's October report identified 134 policies and regulations across 34 jurisdictions covering data center integration, nearly 40 percent of them announced or implemented in the preceding 12 months, and estimated that non-firm connections, which allow electricity demand to be restricted during periods of network stress, could unlock an additional 750 to 900 gigawatts of grid hosting capacity worldwide. The agency's six principles center on transparency about planned demand, predictable connection procedures, a holistic approach to energy and water efficiency, fair allocation of infrastructure costs, and frameworks that let data centers support grid flexibility. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission ordered in December 2025 that PJM's tariff was unjust and unreasonable for co-located load and directed new transmission service options. The numbers to track are the operating base, not the announcements: 2 gigawatts running against 90 announced. If the operating number starts climbing fast, the labs have become utilities in fact. If it stalls, the queue will have beaten them too.</p>
