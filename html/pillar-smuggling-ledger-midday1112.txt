<p>Five charged cases, about $3 billion in hardware, and one pattern. Between August 2025 and October 2026, United States prosecutors charged five separate operations with moving advanced Nvidia chips to China in violation of export controls, and the cases add up to roughly $3.02 billion in quantified value, plus tens of millions more in a case where prosecutors never put a total on the shipments. Two defendants have already pleaded guilty, including the first AI smuggling conviction on record. One of the cases was stopped before the hardware moved. And beside the charged total sits a reported $7.5 billion smuggling ring that no prosecutor has charged, which is why it is not in the total. This is the full ledger, case by case, with the amounts, the routes, the charges, and the outcomes, and with the honest distinction the single-case coverage leaves out: what was shipped, what was only sought, and what is merely alleged.</p>

<p>The total is ByteHerald arithmetic, and the arithmetic needs its caveats in the open. The four quantified charged cases add to about $3.02 billion: $2.5 billion, $300 million, $160 million, and $61.79 million. The ALX case adds tens of millions more that prosecutors never totaled. The $2.5 billion and the $300 million are amounts prosecutors alleged, not amounts a court has found, and no conviction in either case establishes those amounts. The $61.79 million was sought and blocked, so it never shipped, and counting it as moved hardware would overstate the flow. The $160 million is the only figure attached to a guilty plea, which makes it the only proven number in the table. The $7.5 billion is a reported allegation, not a charged case, and it stays out of the total for that reason.</p>

<h2>How the chips moved</h2>
<p>The five cases share a shape, and the shape is the story. Singapore and Malaysia appear in two of the five charged cases, as the transshipment point where the paperwork changes and the destination disappears. ALX Solutions, run by Chuan Geng of Pasadena and Shiwei Yang of El Monte, shipped more than 20 times between October 2022 and July 2025 through freight forwarders in Singapore and Malaysia, declaring end users in Singapore and Japan while the payments came from companies in Hong Kong and China, including a $1 million payment from a China-based company in January 2024. The company bought more than 200 Nvidia H100 chips from Supermicro between August 2023 and July 2024, and on one 2023 invoice valued at $28,453,855 it told Supermicro the devices were ordered for a Singapore-based customer. Earthmade Computer, run by Greg Lui of San Gabriel, California, used the same two countries to move more than $300 million in servers between 2023 and 2024, with falsified export paperwork, and received more than $176 million in payments from two Malaysian transshipment companies. The falsified end user and the third-country forwarder are the two moves that recur across the cases, which is why the enforcement keeps returning to the same two countries.</p>
<p>The other two cases show the route wearing different clothes. In the Supermicro case, prosecutors say a Southeast Asian company bought about $2.5 billion of servers between 2024 and 2025, ostensibly for its own use, and the servers were assembled in the United States, shipped to Supermicro's facilities in Taiwan, and handed off at a separate location to the intermediary. At least $510 million in servers are confirmed to have reached Chinese soil, according to the indictment, and prosecutors say a substantial portion was diverted to China. The three charged are Yih-Shyan "Wally" Liaw, 71, Supermicro's co-founder, director, and senior vice president of business development, Ruei-Tsang Chang, a general manager in Supermicro's Taiwan office, and Ting-Wei Sun, a third-party broker prosecutors call a fixer. In the Thailand case, two United States citizens and a Hong Kong national conspired from mid-2023 to buy servers from a California hardware company and ship them to Thailand with China as the ultimate destination, placing a purchase order for 232 servers worth $61,788,560 in December 2023 and later asking for 500 Nvidia H100 graphics processors to be exported to another Thailand company. The pattern in all five is the same shape: a legitimate-looking buyer, a plausible intermediate destination, and a payment trail that points to China.</p>
<h2>What was caught, and what was blocked</h2>
<p>The ledger also records where the system worked, because the cases that were stopped are as informative as the ones that moved. In the Thailand case, the order never shipped. The Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security received an anonymous email tip in early 2024 that the buyers were transshipping export-controlled commodities to China, the server company put the purchase order on hold and refunded the partial payment after the chip maker's vetting raised suspicions, and the three men were arrested only after the paper trail had already stopped the hardware. Matthew Kelly was stopped at Newark International Airport on February 13, 2026, after flying in from Rome, and his phone held a WhatsApp group named "GPU Partnership" with messages about securing products to ship to China through Thailand. Zheng was arrested on March 22, 2026, and Kelly and English surrendered on March 25, 2026. Nvidia's response to the case was that its due diligence process works well, and that the would-be smugglers failed to clear it and did not receive graphics processors from the company. That is a claim about a single blocked order, not about the whole market, and the ledger keeps it in that frame.</p>
<p>Enforcement has also moved outside the courtroom. On October 15, 2026, Malaysian customs seized 30 server units containing advanced AI chips at Kuala Lumpur International Airport. The same airport had produced a seizure four months earlier: on June 5, 2026, <a href="https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/malaysia-customs-seizes-ai-chips-worth-13-mln-kuala-lumpur-airport-2026-06-26/">customs officers found 72 server units</a> containing advanced AI chips, worth about $12.9 million, falsely declared as computer components and bound for re-export without the required permit. In June 2026, Supermicro said it had been working closely with Taiwanese authorities to prevent the deceptive acquisition of its servers, and that the authorities had seized 50 servers that had been deceptively acquired after being sold by Supermicro to an authorized reseller. The company also said it is not the target of the investigation and that its Taiwan offices were not raided, correcting reports from earlier in the week. The distinction matters for the ledger: the company is a witness and a victim of deception in the Taiwan strand, and a defendant in no case, while its co-founder is a defendant in the largest case on the board.</p>

<h2>The $7.5 billion allegation, placed</h2>
<p>The number that dominates the coverage is not in the ledger's total, and the reason is worth stating plainly. The Information reported in November 2026 that an alleged smuggling network routed roughly 240,000 restricted Nvidia processors worth about $7.5 billion into China through intermediaries in Singapore and Malaysia, supplying some of China's largest technology companies. The report says the bulk of the volume moved through a network involving Alice Huang, a former executive at Singapore-based Megaspeed, which allegedly acted as a front to import $4.6 billion worth of AI chips between 2023 and 2025, with the ultimate destination reportedly Shanghai Century Litong, a Chinese firm established in June 2023 that secured preferred cloud partner status with Nvidia by October of the same year, and whose parent company reported $180 million in AI computing revenue using the restricted hardware. United States authorities separately suspected Megaspeed affiliates in Malaysia and Indonesia of diverting about $2 billion in Nvidia H100 and H800 chips to China, according to an account of a New York Times investigation, and the investigation reportedly began in late 2024 after a Commerce Department official found Nvidia equipment still boxed at a Megaspeed data center in Malaysia. None of this has produced a charge. The $7.5 billion is a reported estimate of a network's volume, not a figure in an indictment, and treating it as proven would repeat the mistake the ledger exists to prevent. It belongs in the story as the allegation that frames the enforcement question, and outside the total until a prosecutor files it.</p>
<p>The allegation also connects to the week's biggest story. The same Alice Huang is the businesswoman at the center of the reports around Jensen Huang's resignation as Nvidia's chief executive on November 12, 2026, and Reuters reported the same day that Chinese AI firms are rushing to rent Nvidia-powered computing capacity outside China, worried the scandal could spur Washington to tighten restrictions on their remote use of advanced processors. The demand side of the ledger is not in doubt: the chips are worth smuggling because the buyers cannot buy them, and the buyers cannot buy them because the controls work at the front door, which is why the back door exists. The startups trying to supply that demand legally are the subject of <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/the-nvidia-challenger-ledger-10-chip.html">our ledger of 10 chip startups and $15 billion raised</a>, and the controls themselves are mapped in <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/the-great-decoupling-every-ai-export.html">our two-sided export-control map</a>.</p>
<h2>What the ledger means</h2>
<p>Three conclusions survive the caveats. First, the enforcement record is real and growing: one conviction, four more charged cases, and three seizures in fourteen months, with the first conviction in an AI smuggling case ever recorded in December 2025, when the Justice Department announced that Alan Hao Hsu and Hao Global had pleaded guilty to moving at least $160 million in H100 and H200 graphics processors. Hsu, 43, of Missouri City, Texas, agreed to buy 3,872 H100 and 3,160 H200 processors for $160,815,000, shipped them under the fabricated brand name Sandkyan to bypass customs, and received more than $50 million in wire transfers from China. The same operation, called Operation Gatekeeper, later produced the arrests of Fanyue Gong, 43, a Chinese citizen living in New York, on December 3, 2025, and Benlin Yuan, 58, a Canadian citizen and chief executive of a United States subsidiary of a Beijing technology company, on November 28, 2025, and prosecutors say the scheme had run since at least November 2023. The operation remains ongoing, with additional enforcement actions possible.</p>
<p>Second, the controls leak at the paperwork layer, not the border. Every case in the ledger was defeated, or nearly defeated, by a false end-user declaration, a third-country forwarder, or a payment from a different company than the one that received the shipment. The hardware is not the hard part to move. The hard part is the lie about where it is going, and the enforcement that catches it is tips, phone searches, and vendor diligence, not scanners. That is why the blocked Thailand order matters as much as the $2.5 billion that moved: the system caught one because an anonymous tip reached the Bureau of Industry and Security and the vendor asked a question, and missed the other because the buyer looked legitimate for two years.</p>
<p>Third, the policy is moving in both directions at once, and the ledger catches it mid-swing. In December 2025, President Donald Trump announced the administration would approve H200 sales to China under a framework requiring a 25 percent tax remitted to the United States government, which means the newest restricted chip can now be sold legally while the older H100 remains the smugglers' prize. The next test is whether the Huang resignation tightens the remote-use rules for Chinese firms renting capacity abroad, which is the question Reuters reported the firms themselves are now asking. The ledger will be updated as the cases move: Sun pleaded guilty on October 8, 2026, Liaw has pleaded not guilty, and Chang has not appeared, the Lui case is at the indictment stage, the Hsu sentencing was set for February 18, 2026, and the $7.5 billion allegation is either the next indictment or the next correction.</p>
<h2>How export controls actually work</h2>
<p>The controls are a licensing rule, not a wall. The Commerce Department restricts advanced AI chips for China based on total processing performance thresholds, and a sale needs a license that the department can deny. The chips can be sold to licensed buyers in third countries, which is where the transshipment problem lives: a server sold legally to a company in Singapore or Malaysia can be re-exported to China without the original seller knowing, unless the seller checks the end user and the payment trail. That is why Singapore and Malaysia recur in the ledger, and why the United States has pushed both countries on enforcement. The controls also change: thresholds move, new chips are added, and frameworks like the December 2025 H200 decision can open a legal channel for one chip while another stays closed. A reader who wants to judge any future smuggling claim should ask three questions. Who was the declared end user, and who paid. Whether the hardware actually moved, or only the order. And whether the number comes from an indictment, a guilty plea, or a report, because those three sources do not carry the same weight, and this ledger is built on keeping them apart.
