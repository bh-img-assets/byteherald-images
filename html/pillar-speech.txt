<p>Artificial Analysis now rates 62 speech-to-text models, and 15 of them carry open weights. The direct answer for anyone choosing an open transcription model is this: the best open models sit at 2.8 percent error, but the headline tie between Mistral Voxtral Small and Nvidia Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe is a rounding artifact, and accuracy alone picks the wrong model for most buyers. Hardware, price, speed, and whether the audio is live or recorded matter as much as the error rate, and the two public leaderboards measure different audio, so they must never be merged into one ranking.</p>

<p>Artificial Analysis lists Voxtral Small first among open-weights models and Nemotron second, both displayed as 2.8 percent. The ranking order is the tell: unrounded, Voxtral Small sits at 2.77 percent and Nemotron at 2.81 percent, a gap of four hundredths of a point. Nvidia's own materials call the result a tie for first, which is fair as a description of practical accuracy and wrong as a description of the leaderboard, where one model is first and the other is second. The gap is small enough that a different audio sample could flip it, and that is precisely why the table above shows unrounded figures where they are published. A buyer who treats 2.8 and 2.8 as identical will not be misled much on accuracy, but a journalist who reports a tie is reporting the rounding, not the ranking.</p>

<h2>Two boards, two different tests</h2>
<p>AA-WER version 2 is a non-streaming index: about eight hours of audio, time-weighted across three datasets, with AA-AgentTalk at 50 percent, VoxPopuli-Cleaned-AA at 25 percent, and Earnings22-Cleaned-AA at 25 percent. It rewards accuracy on recorded, real-world speech with varied accents and domain language. The Hugging Face Open ASR Leaderboard's English track is a different set of recordings with a different scoring mix: Nemotron leads it among open-weights models at 5.33 percent average word error rate, with Qwen3-ASR-1.7B next in this table at 5.4 percent and Granite Speech at 5.68 percent. Those two numbers, 2.81 percent and 5.33 percent for the same Nemotron model, do not contradict each other, because they measure different audio. Any ranking that sorts models by mixing one board's numbers with the other's is sorting noise. The table above keeps the boards in separate columns for that reason, and any buyer comparing two models should compare them inside one column, not across columns. A second entry warning applies: Artificial Analysis's provider table shows a 3.4 percent figure for a DeepInfra-hosted Transcribe entry, so which entry you read changes the number. The model-level figures in the table above are the ones to quote.</p>
<h2>What the closed models still win</h2>
<p>The open field does not lead the whole board. Across all 62 models Artificial Analysis evaluates, the most accurate is Fun-Realtime-ASR-preview at 1.7 percent AA-WER, followed by MAI-Transcribe-2 at 2.0 percent, Voxtral Small Transcribe 3 at 2.1 percent, Scribe v2 from ElevenLabs at 2.2 percent, and Voxtral Mini Transcribe 3 at 2.2 percent. The open best at 2.8 percent trails the overall leader by more than a full point, which is a real gap on transcription: roughly one extra wrong word in every hundred. Price and speed tell a different story, and they are where open and small models compete. The cheapest transcription on the board is StepAudio 2.5 ASR at $0.3667 per 1,000 minutes, followed by Modulate STT Batch English VFast at $0.417 and Wizper at $0.50. The fastest is Parakeet TDT 0.6B V3 at 675.6 times real time, followed by Nova-3 at 625.6 times and Nova-3 through Telnyx at 500.9 times. None of those leaders is the accuracy leader, which is the point: the board has no single winner, only winners per constraint.</p>
<h2>Pick by the job</h2>
<p>For accuracy-first recorded transcription, the choice is Voxtral Small or Nemotron, separated by four hundredths of a point on AA-WER, with Nemotron leading the Hugging Face English track. For self-hosting on one GPU, Nemotron is the clearer pick: about 4.3 billion active parameters against about 24 billion for Voxtral Small, in a 30-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts design where most of the network stays quiet on any given token. That sparsity is what lets it run from edge hardware to data-center GPUs, and it connects to the wider small-model story we covered in <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/small-ais-big-scoreboard-what-4gb-model.html">our 4GB model scoreboard</a>, where the usable model is the one that fits the hardware you actually own. For budget transcription at scale, Canary Qwen 2.5B at 4.3 percent AA-WER trades about one and a half points of accuracy for a much smaller footprint. For edge and on-device work, Qwen3-ASR-1.7B at 5.4 percent on the English track is the small-model candidate, with the caveat that it has no published AA-WER to compare. And for any buyer, two limits come before price: Nemotron is built for recorded audio, not live streams, with files up to one hour and up to eight speakers labeled, and AA-WER itself is a non-streaming index, so neither number in this article says anything about live caption quality. Test live audio separately, on your own recordings, before signing anything.</p>
<h2>What to watch</h2>
<p>Three things will move this ranking. First, Artificial Analysis updates its board as new models arrive, and the 15-model open field will grow; treat every figure here as an October 29, 2026 snapshot, not a permanent order. Second, the unrounded gap between first and second is small enough that a new dataset version could flip it, so watch the unrounded figures, not the displayed tie. Third, the interesting contest is no longer open versus closed on raw accuracy, where closed models still lead, but open versus closed on the full job: transcription plus translation plus speaker labels plus question answering in one checkpoint, which is the bundle Nemotron sells and which no single accuracy number captures. The next model that wins this board will probably win it on the bundle and the hardware it runs on, not on four hundredths of a point.
