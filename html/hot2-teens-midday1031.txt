<p>Nearly two-thirds of tweens and teens use AI chatbots, with use reaching 77 percent among teens and 49 percent among tweens, and only 11 percent of teen users say they enjoy it a lot, according to Common Sense Media's 2026 Census, <a href="https://www.npr.org/2026/10/30/nx-s1-6022278/ai-artificial-intelligence-teens-jed-common-sense-media">as reported by NPR on October 30, 2026</a>. The headline is not that young people found chatbots. It is that they found them early, use them heavily, and mostly do not love the experience.</p>

<h2>Two surveys, two different questions</h2>
<p>The NPR report rests on two surveys, and the most common mistake is to treat their numbers as a contradiction. They are not. Common Sense Media's Census, a nationally representative survey of 1,211 youth ages 9 to 17, asked about AI tools used in free time and found 65 percent overall, 77 percent of teens and 49 percent of tweens. The JED Foundation surveyed 5,500 students ages 9 to 17 in schools across the country and found 90 percent have used an AI chatbot at all. Free-time use and ever used are different questions, and the gap between 65 percent and 90 percent is exactly what you would expect when one survey asks about a habit and the other asks about any use.</p>
<p>The JED survey adds the purpose split that the Census headline misses: nearly two-thirds of respondents use a chatbot both for schoolwork and as a companion, or when stressed, lonely, anxious or overwhelmed. That dual use, homework help and emotional support in the same tool, is the fact parents and schools most need to understand, because the rules for a homework tool and the rules for a confidant are not the same rules.</p>

<h2>The age ladder, and the age gate that did not hold</h2>
<p>Use rises with age, but it starts earlier than the rules allow. Nearly half of 9 to 12 year olds have used an AI chatbot or companion, according to the Census as reported by NPR, and by age 13 nearly three-quarters have used chatbots. Most popular chatbots set a minimum age of 13. The ladder therefore shows a gate that is widely passed before it is reached, not a cliff at 13 where use suddenly begins.</p>
<p>That distinction matters for how adults respond. A rule that is already bypassed by nearly half of the younger group cannot be enforced by repeating it. It has to be enforced by design, by supervision, or by accepting that under-13 use is a fact to manage rather than a violation to discover. The surveys do not say which response works, and this article does not claim one does.</p>
<h2>The enjoyment gap</h2>
<p>The Census finding with the most signal is the one least quoted: among users on the surveyed day, both tweens and teens spent a median of one hour with AI, yet only 11 percent of teens say they enjoy it a lot. That is the largest use-to-enjoyment gap in the Census, and it reframes the story. Teenagers are not flocking to chatbots because they love them. They are using a tool that is available, useful for schoolwork, and present in moments of stress, while rating the experience as ordinary.</p>
<p>For readers tracking how communities set their own AI rules, <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/every-forum-and-platform-that-banned-ai.html">ByteHerald's database of every forum and platform that banned AI content shows the same gap between a rule and a practice, with six communities banning AI content and one reversing its ban</a>. Age minimums, community bans and school policies are all versions of the same problem: writing a rule is easy, and making it match how people actually behave is the hard part.</p>
<h2>What the surveys do not show</h2>
<p>Three limits keep this story honest. First, both surveys rely on young people reporting their own use, which is reliable for broad patterns and unreliable for exact hours or exact purposes. Second, the mental health picture is an association, not a cause: NPR reports that certain uses were linked with worse mental health symptoms, and that some young people turn to chatbots when already stressed, lonely, anxious or overwhelmed. A link in that direction does not prove chatbots caused the symptoms, and it does not prove they relieved them. Third, the Census report itself is the primary document a reader can check directly, while the JED figures here are those reported by NPR, not a full methods document.</p>
<p>None of that makes the headline less important. It makes the headline more specific: by age 13, chatbot use is normal, not exceptional; by the teen years, it is the majority experience; and the experience itself is rated as useful but unremarkable. Adults who plan for an exceptional case will miss the normal one.</p>
