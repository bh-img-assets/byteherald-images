<p>DoorDash said on its third quarter earnings call on October 29, 2026, that outside AI agents have placed only several hundred orders in total on its platform. Chief executive Tony Xu named Muse, Instinct, Fo and others, and said users still predominantly order through DoorDash's own app. The figure is the first hard number anyone has put on agentic commerce at scale, and it lands in the middle of a year in which investors treated AI shopping agents as an existential threat to every company that owns a customer relationship.</p>

<h2>What Xu actually said</h2>
<p>Xu was answering an analyst question about whether DoorDash wants to power food-ordering agents itself or leave that work to general-purpose services. His answer refused both panic and hype in the same breath. He said using agents to order is still a very dynamic area, that you never want to fight gravity, and that at the same time you do not want to be delusional. The number followed: looking at where AI agents are today, DoorDash is seeing maybe several hundred orders in total from some of these third-party agents. He added that DoorDash wants to learn more about agentic use cases, develop a healthy relationship with third-party agent makers, and that in the future there could be an opportunity to advertise inside these agents. Current volumes, by his own framing, are too low for that opportunity to have value now.</p>

<h2>How small is several hundred, really</h2>
<p>Scale is where this story earns its headline, so the arithmetic should be stated plainly and labeled honestly. DoorDash completes about 3 million deliveries on an average day. If outside agents had placed 1,000 orders in total, which is above the top of several hundred and therefore a generous upper bound, that total would equal about 0.03 percent of a single average day of deliveries. The 1,000 figure is ByteHerald's illustrative upper bound, not a DoorDash figure, and DoorDash has not published an exact count or the time period over which the agent orders accumulated. The chart below uses a logarithmic scale precisely because a linear chart would make the agent bar invisible, which is itself the finding.</p>

<h2>What the number does not measure</h2>
<p>Three limits keep this number from being the whole story, and any honest reading must carry all three. First, it counts third-party agents only, so orders placed through DoorDash's own Ask DoorDash chatbot, its text-to-order agent, its command-line agent, or its MCP connector are excluded by definition. DoorDash is building the agent channel itself while reporting that outside agents barely use it. Second, it cannot see an agent that drives an ordinary browser checkout, because to DoorDash's systems that looks like a person ordering quickly, not an agent. The true agent-originated volume is somewhat higher than several hundred, though on any reading it remains a rounding error. Third, the agents being measured are weeks old in practical terms, and judging a channel in its first weeks by its first totals will always understate it. None of those limits rescues the hype case, because even a generous correction leaves the same conclusion: agentic food ordering is real, tiny, and early.</p>
<h2>Why the platforms split on agents</h2>
<p>DoorDash's open stance sits between two opposite bets, and that split is the strategic story underneath the number. Amazon has spent the past year blocking personal shopping agents, while Walmart has rolled out the welcome mat with an agent gateway, per-agent profiles, and published terms. The difference is not philosophy. It is who owns the customer. A platform that believes its app, its prices, and its fulfillment are the product treats an agent as a middleman to be kept out. A platform that believes distribution is the product treats an agent as a new storefront to be courted. DoorDash, which owns neither the restaurant nor the diner's loyalty in full, is trying to have it both ways: build its own agent surfaces, keep the outside agents at arm's length but in conversation, and wait for volume to tell it which bet was right. <h2>The test that will settle it</h2>
<p>The number to watch is not the next headline about an agent launch. It is the next time Xu is asked the same question on an earnings call. If the total has moved by an order of magnitude, from hundreds to thousands or tens of thousands, then this quarter was a launch artifact and the channel is compounding. If it is still several hundred, then the constraint is not awareness or access but demand: people do not yet want a general agent to order their food, or the agents are not yet good enough at the job to earn repeat use. DoorDash's own surfaces are the control group for that test. If Ask DoorDash and text-to-order grow while outside agents stall, the lesson is that users want agents inside the app they already trust, not a new front door. If both stall, the lesson is simpler: food ordering was never the killer use case for agents, and the agent economy's first real volumes will come from somewhere else entirely. ByteHerald will update this article when DoorDash gives a new agent-order figure, rather than publishing a competing post.</p>
