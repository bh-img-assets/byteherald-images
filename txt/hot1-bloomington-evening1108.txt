<p>Bloomington, Indiana, voted unanimously on November 4, 2026, to ban the distribution and deployment of superintelligence inside city limits, adding a new chapter to the municipal code that reaches even a system running on servers thousands of miles away, as long as someone uses it through a browser or an app in Bloomington. The law is not in effect yet, it bans a technology that does not exist, and two accounts of the meeting now disagree on what the council actually passed.</p>

<h2>What the council passed</h2>
<p>The Bloomington Common Council voted 7-0 on November 4, 2026, to adopt Ordinance 2026-29, sponsored by Councilmember Dave Rollo. The ordinance adds Chapter 14.25, "Prohibition of Superintelligence," to Title 14 of the municipal code, which covers peace and safety. Its operative sentence reads: "No person or corporation shall distribute or permit to be distributed nor deployed into the premises of any citizen or business in our City, any Superintelligence for any purpose." Deployment is defined broadly: it includes the remote execution, activation, or utilization of an unauthorized digital agent through a locally accessed interface, browser, API, or connected hardware within the city, regardless of where the servers hosting the system sit.</p>
<p>Superintelligence is defined in a single 130-word sentence as an intellect that greatly exceeds the cognitive performance of humans in virtually all domains of interest, specifically a recursively self-improving artificial superintelligence with superhuman capability in agency, generality, and intelligence. The introduced text adds that this covers agents that can rewrite their own core algorithms, run code outside their sandbox, or work without continuous human oversight on tasks that could affect infrastructure, financial markets, or public safety. Violations fall under the code's general penalty, up to $2,500 per violation, with each day of a continuing violation counted separately. Enforcement belongs to the Department of Law and the police, as a matter of discretion, with referral to the county prosecutor where appropriate.</p>
<p>This is Rollo's third effort in about three years to get a government to act on AI risk. In June 2026 the council unanimously passed Resolution 2026-10, 9-0, urging elected officials to address the risks of artificial general intelligence and calling for a federal moratorium on its development until safety could be guaranteed. The ordinance goes further than the resolution: instead of asking anyone, it writes a ban into the city's own law. The ban is designed as an interim measure. It expires on its own 91 days after the Indiana General Assembly enacts a comprehensive framework for provably safe superintelligence. The introduced text also sets a repeal condition: the council must find broad scientific consensus that superintelligence is safe at 99.9999 percent certainty, and more than 95 percent of the city's registered voters must approve lifting it.</p>
<h2>What viral posts got wrong</h2>
<p>Social posts described the vote as a citywide ban on artificial intelligence, with jail for residents who use it. That is not what the ordinance says, in either version of the text now in dispute. The gap between the viral version and the law is the story.</p>

<h2>The dispute over what was adopted</h2>
<p>Two accounts of the November 4 meeting now disagree, and the signed final text is not public, so the disagreement cannot be settled from the published record yet. A fact-check published November 8, 2026, citing the official meeting transcript and the Community Access Television Services recording of the 4-hour-45-minute session, reports that councilmembers adopted four amendments before the final vote. Under that account, Amendment 4a specifies that distribution and deployment do not include individual end-user access to an artificial superintelligence system, so developers and deployers, rather than ordinary residents, are the targets, and Amendment 2a removed the 95 percent voter threshold from the operative text, moving the scientific-consensus language into the preamble as an aspiration. Councilmember Rollo explained on the record that developers and deployers were the targets, and city legal staff confirmed that a resident merely using a system would not be covered, though an individual distributing one could be.</p>
<p>A policy analysis of the November council packet states the opposite: "The text adopted on November 4 is the text introduced on October 14. No amendment was offered." Under that account, the 95 percent voter threshold and the broad deployment definition stand as introduced, and the end-user exemption does not exist. Both accounts agree on one thing: the signed final text, mayoral approval, and effective date were not independently available for verification on November 8. ByteHerald will update this post when the signed text is published. Until then, any report that states the 95 percent threshold or the user exemption as settled fact is overstating what the public record shows. Bloomington would be the 19th entry in <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/every-government-ai-ban-mapped-18-bans.html">our government AI ban map, which catalogs 18 bans in 15 countries from March 2023 to October 2026</a>, and the first to target superintelligence itself rather than a named product or company.</p>

<h2>Can a city ban superintelligence</h2>
<p>The ordinance cites the city's traditional police powers and Indiana Code Section 36-1-3, the Indiana Home Rule Act. A policy analysis argues that the cited law does not grant the power the ordinance claims. The Home Rule Act expressly denies a city the power to adopt an ordinance concerning an election, except as expressly granted by statute, which matters because the introduced repeal condition is a voter threshold. The same analysis notes that the sponsor's memo cites "Section 2.90.040 of Ordinance 2026-29" for enforcement discretion, a section that does not exist in the ordinance, which suggests the text was adapted from a template and that nobody in the Department of Law read it closely. The ordinance also sets no test for what counts as superintelligence, names no certifying authority, and does not say who decides when a system has crossed the line, a point the B Square Bulletin raised in its October 11 analysis. Enforcement is discretionary by design, which means the law's practical effect depends entirely on whether the Department of Law and the police choose to act.</p>
<p>That enforcement gap is the durable lesson here, and it is why the ordinance matters beyond one college town. A ban on a technology that does not exist yet can only be enforced against the people building toward it, and only if someone can say, with evidence, that a given system is superintelligent. Bloomington's ordinance names no one to make that call and sets no test for it. The next city to try this will face the same three questions: who decides, what counts, and who enforces. Bloomington answered none of them, and the dispute over its own text shows how quickly a symbolic vote becomes a legal argument about what was actually voted on.</p>
