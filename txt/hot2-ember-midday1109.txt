<p>Meta released Muse Ember on November 9, 2026, its smallest and most efficient model, built for the work AI agents repeat all day: tool calls, coding subtasks, routing, extraction, and long document parsing. It scores 36 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, the same score as Claude Haiku 5.5 at its best setting, and it does so at 14 cents per index task, about a third of what Haiku costs per task. No model scores higher for less per task, according to Artificial Analysis, which makes Ember the cheapest model ever to reach that score.</p>

It costs 10 cents per million input tokens and 50 cents per million output tokens, with a 1 million token context window and up to 512,000 output tokens.</p>
<h2>The cost-per-task table</h2>
<p>The list price is not the story. Ember, Haiku, and GPT-6 Luna all charge the same headline rates for input and output tokens, yet Ember costs far less per completed task, because most of an agent's bill is cached input and output volume, and Ember is cheaper on both. The table below is compiled from Artificial Analysis, the independent evaluator, with one addition from Meta's own pricing page.</p>

<p>The gap at the top of that table is the point. Ember is 8 points behind Muse Wonder on the index, yet Wonder costs about 50 times as much per task. Ember is 1 point behind Muse Spark 1.3 and costs about 17 times less per task. The efficient tier has stopped being a compromise on capability and become a different price for nearly the same score, at least on this index. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/flash-models-priced-cheapest-pick.html">We priced the flash tier the same way in October, when the cheapest pick changed by the hour; Ember resets the floor that comparison was built on, because it beats every flash model on cost per task while matching a frontier small model's score</a>.</p>

<h2>Why it costs a third</h2>
<p>The mechanism is in the fine print, and it is the part buyers should understand before they switch. Ember's input and output token prices are the same as Haiku 5.5 and GPT-6 Luna. The saving comes from two places: its cached input, which is most of the cost in Artificial Analysis's agentic evaluations, is 60 percent cheaper, and it uses roughly a third as many output tokens as Haiku to finish the same tasks. Cached input costs 0.4 cents per million tokens, against 10 cents for fresh input. The lesson is that the list price of a model and the price of a task are different numbers, and the gap between them is where the efficient tier wins.</p>
<h2>Where it wins, and where it does not</h2>
<p>The benchmark picture is mixed in exactly the way a small model should be, and Meta's announcement is unusually honest about the losses. On Artificial Analysis's runs, Ember scores 68.1 percent on AutomationBench-AA, level with Muse Wonder, and beats Haiku on Terminal-Bench 4.0, 40.9 percent against 32.8 percent. In its own coding agent, Muse Code, it scores 46 on the Coding Agent Index, ahead of Haiku in Claude Code at 37 and Luna in Codex at 31. But Haiku beats it on FrontierCode 2.0, 34.9 percent against 27.2 percent, and on the AA-Briefcase Elo rating, 1574 against 1521. The sharpest trade-off is knowledge: Ember's accuracy on the AA-Omniscience test is 21.1 percent, the lowest of the similarly intelligent models Artificial Analysis compared, against 34.7 percent for Haiku and 50.5 percent for Gemini 3.8 Flash. That is mostly because Ember declines to answer 64 percent of questions, which keeps its hallucination rate at 17.4 percent, half of Haiku's. It is a model that would rather say nothing than guess, which is a feature for agents that act on what they read and a limit for work that needs an answer.</p>
<p>One discrepancy deserves a note, because it shows how benchmark numbers travel. Meta's own table shows Haiku at 39.2 percent on Terminal-Bench 4.0, while Artificial Analysis's run shows Haiku at 32.8 percent on the same benchmark, and Meta's table shows Ember at 37.8 percent on AutomationBench-AA where Artificial Analysis reports 68.1 percent. The two sources run different harnesses and task slices, so the honest way to read them is to compare within one source's table, not across them. The head-to-head claims in this article use Artificial Analysis's table throughout.</p>
<h2>What it is for</h2>
<p>Ember is built for volume, not for the hardest problem in the room. Agents spend most of their tokens on many small, structured steps, and Ember is designed for exactly that: carrying a business workflow end to end, pulling records, calling tools in sequence, and updating systems across apps, with a 1 million token context window that can take in a full codebase or document set in a single call. It natively understands text, images, audio, and video, so agents can read screenshots, charts, and scanned documents, or work from voice memos and meeting recordings, without a separate model. Box, an early tester, put it on contract review: Yashodha Bhavnani, the company's vice president of AI products, said Ember can score a contract against a negotiation playbook clause by clause and land on the right overall risk rating. Meta trained Ember using Muse Wonder Full, its largest model, and it rounds out a portfolio with a clear division of labor: Wonder for the hardest problems, Spark for coding and agentic workflows, Ember for repeatable coding and agentic tasks at scale. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/the-computer-use-scoreboard-who-can.html">Ember also operates a computer directly from screenshots, scoring 69.5 on the partial-credit measure of OSWorld 2.1, which sits below the leaders on our computer-use scoreboard but ahead of GPT-6 Luna's 50.5</a>. It is available today on the Meta Model API and in Muse Code as muse-ember-1.0, and it supports the Responses API.</p>
<h2>What to watch</h2>
<p>First, whether the 14 cents per task holds outside Artificial Analysis's task mix, since cost per task depends on how much an agent caches and how many output tokens it burns. Second, whether the knowledge weakness matters in practice: a model that declines 64 percent of questions is safe, but it is also silent, and the buyers who need answers will test that boundary first. Third, whether the efficient tier keeps collapsing the price of intelligence this fast, because Ember's arrival makes the 2.39 dollar Spark and the 6.95 dollar Wonder look expensive for work that does not need their extra points. And fourth, whether Meta's claim holds that it has even more efficient models in training, because each new floor resets the comparison for every model above it.</p>
