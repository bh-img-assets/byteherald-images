<p>Mark Cuban says he gets an email about it every week. An older American writes to him, believing they bought a diabetes cure from him, or asking whether the cure he is supposedly selling actually works. He has never sold it. The ads selling it use an AI copy of his face and voice, and they run on Facebook. On August 21, 2026, Cuban told the Pivot podcast that Meta knows the ads are running and runs them anyway. The story returned to the front of Hacker News on November 5, 2026, where the $16 billion figure did the traveling. The number is real. What it counts is the part that needs reading carefully.</p>

<h2>What Cuban actually said</h2>
<p>Cuban's account is specific. On the August 21 episode of Pivot, the podcast hosted by Kara Swisher and Scott Galloway, he described receiving emails every week from older people who believe he is selling a diabetes cure. Some ask whether the product will work. Others say they have already paid $294 for a treatment advertised with his likeness. The ads are deepfakes built with an AI copy of his face and voice, Cuban said, and Meta runs them anyway. "Facebook knows the little old ladies are getting ripped off," he said, calling the platform "the worst of the worst."</p>
<p>One detail in his account cuts against the idea that the ads are crude fakes. Cuban said some of the ads include fine print acknowledging that the person on screen is AI-generated and that the clip is meant as entertainment. A disclosure that small, in a format older viewers may not read, does not stop the sale. It gives the platform a line to point to when the ad is flagged. The Federal Trade Commission has previously warned about scammers using doctored images and videos of Shark Tank personalities to promote questionable products, which is the same playbook with a different face.</p>
<p>There is an irony Cuban himself supplies. He co-founded Cost Plus Drugs, an online pharmacy that sells low-cost generic medications. The man warning about a fake diabetes cure runs a real low-cost pharmacy, and the scam works in part because the audience already trusts the face selling it.</p>
<h2>Where the $16 billion comes from</h2>
<p>The figure does not come from Cuban. It comes from internal Meta documents reported by Reuters on November 6, 2025. According to that reporting, the documents showed the company expected roughly $16 billion, or about 10 percent of its 2024 revenue, to flow in from ads pushing scams and prohibited products. That is the number now circulating next to Cuban's name, and it is a projection about a category, not a fine, a judgment, or an admission.</p>
<p>The same documents contain the number that sharpens the picture. Users across Meta's apps were seeing about 15 billion "higher risk" ads a day, the ones with obvious markers of fraud. One late 2024 document put that category's value at about $7 billion a year. A May 2025 presentation by Meta's own safety staff estimated that the company's platforms play a part in a third of the scams that succeed in the United States. And before Meta bans an advertiser, its automated systems must peg the account as at least 95 percent likely to be running a scam, according to the documents.</p>
<p>The Securities and Exchange Commission is looking into Meta over advertising tied to financial fraud, the same documents note. That is an inquiry, not a finding, and it belongs in the same category as the rest of the document figures: reported from leaked papers, not confirmed by a court.</p>

<h2>What Meta says in its defense</h2>
<p>Meta spokesman Andy Stone told Reuters the documents "present a selective view that distorts Meta's approach to fraud and scams," and called the company's 10 percent estimate "rough and overly-inclusive." The company said complaints from users about scam ads had fallen 58 percent over the prior 18 months, and that it had pulled down more than 134 million scam ads in 2025. Meta declined to give an updated revenue figure for the category, according to the reporting.</p>
<p>Both sides of that exchange can be true at once. A company can remove 134 million scam ads in a year and still show its users about 15 billion higher-risk ads a day. The removal count is a count of ads taken down. The exposure count is a count of views. They measure different things, and neither one, on its own, answers whether the platform is doing enough.</p>
<h2>What the victims' numbers say</h2>
<p>The FBI's 2025 Internet Crime Report gives the complaint side of the same story. People aged 60 and older told the bureau's Internet Crime Complaint Center they lost $7.7 billion in 2025, across 201,266 complaints, which is 59 percent more money than the year before. The bureau identified 3,143 AI-related complaints among people in that age group. Across all ages, the FBI recorded more than 22,000 complaints involving AI-related information, with reported losses exceeding $893 million.</p>
<p>Those figures let one piece of arithmetic be done honestly. Divide the $7.7 billion older Americans reported losing by the 201,266 complaints they filed, and the average complaint is worth about $38,000. The fake diabetes cure in Cuban's account costs $294. The average loss is about 130 times the price of the cure, which is what makes a $294 ad worth running at scale: the entry price is small, and the losses behind it are not.</p>
<p>That arithmetic is ByteHerald's, computed from the FBI's published totals, and it carries the usual caveat: an average blends small losses with large ones, and a single average does not describe any one victim's loss. The point of it is scale, not precision about any individual case.</p>

<h2>How to read the next deepfake endorsement</h2>
<p>The pattern in this story repeats with different faces, and the checks are the same each time. First, the product: a cure for a chronic condition, sold by a celebrity who has never mentioned it on their own channels. Second, the format: a video clip that looks like an interview or a podcast segment, with a purchase link the real person never posted. Third, the fine print: a line admitting the person is AI-generated or the clip is for entertainment, small enough to miss and large enough to cite later. Fourth, the price: low enough to buy without thinking, in a category where the real losses run far higher than the entry price.</p>
<p>The durable question is not whether any one ad is fake. It is who verifies what an automated system puts in front of a user before it reaches them. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/ai-agents-need-id-agent-identity-stack.html">Our comparison of the emerging agent identity stack</a> looks at who verifies an agent, what credential it carries, and who is liable when it acts, and the same question applies to an ad platform: without a verified check on what is shown and who it claims to be, the only thing standing between a viewer and a fake endorsement is the viewer's own skepticism, and the FBI's numbers say that is not holding.</p>
