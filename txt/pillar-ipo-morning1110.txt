<p>Anthropic set the price range for its initial public offering on November 10, 2026, in Amendment No. 2 to its Form S-1: 70 to 78 dollars a share for 825 million Class A shares, a deal that would raise as much as 64.4 billion dollars and list the company on Nasdaq under the ticker ANT. The filing answers the question investors have asked since Anthropic first filed on October 26: what is the company worth, and what exactly is for sale. This article is the complete file: every term in the offering, every valuation measure with the method behind it, and every financial figure in the prospectus, audited against the filing.</p>
<div class="separator" style="clear: both; text-align: center;"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bh-img-assets/byteherald-images/main/img/pillar-ipo-morning1110-hero.png" alt="Editorial graphic: the headline Anthropic's 64.4B IPO, Every Number, Audited, November 10, 2026" /></div>
<p>The roadshow launched the same day, with Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley leading the underwriting group. The registration statement is still preliminary and has not become effective, so the terms can still move, and they already have once: a draft circulated to banks the night before was retracted within hours. What follows is the file as it stands on November 10, with each number labeled for where it came from and what it counts.</p>

<p>Three features of the table deserve emphasis. First, nearly all of the raise is new money: about 62.5 billion dollars of the 64.35 billion dollar maximum goes to Anthropic, and only about 1.84 billion dollars goes to existing holders selling stock. Second, the 26 billion dollar private placement is separate from the public offering, and it is the deal's most telling detail: the companies buying in are Anthropic's own suppliers and customers, the chipmakers and cloud providers whose hardware and infrastructure the company rents. Third, every figure at the top of the range is a maximum implied by the range, not an amount already raised. The pricing document uses 74 dollars, the midpoint, in its estimated net-proceeds calculations. Share counts and price range are from Amendment No. 2 to Form S-1, filed November 10, 2026, <a href="https://www.reuters.com/business/anthropic-ipo-raise-much-644-billion-latest-draft-prospectus-shows-2026-11-10/">as reported by Reuters</a> and <a href="https://dealroom.co/news/170996-anthropic-sets-ipo-terms-up-to-69-3b-at-70-to-80-a-share-plus-26b-from-s/">Dealroom</a>.</p>
<h2>What the company is worth depends on how you count</h2>
<div class="separator" style="clear: both; text-align: center;"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bh-img-assets/byteherald-images/main/img/pillar-ipo-morning1110-chart1.png" alt="Chart: Anthropic's valuation by measure, in billions of dollars. May private round 965, IPO low on outstanding shares 916, IPO high on outstanding shares 1020, IPO high fully diluted 1400, discussed high 2000." /></div>
<p>The headline number is just over 1 trillion dollars. On the roughly 13.09 billion shares that would be outstanding after the offering and the concurrent placement, 78 dollars a share implies an equity value of about 1.02 trillion dollars, and 70 dollars implies about 916 billion dollars. That is the figure most outlets reported on November 10, and it is the right number to use if you are counting shares that exist today.</p>
<p>It is not the only count. On a fully diluted basis, counting the shares that could become outstanding, the range is higher. The filing's outstanding figure excludes about 1.77 billion shares issuable on outstanding options, with a weighted-average exercise price of 2.91 dollars, about 1.07 billion unvested restricted stock units, 1.92 billion shares reserved for a new 2026 equity plan, and 128 million shares reserved for an employee stock purchase plan. Adding those awards and reserves implies an equity value of about 1.26 trillion dollars at 70 dollars and about 1.40 trillion dollars at 78 dollars, by Dealroom's math from the filing's share counts. The Motley Fool's count, on about 16.9 billion fully diluted shares implied by the filing, lands at 1.19 trillion to 1.32 trillion dollars, rising to about 1.27 trillion to 1.41 trillion dollars once the new IPO and placement shares are added. The difference between the counts is a method difference, not an error: the fully diluted number counts promises of shares, the outstanding number counts shares that exist.</p>
<p>Two other figures frame the range. Anthropic was valued at 965 billion dollars in its private financing round in May. On the outstanding-share count, the low end of the IPO range, 916 billion dollars, is below that May price, and the high end, 1.02 trillion dollars, is about 6 percent above it. On the Motley Fool's fully diluted count with the new shares added, the range is 1.27 trillion to 1.41 trillion dollars, about 32 percent to 46 percent above the May valuation. And the Wall Street Journal reported figures of 1.5 trillion to 2 trillion dollars discussed among prospective investors in October, before the tech sell-off that followed the midterm elections cooled appetite. Those higher figures were never a binding offer price, and the filing prices the company well below them. Morningstar, as reported on November 10, put a fair value of 1.7 trillion dollars on Anthropic, above the IPO range. The gap between what investors discussed, what the filing prices, and what an analyst thinks it is worth is the honest picture of this IPO: the market has already marked the company down once, and the filing prices it down again.</p>
<h2>The retracted draft</h2>
<p>The range in the November 10 amendment is already a revision. Late on November 9, Anthropic sent bankers a copy of the filing showing about 867 million shares at 70 to 80 dollars, which at the top would have raised about 69.3 billion dollars. Within hours, the company emailed again to say the pricing details were inaccurate, according to Reuters, and the document would be updated. The revised draft lowers the ceiling by 2 dollars a share and cuts the maximum proceeds by roughly 5 billion dollars.</p>
<p>The retraction matters for two reasons. First, it makes the November 10 terms more substantive than the first indication: these are the numbers in a filed amendment, not a draft that went out by mistake. Second, it is a warning that the range can move again. Anthropic has already trimmed it once, and the final offer price, the closing, and the first day of trading are all still subject to the IPO process. Anyone quoting the 70 to 78 dollar range as final is quoting a draft.</p>

<p>The revenue growth is the fastest ever reported by a company of this size: 16.956 billion dollars in three months, twelve times the same quarter a year earlier, and 47 percent more than the previous quarter. The loss needs more care than the revenue. The nine-month net loss of 104.04 billion dollars is dominated by about 94.32 billion dollars of fair-value changes on convertible notes and other financial instruments, which are non-cash accounting charges, not money the company spent. The operating loss is the cleaner read on whether the business itself makes money, and it is much smaller: 567 million dollars in the third quarter, after an operating profit in the second quarter, as computing costs rose. The company returned to an operating loss because short-term compute got pricier, which is the same pressure behind the largest number in the filing.</p>
<p>That number is about 900.9 billion dollars of purchase commitments and leases, substantially all of it for compute and infrastructure, entered into as of and after September 30, 2026, per Amendment No. 2 to the filing, which updates the 895.4 billion dollar figure in the November 2 amendment. It is the bill for the revenue growth, signed in advance. The filing gives the schedule behind it: about 70.7 billion dollars falls due in the 12 months after September 30, 2026, and about 451.6 billion dollars is expected before December 31, 2030, which leaves about 449.3 billion dollars due after 2030, by ByteHerald subtraction. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/the-32-trillion-dollar-ai-debt-nobody.html">Anthropic's commitments sit inside a much larger pile of signed AI obligations we tallied on November 3: about 3.2 trillion dollars across five companies, none of it on a balance sheet</a>. And the money the IPO raises is the money that pays for the power behind those commitments: <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/how-ai-buildout-is-paid-for-november.html">the 26 billion dollar placement is one more instrument in the financing wave paying for the buildout, which we mapped on November 2</a>. TechCrunch reported on November 9 that Anthropic bought 3.4 billion dollars worth of natural gas turbines, which is what buying your own power looks like when the grid cannot keep up. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/ai-spending-stalled-as-anthropic-nears.html">We covered the first numbers from the filing on November 6, when the valuation talk had already been marked down from 2 trillion dollars to between 1.2 and 1.3 trillion dollars</a>.</p>
<h2>When it trades</h2>
<p>The filing sets no date. The Wall Street Journal reported that Anthropic aims for its shares to start trading next Thursday or Friday, November 19 or 20, and Reuters reported on November 3 that the company is expected to go public before Thanksgiving, November 26, with an Australian retail share offer planned alongside the United States listing. Neither date is in the filing, the roadshow could start no earlier than November 10, and market conditions can still change the plan. The launch timing is also notable for what else Anthropic shipped the same week: CNBC reported on November 10 that the new Claude Fable and Mythos 5.2 models are the company's first frontier models since its chief executive called for a slowdown, which puts a product launch and an IPO roadshow in the same seven days.</p>
<h2>Five questions to ask before the first trade</h2>
<p>First, which share count is the valuation using. A headline that says 1.02 trillion dollars is counting outstanding shares; a headline that says 1.4 trillion dollars is counting everything that could become a share. Both are correct, and they answer different questions. Second, how much of the raise is new money. Here, almost all of it is: about 62.5 billion dollars of the 64.35 billion dollar maximum goes to Anthropic. Third, what the greenshoe adds. The underwriters' option for 123.75 million more shares would take the total to about 74.0 billion dollars at the top of the range, and it is exercised only if demand is strong. Fourth, whether the loss is operating or paper. The 104.04 billion dollar nine-month net loss is mostly a fair-value accounting charge; the operating loss of 1.260 billion dollars over the same period is the number that describes the business. Fifth, what the 900.9 billion dollars of commitments buys, and over how long. The filing gives the schedule, about 70.7 billion dollars due within a year of September 30, 2026 and about 451.6 billion dollars before the end of 2030, so the question is what the remaining roughly 449 billion dollars, due after 2030, is committed to, and whether the revenue growth that justifies it holds up that long.</p>
