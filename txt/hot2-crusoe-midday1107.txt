<p>Google bought Crusoe's unfinished Wyoming data center site on November 6, 2026, and plans to spend $17 billion building it into its first Wyoming campus, after raising concerns about the project's cost and construction timeline under Crusoe's management. Crusoe had tried and failed to land Google as a customer for the project. The purchase price was not disclosed, and the $17 billion is Google's planned investment in the campus, not what it paid Crusoe. What Google actually bought is a site plus groundwork: site selection, government approvals, and a power plan, finished by a developer that could not finish the project itself.</p>

<h2>What Google bought</h2>
<p>Crusoe <a href="https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/11/06/3397845/0/en/crusoe-announces-wyoming-data-center-campus-update.html">announced on November 6, 2026</a> that its site in southeast Wyoming had been acquired by Google, and that the transaction marked the completion of its pre-development work on the project. That work covered site selection, governmental approvals, and power strategy, developed jointly with Tallgrass Energy, a Blackstone-backed infrastructure company. Google announced the day before, on November 5, that it would make a long-term $17 billion investment to build its first data center in Wyoming, in Laramie County, with Tallgrass Energy, Black Hills Energy, American Electric Power, and Related Digital as partners. The campus was first announced by Crusoe and Tallgrass in July 2025 as a 1.8-gigawatt development with the potential to expand to 10 gigawatts. Those figures describe development ambitions, not completed capacity: nothing has been built yet, and Google's $17 billion is a plan for construction, not a price for a finished building.</p>
<h2>The power math</h2>
<p>The power plan is the most concrete part of the announcement, and it shows how far the project still has to go. Google's Wyoming plan includes a contracted 702-megawatt fuel-cell development with carbon capture and storage, using Bloom Energy fuel cells, and an air-cooled data center intended to minimize strain on local water resources. Against the campus's initial 1.8-gigawatt scale, 702 megawatts of fuel cells covers 39 percent of the power the campus would need at first, and against the eventual 10-gigawatt ambition, it covers 7 percent. The rest of the power has to come from elsewhere: Tallgrass is developing a natural gas power hub next door, and Black Hills Energy holds agreements to serve the planned campus that run through 2048. The chart below puts the three numbers on one scale, because the gap between them is the story: the fuel cells are real and contracted, the 1.8 gigawatts is a plan, and the 10 gigawatts is an ambition.</p>

<h2>How the sale happened</h2>
<p>The sale was not sudden. Bloomberg first reported in June 2026 that Crusoe had sold the unfinished project to Google, and in the same month Crusoe said it had paused work at a customer's request, while the utility arranging electricity supplies said Crusoe was no longer the development partner and the project itself was continuing. TechCrunch reported on November 6, 2026 that Google, which Crusoe had tried and failed to land as a customer for the project, is taking over development after reportedly raising concerns about its cost and construction timeline under Crusoe's management. The November announcement makes the ownership change contractual. It does not resolve the questions the June reporting raised: whether the 10-gigawatt target survives, what role Tallgrass keeps, or what schedule Google will hold itself to. Crusoe's own statement describes the deal as the completion of its engagement, not as a joint venture going forward.</p>
<h2>The never-built ledger, updated</h2>
<p>This sale is the newest row in a ledger ByteHerald has been tracking all year. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/most-planned-ai-datacenters-will-never.html">Our October audit of announced AI datacenters found 509 gigawatts of planned capacity, of which 229 gigawatts would never be built, 127 delayed, and 153 on schedule</a>. Crusoe's Wyoming campus belongs in a fourth category the audit did not need at the time: a project that changed hands rather than died. The developer did not go away. Crusoe, recently valued at $30.9 billion, closed a $3.9 billion Series F round in September 2026, says it has more than 6 gigawatts of contracted capacity and over 40 gigawatts in its development pipeline, and is still developing another Google data center campus in Armstrong County, outside Amarillo, Texas. It is also building a campus in Abilene, Texas, in phases for Oracle and OpenAI, plus a separate 900-megawatt campus next door for Microsoft, with its first building expected in mid-2027. The valuation-versus-delivery gap is the point: a company worth $30.9 billion, fresh from a $3.9 billion raise, still lost its flagship project to the customer it could not sign. Money was not the constraint. Delivery was.</p>
<h2>What to watch</h2>
<p>Three numbers will tell whether Google's takeover changes the outcome. The first is whether the 10-gigawatt eventual scale survives the handoff, or whether Google builds a smaller campus than Crusoe pitched. The second is the construction schedule, which Google has not published and which was the reported reason for the takeover in the first place. The third is the power mix: whether the 702 megawatts of fuel cells plus the Tallgrass gas hub actually arrive on time, because a campus without power is a field with a fence. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/ais-power-and-water-bill-datacenter.html">The resource ledger we published in October shows the same constraint everywhere</a>: the industry's announced capacity keeps growing faster than the power and water to run it. Wyoming is the latest test of whether a hyperscaler can close that gap by buying the project outright, instead of waiting for a developer to deliver it.</p>
