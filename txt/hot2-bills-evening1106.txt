<p>Democrats plan to introduce a flood of bills targeting artificial intelligence systems and data-center development when they take control of Congress in January, <a href="https://www.reuters.com/business/media-telecom/democrats-planning-flood-ai-bills-when-they-take-control-congress-2026-11-06/">Reuters reported</a> on November 6, 2026. The plans mark a sharp shift from the Republican-led Congress, which focused on promoting AI development and passed no legislation restricting the technology. The bills face steep odds while President Donald Trump remains in office, since he has repeatedly dismissed calls for mandatory AI rules.</p>

<h2>The three priorities</h2>
<p>The planned bills cluster around three targets, and each one reaches a different part of the AI industry.</p>

<tr><td>Power bills</td><td>Shield consumers from rising electricity costs tied to data centers</td><td>Senator Chuck Schumer, expected majority leader</td></tr>
<tr><td>Independent testing</td><td>Require outside experts to evaluate leading AI models against safety standards</td><td>Representative Lori Trahan of Massachusetts</td></tr>
<tr><td>Liability</td><td>Clarify when AI companies can be held liable for harm caused by their products</td><td>Trahan; Representative Zoe Lofgren of California</td></tr>
</table>
<p>Schumer said on November 5, 2026, at a Washington event, that protecting consumers from higher energy costs linked to the rapid expansion of data centers would be among his priorities next year. Trahan plans to push legislation requiring independent experts to evaluate leading models, her deputy chief of staff Francis Grubar told Reuters. A November 5 legal analysis by Foley and Lardner notes that Trahan's CLAIM Act discussion draft would create a federal right to sue developers over AI conduct that would be unlawful if a person committed it. Lofgren, who is seeking to chair the House Science Committee, said Democrats would focus on developing responsible regulation while still supporting innovation. Representative Greg Casar of Texas, chair of the Congressional Progressive Caucus, said this week that AI executives should be called to testify under oath.</p>

<h2>Why power bills lead</h2>
<p>Electricity costs are the most politically potent of the three priorities. A household that sees its power bill climb does not need a seminar on transformer architecture to form an opinion, and voters have turned against the rapid development of data centers and their extensive power needs, according to Reuters. The report does not detail the mechanism the bills would use, and no count of bills, vote schedule, or final text has been reported. What is confirmed is the direction: the cost of the AI buildout, and who pays for it, is now a legislative question, not just a utility one.</p>
<p>That is the same buildout we have been tracking all year. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/five-companies-828-billion-2026-ai.html">The five-company capex ledger we published in October</a> adds up to $828 billion in one year of spending, and the power to run it has to come from somewhere. When the bill for that power lands on residential customers, it becomes a political problem, and this is the first Congress that looks ready to treat it as one.</p>
<h2>The passage math</h2>
<p>Writing bills and passing them are different tasks. Any legislation faces steep odds while President Trump remains in the White House, and overriding a presidential veto requires two-thirds of lawmakers in both chambers. Most bills also need 60 Senate votes to overcome a filibuster. The Democrats' plan is a drafting calendar, not a vote schedule, and nothing in the reporting sets a date for any vote.</p>
<p>The politics behind the plan are clearer than the path. A Reuters poll published in October 2026 found that most US voters think the administration and Congress are not taking the risks of AI seriously enough and want the government to write new regulations. The bills are the consequence of that election, and the veto is the wall they run into.</p>
<h2>What to watch</h2>
<p>Three things decide whether this plan matters. First, the bill text: the priorities are public, but the mechanisms are not, and the difference between a disclosure rule and a cost-allocation rule is the whole fight. Second, whether any bill draws bipartisan support, since a veto-proof margin needs it. Third, whether the January majority treats AI as one package or as separate fights, since power costs, testing, and liability have different coalitions and different opponents. The plan is real, the sponsors are named, and the obstacle is named too.</p>
