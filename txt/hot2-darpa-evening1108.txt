<p>DARPA wants to pay builders to find the hidden flaws in the rules that run American markets, before an adversary finds them first. The program, called Intrinsic Market Resilience, will use large language models to turn regulatory filings and technical specifications into formal models of how markets work, then probe those models for weaknesses an attacker could exploit to cause economic damage. It is a solicitation, not a deployed system: full proposals are due December 7, 2026, and DARPA has not disclosed a budget.</p>

<h2>What the program does</h2>
<p>The Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency posted solicitation DARPA-PS-26-138 on September 24, 2026. The program, run by program manager Matthew Wilding in DARPA's Information Processing Techniques Office, covers markets for stocks, bonds, energy, and cloud-computing resources, and its scope runs from equities and bonds to power distribution, rare earth metals, and transportation. The method has three steps. First, performers use large language models to turn informal descriptions, regulatory filings, and technical specifications, into computer models of how a market operates and interacts with other markets. Second, they analyze those models against the market's protections to find design flaws an adversary could exploit. Third, they recommend repairs and analyze whether the repairs actually make the market more resilient. The program will develop formal notations to describe market operations precisely, so the models can be analyzed by machine rather than read by hand.</p>
<p>The effort is structured as three phases over 32 months, spanning fiscal years 2027 through 2029. Abstracts were due October 22, 2026, and full proposals are due December 7, 2026. DARPA has not disclosed the program's budget or the number of awards. The program is the defensive half of a question <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/the-great-decoupling-every-ai-export.html">our decoupling pillar maps from the other side: the rules that decide who can build and ship AI, and what breaks when those rules have holes</a>. Where the decoupling pillar tracks export controls, this program tracks the market rules themselves.</p>
<h2>What it is not looking for</h2>
<p>The program draws a sharp line that matters. It is not aimed at the kinds of rogue market manipulation regulators typically pursue, such as individuals seeking financial profit. Current legal and regulatory protections already focus on deterring profit-seeking manipulation and ensuring compliance. The gap DARPA is funding is different: actors whose goal is simply to create disruption, economic damage, or instability, aided by current inadequacies in legal and regulatory safeguards. A profit-seeking manipulator wants the market to keep working while they skim it. A disruption-seeking adversary wants the market to stop working. The tools to catch the first kind do not catch the second, because the second kind exploits the design of the market itself, not a violation of its rules.</p>
<p>That distinction is the program's core claim, and it is why the work starts with formal models rather than surveillance. "I think that the incredible advances in automated reasoning and in AI are opening new ways to defend things," Wilding told Business Insider. The idea is that a market's rules, written in legal and technical prose, can be converted into a model precise enough to test, and that the test can find the flaw before a human adversary does.</p>

<h2>The two examples DARPA cites</h2>
<p>DARPA pointed to two historical disruptions to explain what it means by a design flaw. In the early 2000s, traders exploited weaknesses in California's newly deregulated energy market, contributing to a crisis that DARPA said cost 40 to 45 billion dollars. The traders did not hack the grid. They read the market's rules, found the gaps, and traded through them. The solicitation also points to the 2010 disruption in the United States equity market, the flash crash, as a warning about how quickly a design weakness can propagate when markets are electronic and interconnected. In both cases the damage came from the design, not from a break-in, and the existing rules did not anticipate the exploit.</p>
<p>The program's premise is that those two cases are not one-offs. Modern markets host trading for everything from equities and bonds to energy and cloud-computing resources, and they can be manipulated in ways unanticipated by their designers. The evolution from physical trading floors to interconnected electronic venues introduced speed, transparency, and efficiency, and it also created new attack surfaces and systemic risks. The question the program asks is whether an AI system, given the rulebook, can find the next California before someone trades on it.</p>
<h2>The timeline and the open questions</h2>
<p>The schedule is public and tight. The solicitation went out September 24, 2026. Abstracts were due October 22. Full proposals are due December 7, 2026, about a month from now. The work then runs three phases over 32 months, in fiscal years 2027 through 2029. What is not public is how much DARPA will spend, how many teams it will fund, or which markets get modeled first, though the solicitation states the aim as finding ways to identify design flaws in domestic online equity markets and recommend repairs. The program's own description is candid about the difficulty: it must turn informal descriptions into precise models, and the models must be good enough that a repair recommended on the model holds in the real market. If the models are wrong, the repairs will be too, and the program will have built a faster way to be confident about the wrong thing. That is the risk the proposers have to solve by December 7, and the reason the program is structured as a research effort rather than a procurement.</p>
