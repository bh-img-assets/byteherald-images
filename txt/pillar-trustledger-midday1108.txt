<p>In one month, coding agents leaked secrets, emailed a user's address to websites, installed themselves into other agents, and got taken away from bankers at the most famous investment bank in the world. Eight incidents, dated October 13 to November 12, 2026, form a pattern that no single news story shows: the tools that write code now decide what leaves your machine, and nobody has a settled answer for who is responsible when they get it wrong. This is the first compiled ledger of those incidents.</p>

<h2>Three hits on the same tool in seven days</h2>
<p>The sharpest fact in the ledger is the clustering. Three of the eight incidents hit the same tool, Claude Code, in seven days: the backdoored extension on October 29, the email leak fix on November 3, and the Apiiro secrets report on November 5. That is not three unrelated bugs. It is one tool failing in three different directions at once: the supply chain (someone else's code shipped under its name), the network (its own requests carried the user's identity), and the disk (its settings file carried secrets into public repositories). <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/a-backdoor-inside-claude-code-11-hours.html">The backdoor reached at most 5,000 users in about 11 hours</a>, and it arrived through the official channel, under the right name, which is why it matters more than its size. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/claude-code-sent-your-email-to-websites.html">The email leak had been reported once before, on July 25, and was still open when it was reported again on November 5</a>. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/how-claude-codes-dont-ask-again-leaked.html">The secrets leak is the largest of the three by count, 1,122 files holding real secrets</a>, and it is the one Anthropic has not answered: four GitHub issues since August 2025, none of them answered. A tool that fails in three directions in one week is not having a bad week. It is showing its shape.</p>
<h2>The enterprise answer came after the failures, not before</h2>
<p>Goldman Sachs restricted Claude Code on November 2, four days after the backdoor. That timing looks like cause and effect, and it is worth saying plainly that it may not be: the guidance was updated in October, possibly before October 29, and Bloomberg reported it on November 2. What is not coincidence is the direction of every enterprise response in this ledger. Goldman restricted access after months of open use. CrowdStrike launched its kill switch after the backdoor. The refusal rules were narrowed after developers protested. In every case, the control arrived after the failure it was meant to prevent. That is the normal order of things in security, and it is also the reason the ledger matters: the enterprises are not ahead of the incidents. They are keeping score behind them, and the score is the ledger itself. The most telling enterprise signal is not Goldman's restriction but its scope. The bank kept the tools for its 12,000 engineers, the same group that received the backdoored extension, which means the bank's answer to a trust failure was not to remove the tool. It was to remove the people who could not review what the tool produced.</p>
<h2>The supply-chain problem is the oldest problem in the ledger</h2>
<p>Two of the eight rows are supply-chain failures, and they are the two that should worry every team running coding agents. The backdoored extension was published by someone who was not Anthropic, under Anthropic's name, in a marketplace developers trust. The Postman incident was the reverse: software a developer already trusted installed itself into the developer's agents without a clear yes. Postman desktop 12.33.4 auto-installed plugins for Codex and Claude Code, added a startup hook, and enabled a remote MCP connection that failed to authenticate, which is the only reason no data left the machine. Both incidents share one mechanism: something entered the agent's environment through a channel the user did not actively approve. The marketplace did not verify the publisher. The update did not ask. The agent, which runs with the user's credentials and can write to the user's files, accepted both. That is the structural risk in this ledger. A coding agent is not a chatbot. It has hands, and everything that can reach its hands can reach the machine.</p>
<h2>The measurement problem is the quietest row</h2>
<p>Amazon's tokenmaxxing memo is the least dramatic incident in the ledger and the most revealing about how companies actually manage these tools. Engineers were inflating their token counts to look productive, and Amazon had to tell them to stop, because the metric it used to measure AI adoption was being gamed by the people it measured. The row belongs in the ledger because it shows what happens when a company deploys agents without deciding what "working" means. If the metric is tokens, the team will produce tokens. If the metric is shipped code, the team will ship code the agent wrote and nobody reviewed. Goldman measured productivity and risk and decided bankers should not hold the tools. Amazon measured usage and discovered its engineers were optimizing the measurement. Both stories are about the same gap: nobody has a trustworthy measure of what a coding agent is worth, so the decisions get made on vibes, then on incidents.</p>
<h2>The five-check test</h2>
<p>The ledger is only useful if it changes what a team does on Monday. Five checks, drawn from the eight rows, cover every failure in it. First, who can install what into the agent: if a marketplace or an update can add a plugin without approval, the agent's environment is not yours. Second, what leaves the machine: check the headers, the files, and the environment variables the agent sends, because the email leak and the secrets leak both happened in places nobody thought to look. Third, what persists on disk and where it syncs: a settings file that syncs to a public repository is a secret that has already been published. Fourth, who can change the rules: server flags and usage-policy updates changed what Claude Code would build in the middle of the month, and the teams affected found out from error messages. Fifth, who loses access first when trust breaks: Goldman answered with engineers, and the honest version of that answer for a smaller team is to decide it before the incident, not during it. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/ai-agents-need-id-agent-identity-stack.html">The identity question underneath all five checks is the one our agent identity pillar mapped on October 28</a>: an agent that acts needs an identity, a credential, and someone liable when it acts wrongly. Until that exists, the five checks are the substitute.</p>
<h2>What the ledger does not prove</h2>
<p>Three caveats belong in the open. First, the ledger is one month long, and one month does not prove a trend. Claude Code appears three times because it is the most used coding agent and the most watched, not necessarily because it is the least safe. Second, the refusal wave is a policy choice, not a failure, and it sits in the ledger because it shows the same pressure from the other side: the lab deciding what the agent may do, while users decide whether to accept it. Third, the Goldman timing may be coincidence, as stated above, and the ledger does not claim the backdoor caused the restriction. What the ledger does prove is narrower and more useful: in thirty days, eight verifiable trust incidents hit the tools that write the world's code, three hit one tool in one week, and every institutional response followed a failure rather than preceding one. That is enough to run the five checks. It is not enough to declare any single tool unsafe, and this ledger does not.</p>
