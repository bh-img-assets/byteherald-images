<p>A Tennessee Uber driver lost his account without a clear explanation. He asked ChatGPT what to do. The chatbot helped him write a letter citing Uber's driver contract and asking to begin arbitration, and it suggested he email the letter to chief executive Dara Khosrowshahi rather than the generic arbitration inbox. About a week later, he could access his account again. "I followed AI, and I guess it worked," he told Business Insider on November 8, 2026. He is one of a growing number of gig workers using AI to fight deactivations on Uber, Instacart, Uber Eats and DoorDash.</p>

<h2>What the workers are doing</h2>
<p>The pattern Business Insider found is the same in both cases it profiled. A deactivated Instacart worker is using ChatGPT to challenge the company's claim that he did not follow the correct route to deliver an order. He has used the chatbot to write evidence reports and to estimate how much he has lost in wages since Instacart removed him earlier this year, and he has consulted it on when to send his latest response and whether he might find pro bono legal advice. "ChatGPT said to hold off on that for now," he said. "We're gonna see if we can get a lawyer." On Reddit pages for gig workers, other users have posted stories about using ChatGPT to write letters to Uber Eats and DoorDash requesting reinstatement, and some wrote that the AI-generated messages worked. The work the chatbots do is unglamorous and specific: summarize the evidence, draft the letter, cite the contract, time the reply. It is the paperwork of a legal fight, done for free, by workers who usually fight alone. That is the point. As independent contractors, Uber drivers and Instacart shoppers have few options when they try to take on their employers: the companies usually have wide latitude to terminate them, and lawyers are often hesitant to represent them because of the arbitration requirements in the companies' terms. Robert Bruno, a professor of labor and employment relations at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, put it plainly: "They're not locked out of being able to defend themselves because they lack the financial means to hire a very good labor attorney."</p>

<h2>The appeals scoreboard</h2>
<p>The reason a chatbot matters this much is that the appeal path decides the outcome. The numbers, from three different cities and three different years, point the same way. In New York City, the Asian American Legal Defense and Education Fund and the New York Taxi Workers Alliance surveyed 341 deactivated drivers for a report released in October 2025, and found that among 154 drivers who had appealed to Uber, 95 percent were still deactivated, and among 86 who appealed to Lyft, 98 percent were. In Seattle, a University of Washington study of 1,420 cases under the city's deactivation rights ordinance found that 80 percent of terminated drivers were reinstated when they had real due process, with a neutral arbitrator and representation. In New York City, the Independent Drivers Guild opened 4,800 deactivation cases in 2024 and reported that 90 percent of those drivers were successfully reinstated, many within days, and that it has helped 20,000 drivers get reinstated over the last five years. The baseline is bleak: Chicago city data recorded 30,881 rideshare deactivations between January 2019 and July 2026, and only 1,085 drivers, about 3.5 percent, were ever reinstated. Human Rights Watch surveyed 127 gig workers in Texas and found 40 had been deactivated, with nearly half later cleared of any wrongdoing. The figures come from different cities, different years and different methods, so they are not one clean experiment. But the direction is unmistakable: an appeal heard by the company itself almost never succeeds, and an appeal heard with representation and due process usually does. That is the gap the chatbots are filling, badly and for free, for workers who cannot afford the real thing.</p>
<h2>Both sides already use AI</h2>
<p>The workers are not the only ones bringing AI to this fight. Uber customer service employees often use AI to write responses to drivers' messages, Business Insider reported last month. Uber and Instacart have in the past declined to say what role AI plays in the initial decision to deactivate a worker, while insisting the final call is human: Uber said appeals of deactivation decisions are reviewed by "a real person," and Instacart said final deactivation decisions are "always reviewed by a human." An Uber spokesperson said the company uses "data and facts" to evaluate claims "regardless of how an appeal is submitted," and an Instacart spokesperson said the company does not "take deactivation lightly" and gives workers the chance to appeal. Neither company commented directly on workers using AI to fight deactivations. The shape of the contest is now clear: a worker's chatbot writes the appeal, the company's AI writes the response, and a human somewhere is supposed to decide. The law is starting to catch up. In June 2026, the International Labour Organization adopted Convention 193 on Decent Work in the Platform Economy by a vote of 406 to 8, the first binding international standard on algorithmic management, requiring governments that ratify it to ensure workers can request written explanations of significant automated decisions and get human review when a decision withholds pay or cuts access to work. In Seattle, the city's deactivation rights ordinance survived a challenge by Uber and Instacart in the Ninth Circuit in March 2026, and since enforcement began in January 2025 the city's labor office has assisted more than 30 workers in getting reactivated. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/the-agent-liability-ledger-who-pays.html">The liability question is the same one our agent liability ledger mapped on November 7</a>: when an automated system takes a person's livelihood, who is responsible for the decision, and what appeal exists. For gig workers, the answer so far has been: the worker, alone, with a chatbot.</p>
<h2>The limits, stated plainly</h2>
<p>Three limits belong in any honest account. First, the evidence for AI help is anecdotal. Two cases in one article, plus Reddit posts, do not prove that ChatGPT-written appeals work in general, and the Tennessee driver's reinstatement about a week after his letter is one case, not proof the letter caused it. Second, AI-generated briefs can introduce errors and undermine credibility, as Business Insider itself notes: the responses are written in an authoritative tone and can turn out official-looking documents with a prompt or two, and a wrong citation in an arbitration filing hurts the worker who files it. Bruno made the same point from the other side: using AI to generate evidence summaries is only one part of the process, and a worker still has to make a persuasive argument tailored to the specific arbitrator hearing the case. "I don't think you could just spit that out of Copilot or Claude," he said. Third, the scoreboard numbers are disputed. The Independent Drivers Guild dismissed the AALDEF report as "erroneous data," its president Brendan Sexton questioned how the 341 survey responses were gathered, and Uber said it could not confirm the guild's 4,000 figure. The guild's own appeals process, the one behind the 90 percent figure, has been on hold since July 2026, after New York City legislation required Uber and Lyft to participate in a new government-run hearing process through the city's OATH court system. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/courts-vs-ai-tricks-five-rulings-one.html">Courts have already begun sanctioning lawyers for AI-generated filings</a>, and the same credibility question now faces workers who cannot afford a lawyer at all. None of that makes the chatbot useless. It makes it what it is: the cheapest available advocate for people the system was not built to hear.</p>
