<p>Nvidia's board of directors said on Sunday, November 8, 2026, that it has opened an independent investigation after reports that chief executive Jensen Huang was the target of a foreign intelligence effort. Huang admitted in a message to the company that he had an affair with Alice Huang, a former executive at Singapore chip firm Megaspeed, and apologized to his family and colleagues, while the board said it has no reason to believe national security or Nvidia secrets were compromised.</p>
<h2>What the board announced</h2>
<p>The board's statement, published in the Nvidia newsroom on November 8, says a committee of independent directors, assisted by outside independent counsel, will oversee the investigation. The board said it prioritizes US national and economic security, which it called paramount to the interests of Nvidia and its shareholders, and that the inquiry will examine all available information. Huang fully supports the review and has committed to cooperating, the board said, adding that it has confidence in Huang and his leadership team and will provide further updates as appropriate.</p>
<p>TechCrunch, which reported the board's announcement on November 8, also reported that Huang published a message to the company admitting the affair. In it, Huang wrote, "I owe my family and all of you an apology. I was unfaithful to my wife. My actions hurt her and our children. There is no excuse for what I did. I am deeply sorry." He added that he had "fallen short" of his responsibility to the company.</p>

<h2>Why a board investigation matters</h2>
<p>When a board hands an inquiry to independent directors and outside counsel, it is separating the investigation from the management team under scrutiny. That structure matters here because the person at the center of the story is the chief executive himself, and because the board framed the inquiry around national and economic security rather than personal conduct alone. Nvidia had earlier said the pair's interactions were "not unusual" and that the company does not share classified information with customers. The board's statement goes further: it opens a formal inquiry while stating its current assessment that nothing was compromised.</p>
<p>The security framing also reflects where Nvidia sits in the US-China technology contest. Its chips are the hardware the AI race runs on, and they are already at the center of US export-control fights: <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/nvidia-chips-went-to-empty-office-us.html">ByteHerald reported on November 7 that Nvidia chips reached an empty Beijing office through a US-approved assembler</a>. What to watch next is whether the inquiry's findings are published, whether any disclosure follows, and whether the board's "no reason to believe" assessment holds once the inquiry has examined all available information.</p>
