<p>The House Foreign Affairs Committee released export licence data on November 19, 2026, showing the Commerce Department approved 879,868 advanced AI chips for Chinese companies between December 2025 and May 2026, out of 3.4 million requested. Six days earlier, Tom's Hardware reported a research firm's estimate that more than 1.5 gigawatts of banned Nvidia hardware had reached China in recent years, the equivalent of up to 1.5 million GPUs. One number is a licence count. The other is an analyst conversion. Congress now has both, and it wants tighter rules.</p>

<h2>The approved number: 879,868 of 3.4 million</h2>
<p>The Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security approved 879,868 chips across 21 licences, as of May 11, 2026, against applications covering 3,444,160 chips. That is roughly one in four. The approvals covered Nvidia's H200 and AMD's MI325X, the two chips at the centre of the export-control fight, and they ran from December 2025 through May 2026, the period after President Trump agreed in late 2025 to let Nvidia export the H200 to approved Chinese buyers. Committee Chair Brian Mast invoked his powers under the Export Control Reform Act to put the licence applications on the public record, which the committee called the most detailed public disclosure since the 2018 law expanded Commerce's powers.</p>

<p>The four named buyers account for 272,000 of the 879,868 approved chips; the committee's summary names no other buyers. Three of the four had their full requests approved. Alibaba, the exception, got 75,000 of the roughly 949,000 it sought. All four appear on the Pentagon's list of alleged Chinese military companies: Tencent was added in January 2025. Two limits on the data matter. The committee's records show none of the approved orders had shipped as of May 11, 2026, so approvals are not deliveries. And the document did not break down, buyer by buyer, which chip model each approval covered.</p>
<p>Nvidia said in a statement that the approvals were subject to strict conditions and limited volumes, and that it has since stopped shipping H200s to China. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said existing export restrictions are sufficient and saw no need for fresh Nvidia curbs, even as the committee's release gave policymakers who want a tougher crackdown their clearest ammunition yet.</p>

<h2>The smuggled number: 1.5 gigawatts, converted</h2>
<p>The second number comes from SemiAnalysis, a semiconductor research firm, in its latest newsletter as reported by Tom's Hardware on November 13, 2026. The firm estimates that more than 1.5 gigawatts of restricted Nvidia hardware reached China in recent years, and says that could be equivalent to up to 1.5 million GPUs. That is a conversion, not a counted unit total. SemiAnalysis does not disclose the exact number of accelerators shipped illegally, or their split between Nvidia's product generations, and the estimate excludes processors built to comply with US export rules, such as the H20 and H800.</p>
<p>The arithmetic behind the conversion is Tom's Hardware's own. If every chip were an H200 SXM rated at up to 700 watts, 1.5 gigawatts would mean about 2.142 million GPUs. If every chip were a B200 SXM rated at up to 1,000 watts, it would mean 1.5 million. A mixed deployment falls between those figures, so "up to 1.5 million" is the low end of the range, not the middle. The report also claims the Commerce Department reached a similar conclusion about the scale of unauthorised shipments, but names no Commerce document, and it does not explain how it converts accelerator quantities into power figures.</p>
<p>SemiAnalysis believes Jensen Huang's departure may have been driven not only by the revelations about his personal relationships, but also by the widespread illicit exports. The firm further speculates that some members of the US intelligence community sought to remove Huang because he opposed more aggressive restrictions. The report provides no direct evidence that intelligence agencies influenced Nvidia's board, or that Huang participated in unauthorised exports. Both parts of that claim are speculation, and this article reports them as speculation, not as findings.</p>
<h2>The cases behind the estimate</h2>
<p>The estimate rests on real prosecutions. In March 2026, prosecutors charged Supermicro co-founder Wally Liaw, Taiwan sales executive Steven Chang, and contractor Willy Sun, alleging about 2.5 billion dollars of servers were sold to a company in Southeast Asia, with 510 million dollars of them subsequently diverted to China and obtained by Alibaba. The defendants allegedly used substitute servers and transferred identification labels to conceal destinations during audits. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/the-ai-chip-smuggling-ledger-3-billion.html">That case is the largest row in our smuggling ledger, which compiled the charged diversion cases into one table</a>. A second case is newer: nine individuals associated with Nvidia, Albatron, Chief Telecom, Flying Tiger, and Supermicro in Taiwan are accused of arranging unauthorised exports of 130 Nvidia B300-based servers, 74 of which eventually reached China, using shell companies, forged documents, and declaring Japan as the destination. Taiwanese authorities discovered the operation during a drug-smuggling investigation. Both cases are still before the courts.</p>
<h2>The halt, and the 600,000 chips in Hong Kong</h2>
<p>The licensed trade is now closed from Nvidia's side. Interim chief executive Colette Kress told analysts on the November 17 earnings call that Nvidia sold 2.4 billion dollars of licensed Hopper data centre products to China in the fiscal third quarter, the quarter ended October 25, 2026, about 2.2 percent of the quarter's 107.6 billion dollars in data centre revenue. That figure covers the licensed Hopper line, not H200 sales alone. Then she closed the door: "Our guidance does not have any licensed Hopper data centre products included, and we are no longer shipping licensed Hopper data centre products to China." She gave no reason, and did not say whether the halt is permanent. Nvidia's fourth-quarter guidance of about 130 billion dollars includes no data centre compute revenue from China at all. About 600,000 H200 chips sit in storage in Hong Kong, awaiting shipment to the mainland, according to Reuters. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/us-ai-chip-rule-closes-rent-chip.html">The halt follows the Commerce Department's November 16 interim rule closing the rent-a-chip loophole, which we covered when it landed</a>.</p>
<h2>What Congress does next</h2>
<p>Both parties in Congress now want tighter rules, and they have named the mechanism. The Remote Access Security Act, first introduced in the House in April 2025, passed the House and stalled in the Senate, is receiving renewed interest. It would extend US export-control authority to remote access to advanced AI hardware hosted outside China, including data centres in Europe and the Middle East. SemiAnalysis reports that Nvidia and Oracle actively opposed it. Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer has said Huang and the Trump administration need to answer to Congress. Senate Banking Committee Chairman Tim Scott has sent letters to Huang and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick calling on them to publicly testify. House Speaker Mike Johnson has said there is growing consensus in Congress on limiting chip exports to China, according to Reuters, and that he personally supports restricting China's access to powerful AI chips. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/sec-probes-nvidia-huang-pitches-return.html">Huang is due to testify behind closed doors before House committees on December 1, as we reported last week</a>. The licence data gives Congress the numbers to ask about. The smuggling estimate gives it the question the numbers cannot answer: how much got through anyway.</p>
