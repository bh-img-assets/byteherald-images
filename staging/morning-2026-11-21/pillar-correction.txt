<p>On Friday, November 20, 2026, the S and P 500 and the Dow Jones Industrial Average joined the Nasdaq Composite in correction, each closing more than 10 percent below its record. The Nasdaq is down 18 percent from its October 6 record and has erased its gains for 2026. The Kobeissi Letter puts the loss at about 6 trillion dollars in 45 days. The question now is whether this is the AI bubble popping, or a correction. The numbers below are the scoreboard. The test at the end is how to tell.</p>

<p>Friday was the day the correction became general. The Dow fell 949.73 points, or 1.93 percent. The S and P 500 fell 265.44 points, or 3.70 percent, its seventh straight loss, its longest losing streak since February 2020. The Nasdaq fell 1,261.47 points, or 5.28 percent, its biggest one-day fall since November 5. The Nasdaq first closed in a correction on November 5; the S and P 500 and the Dow crossed the line on Friday. For the week, the S and P 500 fell 8.6 percent, the Nasdaq 10.0 percent, and the Dow 8.0 percent. Reuters puts it this way: the S and P 500 and the Dow logged their largest weekly percentage losses since April 2025, and the Nasdaq's weekly drop was its largest since March 2020. The Nasdaq 100 briefly traded 20 percent below its recent high during Friday's session, the usual threshold for a bear market, before recovering slightly into the close. The fear gauge, the VIX, rose 26 percent to close at 35.89, after briefly touching 40 during the session. Breadth was brutal: 38 new highs against 1,199 new lows on the New York Stock Exchange, and on the Nasdaq 928 stocks rose while 3,964 fell.</p>
<p>A correction is a fall of at least 10 percent from a recent closing high. It is a market convention, not an official designation: no exchange or regulator declares one. A bear market starts at 20 percent. By that yardstick, the Nasdaq Composite is about 2.4 percent above its bear-market line, and the Nasdaq 100 touched it intraday on Friday. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/anthropics-ipo-pop-is-gone-nasdaq-lost.html">The Nasdaq's slide began before Friday: our report on Anthropic's second day as a public company covered the index erasing its 2026 gains</a>. Friday made it a correction across the board.</p>

<h2>The 6 trillion dollars, in context</h2>
<p>The Kobeissi Letter puts the loss at about 6 trillion dollars over 45 days, an average of 225 billion dollars a day across the 31 trading days since the October 6 peak. The S and P 500 alone has shed more than 6 trillion dollars in about six weeks, and the constituents of the Nasdaq 100 have lost about 2 trillion dollars in combined market value so far in November. The single worst day was November 5, when the S and P 500 fell 4.4 percent, a record one-day loss of about 2.9 trillion dollars. That is a net figure across the period, not 31 straight down days: the Dow had its best post-midterm day since 1982 on November 4, the day Democrats won both chambers of Congress.</p>
<p>The 45 days, in order: October 6, the S and P 500 closes at a record 7,818.93 and Nvidia at a record 239.24 dollars. October 22, the AI sell-off begins. November 3 and 4, the midterm elections. November 5, the S and P 500 drops 4.4 percent. November 12, Jensen Huang resigns as Nvidia's chief executive after 33 years. November 20, the S and P 500 and the Dow close in correction. The causes the market itself names are the scale of AI capital spending, the Federal Reserve holding rates after its September hike, the midterm result, and the Nvidia turmoil. None of them is a single event. The correction is the sum of all of them.</p>
<h2>Bubble or correction? Five questions</h2>
<p>First, are earnings falling with prices? No. S and P 500 companies are reporting 31.9 percent year-over-year earnings growth for the third quarter of 2026, the strongest in at least four years. Nvidia reported revenue of 115.8 billion dollars last quarter, up 103 percent, while its stock fell 47.5 percent. A bubble popping usually comes with earnings collapsing. Here, earnings are still rising while prices fall.</p>
<p>Second, is the spending stopping? No, and that is the bear case. Amazon, Alphabet, Meta, and Microsoft spent a combined 201.9 billion dollars on capital expenditure in the third quarter of 2026, up 36.9 billion dollars from the quarter before, while their combined free cash flow was negative 26.4 billion dollars. The buildout is real, and it is being funded, not earned. If that gap closes because revenue catches up, the correction was a buying opportunity. If it closes because spending is cut, the correction becomes a repricing.</p>
<p>Third, what would falsify the bubble case? A quarter in which the big four keep spending and free cash flow turns positive, or a quarter in which AI revenue growth matches the spending. Fourth, what would falsify the correction case? A quarter in which earnings roll over, or a major name cuts capital spending. Fifth, what to watch: Huang's closed-door testimony before House committees on December 1, the Federal Reserve's next decision, and whether the Nasdaq holds above its bear-market line near 22,080, about 2.4 percent below Friday's close.</p>
<p><a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/the-ai-bubble-scoreboard-what-five.html">Last week we compiled what five surveys say about the bubble, from the Federal Reserve's contacts to fund managers and chief information officers</a>. This is the other side of that question: not what people say about the AI trade, but what the market has actually done to it. The surveys said the risk is real. The scoreboard says the damage is real too, and the earnings say the businesses are not broken yet. That is the whole argument, in three sentences.</p>
<h2>How to read a correction</h2>
<p>Corrections are common. The S and P 500 has fallen 10 percent from a peak many times without becoming a bear market, and the index is still up 0.9 percent for the year, with the Dow up 0.2 percent and only the Nasdaq down, at 2.7 percent. What makes this one different is the concentration: the losses sit in the same names that carried the rally, the AI infrastructure trade, and the trigger is a question about the trade itself, whether the spending earns its return. A correction becomes a bear market when the answer turns out to be no. Until the earnings say so, the honest label is the technical one: a correction, 45 days old, 6 trillion dollars deep, and still open.</p>
