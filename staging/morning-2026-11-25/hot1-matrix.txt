<p><a href="https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2611.matrix-multiplication-exponent-lower-bound">OpenAI posted a 111-page preprint on November 24, 2026 claiming the matrix multiplication exponent is exactly 9/4, or 2.25, over every field</a>. If the proof holds, a question open since 1969 is closed, and the long-hoped-for exponent of 2 is impossible. The claim is not yet checked. The matching upper bound from October has outside confirmation; the new lower bound, the part that would settle the question, has had no public review, and OpenAI's machine-checked formalization covers a narrower version of the result than the paper states.</p>

<h2>What the exponent measures</h2>
<p>Multiplying two n by n matrices produces n squared output numbers. Computing each output as its own dot product costs on the order of n cubed operations. Faster methods exist because outputs can share work. The exponent, written as the Greek letter omega, is the smallest number s such that multiplication can be done in roughly n to the power s operations as n grows. The output alone forces omega to be at least 2. The schoolbook method gives 3. Everything between those two numbers is the contest: how much sharing is mathematically possible.</p>
<p>Volker Strassen showed in 1969 that seven multiplications suffice for two-by-two matrices where the schoolbook method needs eight, which pushed the exponent to about 2.807. That first crack in the wall set off a 57-year race. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/openais-ai-wrote-722-math-manuscripts.html">Our October report on OpenAI's AI-written math manuscripts covered the batch this result comes from</a>, and the pattern since Strassen has been long plateaus broken by small steps in the third and fourth decimal places.</p>

<h2>Two halves, different levels of proof</h2>
<p>The exact value 2.25 needs two results. An algorithm must achieve 2.25, and a proof must show nothing can go below it. The first half arrived on October 2, 2026, when OpenAI posted a preprint proving the exponent is at most 9/4 over the complex numbers. That bound has been checked by outsiders. Computer scientist Rafael Pass posted an independent, constructive proof of the same bound on October 20, and Alimzhan Amanov, Maxim van den Berg, and Jeroen Zuiddam extended the argument to every field. The November 24 paper supplies the second half: a lower bound arguing that no arithmetic circuit, over any field, can multiply matrices with an exponent below 9/4.</p>
<p>The lower-bound proof works by contradiction. It argues that any method beating 2.25 could be turned into a way to compress certain networks that test whether the entries of several lists are all different, shrinking them below a size that a separate lower bound proves impossible. The paper also gives a concrete floor at every matrix size: for every n of 2 or more, any circuit multiplying two n by n matrices needs at least the ceiling of n to the power 9/4 divided by 2 multiplication or division steps, even if additions are free. We computed that floor at several sizes below. It is a statement about idealized circuit cost, not about time on real hardware.</p>

<h2>What would make this certain, and what is missing</h2>
<p>Three gaps separate the claim from a settled theorem. First, the paper is a preprint with no peer review, and no independent verification of the lower bound had been published as of November 25. Second, the repository holding it describes the manuscripts as raw research data from an unnamed internal OpenAI model rather than polished papers, and warns that unformalized results could have issues; OpenAI has already withdrawn three papers from the same collection a day after posting them. Third, the Lean formalization, the machine-checked version of the proof, covers only circuits that never divide. The paper's main theorem also allows division, and that extra step has not been checked by machine.</p>
<p>None of that makes the claim wrong. The October half of the result survived independent reconstruction within weeks, which is the strongest signal available that the program behind these manuscripts can produce correct mathematics. But the lower bound is the half that contradicts a decades-old expectation that the exponent might reach 2, and extraordinary closures require the checking process to finish. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/two-labs-claim-vp-is-not-equal-to-vnp.html">Our November 24 report on the two VP versus VNP proof claims made the same distinction</a>: an AI-produced proof is a claim until independent readers verify it, and the verification, not the announcement, is the event that changes what mathematicians may cite.</p>
<p>Even a fully verified 2.25 changes nothing practical tomorrow. The result counts arithmetic steps in an idealized model where additions can be free and constants do not matter. Fast methods with exponents well below 3 carry overheads so large that chips rarely use them. What changes is the map of the possible: the sharing limit for matrix multiplication would be known exactly, a quarter-power above the output size, and a 57-year search would end at a number nobody predicted in 1969.</p>
