<p>Three of the largest public school systems in the United States are publicly weighing whether to drop Google Search from student accounts, after Google refused to offer a version of Search without AI features. New York City, Los Angeles, and Chicago each told the New York Times in reporting published November 18, 2026 that they will look at other search engines if Google does not change its position. No district has switched yet. This tracker compiles where each district stands, what Google actually announced on November 3, 2026, and the three options that announcement leaves every school.</p>

<h2>What Google announced, and the catch inside it</h2>
<p>On November 3, 2026, Google announced that Gemini, Gemini Notebook, Gemini in Classroom, and Google Search will be entirely off for K-12 student accounts until school administrators explicitly turn them on, with the change expected in early 2027 and no exact date published. Google clarified the same day that schools can turn on one, two, or all of the services individually. The catch is inside Search itself. A school can enable Search without enabling the Gemini app, but Search still carries AI Overviews and AI Mode. There is no setting that removes the AI from Search. Chris Phillips, Google's vice president of education, told the Times that Search and AI are deeply integrated, and that students at schools blocking AI will not have access to search.</p>
<p>Google frames the change as more control, since nothing is on until a school decides. Districts frame it as a forced bundle, since the one configuration many of them want, Search without AI answers, is the one configuration not offered. Both descriptions are accurate about different parts of the same design. The on and off switches are genuinely per-service. The AI inside Search is genuinely not switchable. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/ai-regulation-worldwide-every-law.html">Our AI regulation comparison tracks how governments worldwide are drawing exactly this kind of line between a service and the AI embedded in it</a>, and the school fight is that argument arriving in procurement form.</p>

<h2>What to watch</h2>
<p>Three facts will decide whether this tracker records a switch or a standoff. First, whether Google publishes an exact early-2027 date and an administrator workflow, neither of which exists yet. Second, whether any of the three large districts moves from considering to announcing, which none had done as of November 25, 2026. Third, whether the Guided Learning pilot, which replaces quick answers with step-by-step help, changes district calculations; New York declined the pilot and is evaluating five other AI tools for high school use. The pressure is widening beyond districts: parents and teachers demonstrated outside Google offices in Boston, Chicago, and New York, at least 11 newly elected Chicago school board members signed a pledge supporting a three-year moratorium on generative AI in schools, and New York City Council leaders wrote to Google chief executive Sundar Pichai on November 12 demanding written answers by November 19.</p>
<p>The stakes are asymmetric. For Google, school Search is one surface among many. For districts, the search box is where students learn to find and judge information, and the dispute is really about who sets the default terms of that learning: the vendor that bundles AI into the tool, or the public system that answers for the classroom. Central R-3, at about 2,000 students, has already answered with a purchase order. The three largest systems in the country are still deciding, and their decision will set the default for everyone else. ByteHerald will update this tracker when a district announces a switch, a signed arrangement, or a Google policy change.</p>
