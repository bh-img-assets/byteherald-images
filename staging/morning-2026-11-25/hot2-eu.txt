<p><a href="https://www.theregister.com/cyber-crime/2026/11/24/european-commission-confirms-personal-data-stolen-in-ai-assisted-cyberattack/5311834">The European Commission confirmed on November 23, 2026 that hackers stole the names and email addresses of external users of its EU Academy learning platform in the AI-assisted cyberattack it first detected on October 22</a>. It is the first confirmed personal-data leak in the campaign. Affected users were not contacted directly. The Commission posted a general notice on the EU Academy website, a platform that has been offline since November 11 and is scheduled to stay offline until January 14, 2027.</p>

<h2>What changed on November 23</h2>
<p>Commission spokesperson Thomas Regnier told reporters on November 23 that the EU Academy platform was affected by the detected cyber campaign and that users' personal data, including names and email addresses, had leaked. The affected users are external: people outside the EU institutions who take courses on EU policies through the platform. Public access to EU Academy has been disabled while additional security measures are put in place. Regnier said the exact scale of the leak remains under investigation, and the Commission has not said how many users are affected or whether other personal information was taken.</p>
<p>The November 23 statement does not contradict what the Commission said four days earlier, but it narrows it. On November 19, Regnier confirmed the campaign had been carried out with a publicly available AI tool and said investigators had detected no evidence of a data breach affecting Commission staff as a whole. That statement was about staff. The November 23 confirmation is about outsiders. Staff systems were disrupted, with internal applications taken offline as a protective measure and most later restored, but the Commission has still announced no finding that staff data was stolen, and Regnier said he was not aware of any ransom demand.</p>

<h2>Confirmed, reported, and still open</h2>
<p>The public record mixes three kinds of statement, and keeping them apart is the difference between reporting this incident and inflating it. The Commission has confirmed the campaign, the AI tool, the October 22 detection date, the EU Academy leak, and that the European Data Protection Supervisor has been informed. Press reporting links the tool to ARTEX, an open-source AI penetration-testing framework; the Commission itself has not named the tool. Attribution is open: the Commission has named no attacker.</p>

<h2>Why the tool matters more than the target</h2>
<p>CERT-EU, the cybersecurity service for the EU institutions, warned on November 5, two weeks before the Commission confirmed it was a victim, that several threat actors around the world were using ARTEX and that similar activity against its own constituency highly likely involved the framework. CERT-EU describes ARTEX as an open-source, LLM-driven autonomous penetration testing platform: an operator states a security objective in natural language, and agent workers carry out reconnaissance and testing with real tooling. The framework ships without its own model and connects to an external LLM provider. CERT-EU's most consequential observation is that scope limits rely on configurable rules and model behavior, with no hard technical enforcement. A testing tool whose boundaries are advisory is, in other hands, an intrusion platform.</p>
<p>That is the durable lesson of this incident, and it outlasts the question of who attacked Brussels. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/shirudo-openai-and-ciscos-shield-for.html">Our November 24 report on Shirudo covered the defensive side of the same shift, with OpenAI and Cisco building AI tooling to shield unpatched systems</a>. The Commission case shows the offensive side arriving first at a major institution: AI-assisted intrusion is no longer a forecast. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/every-major-ai-safety-incident-2016.html">Our AI safety incident database tracks how each documented failure changed practice afterward</a>, and this entry will be judged the same way, by whether the notification gap and the scope-enforcement gap get closed.</p>
<p>The notification choice deserves scrutiny on its own terms. The Commission informed the data protection supervisor in stages, as its obligations require, but told affected users only through a website notice, on a site users had already been told not to return to until January. Regnier defended the general message as transparency without tipping off the attacker mid-investigation. The notice warns users to expect spearphishing, impersonation attempts, and spam built on the leaked names and addresses, and advises them not to hand personal information to anyone claiming to be involved in EU Academy activities. For the people whose data was taken, the practical risk is now in their inboxes, and the warning reached them only if they visited a closed website.</p>
