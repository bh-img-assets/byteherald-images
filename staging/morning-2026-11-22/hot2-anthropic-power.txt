<p>Anthropic's contracted data centers in the United States will run on about 87 percent gas and 11 percent coal power in 2028, according to a <a href="https://betterdatacenterproject.com/2026/11/16/how-ai-is-powered-a-breakdown-of-byop-and-grid-power/">case study published on November 16, 2026, by the Better Data Center Project</a>, a nonprofit started by two former US Department of Energy officials. The group estimates the company's power use at those sites could produce 17 to 38 million metric tons of carbon dioxide a year in 2028, with a mid-case figure of 32 million tons, which would exceed all of California's power generation in 2024. The figures are modeled estimates, not measurements, and Anthropic has not publicly confirmed the fuel mix, but they are the first complete attempt to trace the power behind every data center site the company has signed for.</p>

<p>The scale of the buildout is what makes the percentages matter. As of October 2026, Anthropic had contracted 15 gigawatts of compute, of which about 5.9 to 9.6 gigawatts is contracted to specific data center sites. The top of that range, 9.6 gigawatts, could power about 7 million homes, or cover about 18 percent of the peak power demand of California. This demand will be met with a mix of new on-site gas, new grid gas, and existing grid gas, coal, and nuclear, and in the short term no grid renewables are attributed to Anthropic's power use, because without commitments to new renewable capacity, new load on the grid will be met by dispatchable, marginal fossil resources.</p>
<div class="separator" style="clear: both; text-align: center;"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bh-img-assets/byteherald-images/main/img/hot2-anthropic-power-chart.png" alt="Chart: Anthropic's estimated 2028 power mix, about 87 percent gas, 11 percent coal, and 2 percent other sources" /></div>
<p>The emissions comparisons in the study are deliberately startling. The mid-case 32 million metric tons tops the 31.1 million metric tons all of California's power plants emitted in 2024, not including imports. It could also exceed the combined Scope 2 emissions of Meta, Microsoft, and Google from 2025, whose location-based emissions before renewable energy offsets were 5.9 million, 12 million, and 15 million metric tons respectively. The study notes that those companies' emissions are also expected to grow, so the comparison is not meant to suggest Anthropic will be the largest emitter among the tech companies. The range would represent a 16 to 36 percent increase over 2024 levels for the entire US data center sector, which the study puts at 105 million metric tons, and a 1.1 to 2.5 percent increase for the entire US power sector, at 1,485 million metric tons in 2025. The totals will keep growing, possibly doubling by 2030, as some analysts expect Anthropic to secure 30 gigawatts by 2030.</p>
<h2>What the estimates assume</h2>
<p>Every number above is an estimate built on stated assumptions, and the study publishes them. The group gathered details about Anthropic's data center commitments from Epoch AI's tracker, Anthropic's announcements and filings, partner announcements and filings, and reporting from The Information and Semafor, among others. For projects with on-site gas or new grid gas, it used emissions factors from permits where available, or permit values from comparable facilities. For grid-connected facilities without associated new generation capacity, it assumed the sites will be powered by marginal, dispatchable grid capacity, and it used the EPA's AVERT tool to model which power plants would meet the new incremental load. Power usage effectiveness is assumed at 1.1 to 1.2, and power utilization at 60 to 80 percent. The mid-case assumes a power usage effectiveness of 1.15, utilization of 70 percent, and all 9.6 gigawatts of identified compute capacity online in 2028. Change any of those, and the 32 million ton figure moves, which is why <a href="https://betterdatacenterproject.com/2026/11/16/how-ai-is-powered-a-breakdown-of-byop-and-grid-power/">the study gives a range of 17 to 38 million</a> rather than a single number. Using average rather than marginal grid intensity would give a lower carbon intensity of 839 pounds of CO2 per megawatt-hour, against the 1,033 to 1,076 pounds the study reports, and the authors argue marginal emissions are more representative of the near-term impact of new load.</p>
<p>Two disclosures about the source belong in the open. The Better Data Center Project campaigns for data centers to stop using fossil fuels, so it is an advocacy group with a position, not a neutral auditor. And Anthropic has not confirmed the mix. The 87 and 11 percent figures are the group's projection from contracted and planned capacity, not a company disclosure, and they assume the planned gas capacity comes online on schedule and runs at projected capacity factors. What the study does rest on is verifiable: the 15 gigawatts of contracted compute, the specific sites, the generator purchase, and the grid data are all sourced to filings, trackers, and reporting that readers can check.</p>
<h2>The gas behind the numbers</h2>
<p>The single largest purchase behind the gas share is one readers of ByteHerald already know. Anthropic committed about 3.4 billion dollars in late September for 1.7 gigawatts of heavy-duty natural-gas generators from San Antonio-based OnePWR Solutions, a deal the Financial Times reported on November 8, 2026, and which we covered as <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/anthropics-34b-generators-it-bought.html">the largest known single power-equipment purchase by an AI lab</a>. The case study puts that purchase in context: the group identified 177 gigawatts of planned bring-your-own-power gas capacity across the United States, enough to power 65 percent of all US homes, and estimates that for current proposals less than 20 percent of energy generation will come from renewable sources by 2032. Another 200 gigawatts of new fossil power plants is being built on the grid to meet growing demand, due in large part to data centers. The pattern is not unique to Anthropic. Labs are buying their own power because the grid cannot connect them fast enough, as <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/the-grid-cant-keep-up-so-ai-labs-buy.html">our ledger of private power purchases showed</a>, and the power they buy is overwhelmingly gas.</p>
<p>Two sites show the local cost. Anthropic has used compute capacity at SpaceX's Colossus 2 facility since May 2026, which still receives most of its power from temporary, unpermitted turbines, the majority of which are not equipped with selective catalytic reduction, and Anthropic will use as much as 600 megawatts of power from Colossus 2 by the end of 2026. The company has also contracted 460 megawatts, and is in talks for up to 920 megawatts, from Nscale's Monarch Compute Campus in Mason, West Virginia, a facility powered by inefficient reciprocating engines with significant formaldehyde and hazardous air pollutant emissions compared with conventional gas power plants.</p>
<h2>What Anthropic says</h2>
<p>Anthropic outlined its approach to responsible data center development in October 2026, stating that it follows the air permitting process everywhere it operates and requires the same of the plants powering its data centers, and that those plants must also use emissions control technology, including selective catalytic reduction. The company has also stated that it is working to broaden its energy mix with geothermal, wind, solar, and emerging technologies. The case study's first finding is the gap between that statement and the disclosed commitments: as of November 2026, the only publicly disclosed commitments, contracts, or investments in renewable power are in Norway and Canada, while the company is directly procuring fossil power equipment in the United States. The study does not claim Anthropic has broken any law or any promise. It claims the numbers do not match the framing, and it publishes the dataset behind the claim so the company, or anyone else, can challenge it.</p>
