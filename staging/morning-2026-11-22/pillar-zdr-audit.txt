<p>Zero Data Retention is a promise that no copy of your prompt survives. The provider processes the request in memory, returns the response, and keeps nothing at rest: nothing in application storage, nothing in abuse-monitoring logs, nothing in a review queue. That is the promise. In November 2026, a <a href="https://www.dragos.com/blog/ai-supply-chain-security-canary-tokens">five-month canary test showed how often the promise breaks</a>. Researchers planted about two million prompts carrying unique fake secrets and watched them get copied inside breached providers, kept by marketplace sellers, and fetched by companies that were never supposed to see them, including requests marked Zero Data Retention. This audit compiles what ten providers actually do with your prompts: how long they keep them by default, whether they train on them, how you get zero retention if it is available at all, and what sits outside the promise even when you have it.</p>
<div class="separator" style="clear: both; text-align: center;"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bh-img-assets/byteherald-images/main/img/pillar-zdr-audit-hero.png" alt="Editorial graphic: the headline The Zero Data Retention Audit, Who Keeps Your Prompts, November 2026" /></div>
<h2>Two promises, not one</h2>
<p>The first thing the table below separates is two promises that vendors and buyers alike keep confusing. No training means the provider does not use your prompts or outputs to improve its models. No retention means the provider does not store them. A provider can keep both promises, one of them, or neither, and the common case is one without the other. OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft, and Google all say business API data is not used for training by default, yet each may still store it for a time. DeepSeek's first-party API is the reverse of the pattern: it lists training as a use of your data, with a policy-level opt-out and no API carve-out, and publishes no retention window at all. The two promises also live in different documents, and the retention promise is usually the one that is missing from the marketing page.</p>
<p>The second distinction is scope. Zero Data Retention is a per-endpoint commitment, not a per-vendor one. Stateful features sit outside it everywhere: files, batches, assistants and threads, vector stores, code execution containers, and connectors to third-party tools keep data by design, because the feature cannot work without storage. OpenAI's own documentation lists the endpoints that are not eligible, and Anthropic's lists the features its arrangement does not cover. A customer who turns on Zero Data Retention and then uploads a file to a vector store has not made a mistake about the setting. They have made a mistake about what the setting covers.</p>
<p>The third distinction is the one the canary test proved. A privacy flag binds only the first company to receive the request. Routers forward requests to providers, providers forward them again, and the setting does not always travel with the prompt. The table tells you what each provider promises for its own storage. It cannot tell you what happens after the request leaves that provider, which is why the audit ends with five checks instead of a ranking.</p>

<p>Read the table as a snapshot dated November 22, 2026, not as a permanent verdict. Providers change these terms, and Anthropic and Google both update their retention pages often. The pattern that does not change is the shape of the deal. At the big first-party APIs, zero retention is granted rather than switched on: <a href="https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/your-data">OpenAI</a> and <a href="https://www.anthropic.com/legal/commercial-terms">Anthropic</a> approve it through their sales teams, Azure approves modified abuse monitoring through a Limited Access process, Google asks for an exception request, Mistral asks for a request and approval. Bedrock is the exception among the large platforms, because it starts from the opposite default and stores nothing unless a mode says otherwise. At the inference providers, the default is whatever the provider behind the endpoint does, which is why Together making zero retention the default for new accounts matters, and why OpenRouter stripping 125 endpoints of eligibility in one day matters more than the number itself.</p>

<h2>The case study: what the canary test proved</h2>
<p>The table is the policy. The Dragos canary test is what happens when policy meets practice, and it is the reason this audit exists. The researchers sent about two million prompts carrying unique secrets to widely used AI services between July and November 2026, and the secrets surfaced in three ways. Attackers were inside at least three hosting providers, stealing data without the providers knowing, from mid-July onward. Marketplace sellers kept customer prompts by turning on standard request logging, with no exploit needed. And providers ignored privacy settings, fetching the secret links in prompts days or months later, including about two-thirds of the requests marked no-collection or Zero Data Retention sent to one Chinese lab's models. <a href="https://openrouter.ai/blog/announcements/data-processing-incidents-on-inference-providers/">OpenRouter's response on November 22, 2026, three providers off-boarded and 125 endpoints stripped of Zero Data Retention eligibility</a>, is the first enforcement action the research has produced. The full account, with the three provider notices and the one question nobody has answered, is in the ledger that catalogued ARTEX as one of seven confirmed AI-assisted attacks, <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/the-ai-assisted-attack-ledger-7-cases.html">the AI-assisted attack ledger</a>, whose ARTEX row carries the same November 2026 warning.</p>
<div class="separator" style="clear: both; text-align: center;"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bh-img-assets/byteherald-images/main/img/pillar-zdr-audit-chart2.png" alt="Chart: timeline of the prompt theft, from the first breach in mid-July 2026 through the Together leak, the Dragos report, and OpenRouter's action on November 22, 2026" /></div>
<p>The test also proved a limit that belongs in any honest reading. A canary shows that a prompt leaked, but not where in the chain, because a router typically reports a single upstream provider, and that provider is often just another router. Dragos describes the three breaches as activity indicating exposure and does not name the providers, so nobody outside Dragos and those companies can check the findings yet. The test is evidence of a broken promise, not a map of every break. That is also why the researchers are now working with about a dozen providers, and why OpenRouter is adapting their testing approach to run the checks on an ongoing basis.</p>
<h2>Five checks before you trust a retention claim</h2>
<p>First, read the retention page, not the marketing page. The promise lives in the data controls guide, the commercial terms, or the data governance page, and the marketing page rarely mentions the exceptions. Second, check whether the promise is per endpoint. A vendor that offers zero retention on its stateless chat endpoint and keeps everything on its assistants endpoint has not lied. It has sold you two products with two policies, and the one you are using is the one that counts. Third, ask where the request goes after the first hop. If your provider is a router or a reseller, the retention promise you need is the one held by the company that actually runs the model, and the Dragos traces found routes with undisclosed hops that the advertised path never mentioned. That is the same gap our comparison of agent identity approaches flagged: knowing who the agent is matters less than knowing who stands behind it, and the credential an agent carries is only as good as the chain that issued it, as <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/ai-agents-need-id-agent-identity-stack.html">our agent identity audit showed</a>. Fourth, test the claim yourself with a canary. Put a fake credential in the context your agents and pipelines already send, and treat any use of it as an incident. A free canary token is enough to start, and the test costs nothing except the discipline to watch for the result. Fifth, separate training from retention in every vendor conversation. A provider that does not train on your data may still store it for thirty days, and a provider that stores nothing may still train on data you send through a different product. When an agent gets hacked and the credentials inside its prompts get used, the question of who pays turns on exactly these distinctions, and the banks have already answered it in their own favor, as <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/banks-say-you-pay-when-your-ai-agent.html">our eight-bank liability audit showed</a>.</p>
<p>The audit's finding is not that zero retention is impossible. It is that zero retention is a negotiated, per-endpoint, exception-ridden arrangement, and that the flag on your request is the weakest part of the chain. The providers that do it well say so in their documentation, with the exceptions listed beside the promise. The providers that do it badly say nothing at all, and the canary test is what finds them.</p>
