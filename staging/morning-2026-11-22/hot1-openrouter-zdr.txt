<p><a href="https://openrouter.ai/blog/announcements/data-processing-incidents-on-inference-providers/">OpenRouter said on November 22, 2026, that it has removed three inference providers from its platform and stripped 125 model endpoints of their Zero Data Retention status</a>, after <a href="https://www.dragos.com/blog/ai-supply-chain-security-canary-tokens">security researchers showed</a> that prompts sent through AI providers were being copied for months. The researchers, from the industrial cybersecurity company Dragos, planted about two million prompts carrying unique fake secrets between July and November 2026 and watched where those secrets surfaced. What they found was not one leak but three different failures: attackers inside at least three hosting providers, marketplace sellers keeping customer prompts on purpose, and privacy settings ignored by the companies that received the requests. OpenRouter said its own systems were not breached, and the problems were found at the inference providers running models behind its router.</p>

<h2>How the theft was caught</h2>
<p>The method was a canary test. A canary token is a secret that exists in only one place, so if anyone ever uses it, the place it was sent to has been exposed. Dragos chief research officer HD Moore and his team seeded prompts with unique web links, credentials, passwords, private git repository references, and real AWS keys tied to empty accounts with full logging, then watched for anyone using them. The study ran for five months, from July through November 2026, and the team later built a dedicated service that generates a unique seed for every prompt and runs the servers those credentials point to, so every use could be traced back to the exact prompt, model, router, and privacy setting that carried it. The research was published on November 19, 2026, under the title <a href="https://www.dragos.com/blog/ai-supply-chain-security-canary-tokens">"Three breaches, endless leaks: Your AI supply chain needs canaries."</a></p>
<p>To find out who really handled each request, the team also built what it describes as a traceroute for AI inference: an image URL in the request that gives away the IP address, client, and timestamp of every system that fetches it, plus slightly broken requests whose nested error envelopes reveal each hop. They traced about two thousand routes and found several hundred that included a system they could not identify, and a handful that sent their image to a company that was not on the advertised path at all. One marketplace seller quietly adds about a thousand hidden tokens to every prompt, and another fetches images through a shared consumer subscription account.</p>
<h2>What Dragos found</h2>
<table><thead><tr><th>Finding</th><th>What happened</th><th>Scale</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Provider breaches</td><td>Attackers stole data from inside at least three hosting providers; none knew until Dragos told them</td><td>Earliest incident mid-July 2026; a couple hundred AWS keys in the largest breach</td></tr><tr><td>Marketplace resellers</td><td>Sellers listed popular models at the lowest price, turned on request logging, and kept what flowed through</td><td>Secret links opened about a day and a half later; passwords tried over SSH by hand</td></tr><tr><td>Privacy settings ignored</td><td>A Chinese AI lab fetched secret URLs from over half of prompts sent to its models, including about two-thirds marked no-collection or Zero Data Retention</td><td>Scraper typically arrived two to three days after the request</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>The largest breach is the one with the clearest evidence. A couple hundred planted AWS keys were each sent in exactly one request to one provider, all within a few short windows in late October, and every request sent during those windows was stolen, including requests the provider had rejected before a model ever ran. That detail matters: it tells Dragos the attacker was copying requests at the provider's front door, not somewhere deeper in the inference stack. Once the attacker had the credentials, the pattern was predictable. Keys were tested to see if they were live, in some cases within a few hours of the request. Then the attacker listed S3 buckets and searched Secrets Manager and Parameter Store, and finally tried to enable hosted models in AWS Bedrock, create Bedrock API keys, and add its own IAM users to keep access. In total, the bait keys were used for tens of thousands of AWS API calls. One key seeded on October 21 was first used the next day and was still in use three weeks later. On November 1, the same attacker added ARTEX, an LLM-driven penetration testing framework, which in this case made only a handful of calls to confirm keys already held were still live. The two user agents it sent, artex-agent and artex-judge-r121, do not appear in any public indicator list Dragos could find, and the firm says they are worth adding to detections. ARTEX is the same framework our ledger of confirmed AI-assisted attacks catalogued as a November 2026 case, in <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/the-ai-assisted-attack-ledger-7-cases.html">the AI-assisted attack ledger</a>, and the FBI warned about it separately in <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/fbi-artex-ai-tool-can-breach-network-in.html">its November 13 flash</a>.</p>
<div class="separator" style="clear: both; text-align: center;"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bh-img-assets/byteherald-images/main/img/hot1-openrouter-zdr-chart.png" alt="Chart: timeline of the prompt theft, from the first breach in mid-July 2026 to OpenRouter's action on November 22, 2026" /></div>
<p>The second finding needed no break-in at all. Some routers are open marketplaces where the seller with the lowest price gets the request, and a seller who wants customer data only has to list a popular model at the lowest price, turn on request logging, and wait. Dragos caught this by sending prompts for the same model through different sellers: when secrets sent through one seller were used and the others were not, the leak pointed to the seller. In one case, about a day and a half after two prompts went through a single seller's offer, someone on a home broadband connection opened both secret URLs in a desktop browser and started guessing usernames at the login page. In another, an attacker working from a Mac on a home broadband line in France took the passwords from two prompts and tried to log in as root over SSH, typing each attempt by hand. The same Mac later tested several of the team's AWS keys in a single session. These were individuals, Dragos writes, working from what looked like home internet connections and personal computers.</p>
<p>The third finding is different again, because nobody used a credential. The secret URLs in the prompts were scraped, repeatedly, and the git repositories they referenced were targeted for cloning, all by companies that were never supposed to see the prompt in the first place, and often in direct conflict with the privacy settings on the request. The clearest example involves a Chinese AI lab whose systems fetched the secret URLs from more than half of the prompts sent to its models between July and October, including roughly two-thirds of the requests marked as no data collection or Zero Data Retention, with the scraper typically showing up two or three days after the request. The same scraper fired for prompts sent through US hosting providers that resell the lab's models, so a customer who picked a US provider specifically to keep data in the US did not get what they paid for. The strangest incident involved a company chat: 24 days after a prompt was sent to one Chinese provider, the secret link from that prompt was posted into a Feishu chat channel, and within two minutes it was opened from three desktops and three phones in Beijing and Shanghai. Dragos had disclosed only the first eight characters of that link to the provider, so whoever shared it was reading from a stored copy of the full prompt, more than three weeks after the request was sent to a US-based provider with the Zero Data Retention flag. Two large US providers had their own issues: one kept crawling a secret URL more than two months after a single inference call, and another runs a scraper that returns for the URLs days after each request, regardless of which router was used.</p>
<h2>What the three providers said</h2>
<p>Three providers have posted public notices of their own, and OpenRouter links all three from its announcement. Phala published a <a href="https://phala.com/posts/phala-november-preliminary-incident-report">preliminary incident report</a> on November 10, 2026. An attacker took control of an inference server and could view request data while it was being processed, and the incident was contained on November 8. Phala says users whose DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731 requests were routed to it by OpenRouter between October 21 at 00:00 UTC and November 8 at 05:35 UTC should treat the request contents as exposed and rotate any API keys, tokens, passwords, or other secrets in those requests. The attacker reached internal management interfaces on October 21, inserted malware, and used a similar access pattern to take control of the inference virtual machine. Phala rotated its key management root key on November 6 and says no successful unauthorized access has been observed since containment. The canary evidence confirms misuse only for requests sent on October 21 and 22, but Phala keeps the wider window for user guidance because the tests sampled requests rather than monitoring every one. A full root cause analysis is still to come.</p>
<p>Together AI published a <a href="https://www.together.ai/blog/security-event-prompt-leakage-for-requests-in-june-and-july-of-2026">security notice</a> on November 21, 2026, signed by chief executive Vipul Ved Prakash. An unauthorized party downloaded stored prompt samples from its serverless inference service after an AI coding agent committed a credential for a private Hugging Face repository into the company's public GitHub repository, xorl. The samples came from requests to the MiniMax M3, GLM-5.2, and Kimi K2.6 models, primarily on June 24, July 4, July 8, and July 14, 2026. The party searched the samples for credentials to other services and tested whether they were valid, and five of the credentials tested were canary credentials from the Dragos experiment. Together says it has worked with an external forensic firm and law enforcement, has found no evidence of additional prompt leakage beyond this incident, and has made Zero Data Retention the default setting for new accounts.</p>
<p>Novita published a <a href="https://novita.ai/blog/minimax-requests-privacy-notice/">privacy notice</a> on November 22, 2026. A routing configuration issue caused some MiniMax requests to be forwarded to MiniMax's official API rather than processed on Novita-hosted infrastructure, and the affected models have been suspended. Anyone who used MiniMax models through Novita between May 28 and November 11, 2026, should assume the request contents may have been accessed or retained by a third party, including requests routed to Novita through OpenRouter. Novita says it does not itself retain customer prompts or generated outputs or use them for model training, and it is following up with MiniMax on the handling and deletion of the affected data.</p>
<p>One thing nobody has confirmed is which three providers OpenRouter removed. Dragos has not named the three breached providers, OpenRouter has not named the three it removed, and neither has said publicly whether they are the same three. The three that posted notices are the ones readers can check, and the rest of the removals are known only to OpenRouter and the providers involved.</p>
<h2>What Zero Data Retention actually promises</h2>
<p>Zero Data Retention is supposed to mean a provider will not store your data for any period of time. That is OpenRouter's own definition, and it is the strictest privacy setting a customer can set, at the account level, for an API key, or in each request. The problem Dragos documents is that the flag binds only the first company to receive the request. Routers forward requests to providers, and those providers may forward them again, and the privacy setting does not always travel with the prompt. A customer who picks a US provider to keep data in the US can still have the prompt flow back to a lab overseas. A privacy flag does not help when the attacker is already inside the provider, and it does not help when the provider's own systems fetch the links in the prompt days later. The flag is a contract term, not a technical boundary, and the canary test shows the gap between the two.</p>
<h2>What to do now</h2>
<p>Start with the three published windows. If you sent DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731 requests through OpenRouter between October 21 and November 8, 2026, rotate every secret in those requests. If you used MiniMax models through Novita between May 28 and November 11, 2026, do the same. If you used Together's serverless service in June and July 2026, check whether the company contacts you, and rotate anything sensitive in those prompts. Then look at the route itself. Pick the upstream providers yourself instead of accepting a router's default pool, and check whether your privacy settings actually change where requests go, because a setting that does not change the route changes nothing. Dragos recommends seeding your own canaries, a fake credential in the context your agents and pipelines already send, and treating any use of it as an incident, and enabling secret filtering where a provider offers it, while noting that plenty of sensitive data in prompts is not shaped like an API key. The strongest option remains hosting the model yourself, which is the only setup where nobody else gets to read the prompts, but the hardware is expensive, and for most teams that only makes sense for the most sensitive work. The practical rule from the research is short: where your prompts go is a decision, and if you do not make it, the lowest price will make it for you.</p>
