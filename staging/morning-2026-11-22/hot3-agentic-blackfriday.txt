<p>This is the first holiday season since easy-to-use AI agents were released to the general public, and the first agentic Black Friday is off to a rocky start. A <a href="https://www.businessinsider.com/ai-agents-holiday-shopping-not-yet-trusted-consumers-retailers">Bain and Company analysis this week of Similarweb data</a> found that AI assistants represent less than 1 percent of retailers' web traffic, and DoorDash and Uber have said very few of their customers are using agents. Of about 320 million Uber transactions in the final week of October, only about 500 came from AI agents. Shoppers are using AI to find things, and they are not yet letting it buy things, and that gap is the whole story of the season so far.</p>

<p>The contradiction in the numbers is the point. Three-quarters of shoppers plan to use an AI assistant this season, and nearly half would buy a brand they do not know on its recommendation, but only 15 percent would let an agent pay even after reviewing the transaction, and 10 percent without a review. Retailers are further ahead than their customers: 89 percent of retailers are preparing for AI agents that buy on the customer's behalf, according to the same <a href="https://news.henkanpartners.ai/en/talkdesk-ai-agents-holiday-payment">Talkdesk survey, published on November 4, 2026</a>. The traffic is growing fast from a tiny base. <a href="https://www.cxnetwork.com/cx-experience/articles/agentic-commerce-retail-golden-quarter-black-friday/amp">Adobe projects AI-referred traffic to retail sites will rise 130 percent year on year this golden quarter</a>, with the biggest rises on Thanksgiving at 159 percent, Black Friday at 95 percent, and Cyber Monday at 88 percent. And Bain found that AI search is almost twice as effective as traditional web search at ultimately converting to a sale, which is why retailers are investing even while the transaction numbers stay small. <a href="https://www.cxnetwork.com/cx-experience/articles/agentic-commerce-retail-golden-quarter-black-friday/amp">McKinsey's 2026 agentic commerce survey found more than 50 percent of consumers globally use AI for shopping</a>, with adoption highest in China at 80 percent and lowest in Japan at 37 percent.</p>

<h2>Why shoppers hold back</h2>
<p>The hesitation is not about the technology failing to find things. It is about trust, and about gifts. Dean Mandelos, a 29-year-old therapist in New York City, has used Meta's Muse, launched in September, to list and sell items on eBay and to gather and organize contractor bids for a door-replacement job. When he asked it to find a holiday gift for his sister, who loves to cook, it recommended a set of knives rather than the cookbook he expected. He said the agent was giving some good ideas, but that it consistently misses the social aspect. He also ran into expired or sold-out listings and outright blocks from major e-commerce sites like Amazon and eBay, and he said he would like to let the agent do its own thing but needs a reason to trust it more.</p>
<p>Amrita Bhasin, co-founder and chief executive of the sustainable supply chain startup Sotira, uses OpenAI's ChatGPT and Google's Gemini to save time finding apparel and beauty products, especially unfamiliar brands, but prefers to complete purchases herself. She said figuring out what to buy is where she faces severe decision fatigue, and the process of actually buying is not super complicated. She worries an agent could buy the wrong thing or make another mistake for which she would be accountable, and she notes that sites like Amazon already have fast, easy checkout that leaves her in control. Xavier Starkloff, co-founder and chief executive of the AI-powered shopping platform Joko, said improving product discovery is currently more important for agents than completing transactions, and that agentic shopping is a game changer at a time like Black Friday because it is all about finding the right deals.</p>
<p>Holiday shopping makes the trust problem harder, because it is not pantry restocking. It is a weeks-long festive event, and different gifts require thoughtful personalization, and it is an open question how a recipient would feel on learning a gift was outsourced to an AI agent. Kantar retail analyst Gina Logan compared the hesitation to the early shopping cart: stores had to put people out with the carts, pushing them around, in order to get other people to start using them. Her verdict on the season is measured: it is the first Black Friday that AI can really play a meaningful role, but it is not fully autonomous yet. Zappi founder Steve Phillips made the same point with a different analogy, comparing the moment to early dot-com hotel booking, when people searched online and then phoned to reserve, and adding that there is a massive trust factor at play.</p>
<h2>The retailer split</h2>
<p>Retailers have split into two camps, and the split decides what agents can actually do. Walmart is rolling out a suite of tools to make agentic shopping easier, while Amazon is blocking external bots in favor of having shoppers use its own Alexa, and blocking third-party AI agents from fully accessing its site. Target chief executive Michael Fiddelke and Home Depot's head of online experiences each told Business Insider their companies are focusing their AI strategies on delivering the best experience for customers, without taking a side on third-party agents. Logan warned that a bad agentic experience could undermine years or decades of investment in customer trust, which is why the cautious camp is cautious.</p>
<p>The split is also a money question. When an agent does the buying, somebody has to be paid for the sale, and the retailer, the agent, and the platform behind it all have a claim, which is why <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/when-your-ai-agent-buys-something-who.html">our audit of the agentic commerce money trail</a> found five layers of fees and one missing piece, the agent's own cut. The stakes are large: US retail media is projected at 74.4 billion dollars, with Amazon and Walmart holding about 88 percent of it between them, as <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/ai-shopping-agents-are-after-74-billion.html">our retail-media analysis showed</a>. Whoever controls the agent controls the recommendation, and the recommendation is increasingly where the sale is decided.</p>
<h2>What this season actually tests</h2>
<p>What Black Friday 2026 tests is not whether agents can shop, but whether shoppers will hand over the last step. The evidence so far says they will hand over the search and keep the payment. Agents are already good at the part shoppers find hardest, which is choosing among too many options, and bad at the part shoppers find easiest, which is clicking buy on a site they already trust. Abhishek Bora, head of growth at the streetwear retailer All Star Elite, said agentic traffic is about 1 percent of his website traffic, mostly from Meta's Muse, and that once or twice a week the store gets a Muse purchase. It is small, he said, but the store has been optimizing for it, adding machine-readable data layers to help bots navigate products and promotions. That is the realistic shape of this season: a small number of real purchases, a much larger number of assisted discoveries, and a trust gap that one holiday will not close.</p>
