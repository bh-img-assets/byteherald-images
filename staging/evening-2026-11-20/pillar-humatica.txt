<p>Two in three large enterprises now run a process, a product, or a business model that would break if AI were switched off, according to research published November 19, 2026, by Humatica and CoreWeave. Only about a third of those enterprises have a formal operating model for AI, and only one in five has appointed a formal AI leader. The gap between dependence and ownership is the report's central finding, and it is the number a journalist can cite.</p>

<h2>The gap, in one table</h2>
<p>The study, "AI-Native by Design: The Operating Model Behind Lasting AI Advantage", draws on a panel of 252 enterprises across 14 industries, more than 30 interviews with senior executives and investors, and more than 1,900 annual reports, filings, and investor documents examined between July and October 2026. Every enterprise in the panel had an active AI program, and 95 percent had moved beyond individual experimentation. The study excluded startups, AI labs, AI and cloud vendors, and businesses with less than $50 million in revenue, so the findings describe an AI-active panel, not adoption across the whole economy.</p>

<p>Read the table as a ladder. Almost every company in the panel has moved past experimenting. Two in three now depend on AI for something that would break. Nearly half have AI in what they sell. But the governance rungs are the short ones: about a third have a formal operating model, and a fifth have a named leader. The report defines an operating model as the arrangements for deciding what gets built, who owns it, and how it is funded and governed. By that definition, most of the panel is running AI it does not formally own.</p>
<h2>How we got a sixth survey</h2>
<p>This is the sixth enterprise AI survey we have compiled in two days of coverage, and it answers a different question from the other five. On November 17, 2026, we compiled five surveys on the AI bubble question in <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/the-ai-bubble-scoreboard-what-five.html">The AI Bubble Scoreboard: What Five Surveys Actually Say</a>, from BofA's fund-manager survey to the Federal Reserve's market-contacts survey to Gartner's CIO survey. Those surveys asked whether AI is overvalued and whether the spending will pay off. Humatica's survey asks who owns the AI inside the company, and its answer is that mostly nobody does. The two questions are complements, not duplicates: a bubble is a question about price, and ownership is a question about control.</p>
<h2>Orphan services</h2>
<p>The ownership problem starts before a system becomes essential. The report found that 24 percent of production AI use cases began without a sponsor or budget, creating a risk of systems with no clear owner. CoreWeave's account adds that 42 percent of production AI use cases originated in the business rather than in IT or a central AI team, and nearly one in four started through an individual employee's or team's own initiative.</p>
<div class="separator" style="clear: both; text-align: center;"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bh-img-assets/byteherald-images/main/img/pillar-humatica-chart2.png" alt="Chart: production AI starts outside IT, 42 percent started in the business, 24 percent with no sponsor or budget, 24 percent by employee or team initiative" /></div>
<p>Humatica calls the result orphan services: systems that teams rely on but nobody clearly owns, noticed only when they break. Its proposed response is not to ban decentralized building. It recommends giving employees a shared way to develop AI, with a stricter gate before others depend on the result: build security, access, monitoring, evaluation, and cost controls into a shared platform so new applications inherit them, assign an owner to every AI-dependent process and product, and give production systems a feedback cycle with a retirement date when a system stops earning its place. One executive in the research put building at only about 30 percent of the work. The other 70 percent is owning it.</p>
<h2>The constraint is the org chart, not the servers</h2>
<p>When leaders were asked what constrains their AI progress, 68 percent cited organization and governance, compared with 28 percent who cited technology and infrastructure. Leaders named their own organization as a constraint 2.4 times as often as the technology. That ratio is the study's most useful number for anyone selling technology into the enterprise: the bottleneck is not the model or the chips, it is the decision about who owns what.</p>
<p>The report identifies four approaches to the operating model, ranging from a central AI team to builders embedded across business functions, and each trades control against speed. A central team is easier to govern and slower to build with. Embedded builders move fast and create orphans. The report's answer is a hybrid: let teams build, but provide shared safeguards and name an owner for each AI-dependent process or product. That is a governance design, not a product recommendation, and it is the part of the study that does not depend on who paid for it.</p>
<h2>Why control, and who benefits from the answer</h2>
<p>Among leaders asked why they would move a critical workload onto infrastructure they control, 73 percent cited differentiation and proprietary intellectual property, while cost and predictability came last at 36 percent. The authors recommend buying AI services for everyday work while keeping differentiating or sensitive workloads on infrastructure the enterprise controls, and routing work across models according to task, cost, and sensitivity.</p>
<p>That recommendation deserves a plain disclosure. The study was sponsored by CoreWeave, a company that sells AI cloud infrastructure, and CoreWeave markets its own cloud platform alongside the findings. The 73 percent finding points directly at buying controlled infrastructure, which is what CoreWeave sells. That does not make the finding false: leaders really did cite differentiation over cost, and the panel and method are documented. But it does mean the finding that most serves the sponsor is also the finding to read with the most care. A sponsored study can be right and self-serving at the same time, and this one is both. The dependence numbers, which do not serve any vendor, are the ones to trust most.</p>
<h2>The five practices, and the test for your company</h2>
<p>The report closes with five practices for turning AI ambition into lasting value: make AI an operating-model decision; empower decentralized building and scale proven results; unlock the enterprise's knowledge as a proprietary advantage; keep control of the AI the business depends on; and keep every AI production system owned, measured, and improving. They are sensible and they are generic, which is why the useful part is the test they imply. Ask three questions about your own company. First, who owns each process that would break without AI, by name? Second, what would break if that process stopped, and what would it cost? Third, when does each AI system get retired if it stops earning its place? If any of those questions has no answer, the company is in the 66 percent that depends and the 80 percent that has no leader.</p>
<p>The same governance pressure shows up in the other surveys. Cloudera's October 2026 survey found 89 percent of enterprises limited or redesigned AI work over token costs, and 98 percent pulled back a deployment, the same constraint Humatica measures from the other side. We mapped that redesign wave in <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/89-of-enterprises-redesigned-ai-scale.html">89% of Enterprises Redesigned AI. The Scale Trap, Mapped</a>. The two studies agree on the mechanism: the technology arrived faster than the ownership, and the redesigns and the orphans are the bill coming due.</p>
<h2>What this study does not show</h2>
<p>The study does not name companies, and its interviews were non-attributable, so no single finding can be checked against a named enterprise. It does not prove that controlled infrastructure pays off: the 73 percent is leaders' stated motivation, not a comparison showing one infrastructure choice delivers better returns. It contains no financial results for CoreWeave. And because the panel is AI-active enterprises with more than $50 million in revenue, it says nothing about smaller companies or about companies that have not adopted AI at all. Those limits do not weaken the central finding. They define it: among large companies that already use AI, dependence has outrun ownership, and the gap is now the main AI risk.</p>
