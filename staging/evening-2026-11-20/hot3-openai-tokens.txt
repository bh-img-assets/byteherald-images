<p>The median OpenAI researcher used about $2,400 worth of AI tokens a day in October 2026, and the busiest tenth used about $23,000 a day, OpenAI reported on November 18, 2026. Both figures are list-price equivalents, not cash OpenAI spent, and both rose sharply in a single month: the median up 62 percent from September, the top tenth up 105 percent.</p>

<h2>The numbers, in one table</h2>
<p>OpenAI's report, "Research acceleration: The jagged frontier", is the company's second internal measurement of how its own coding agents are changing its research. It is company-reported evidence, not an independent audit, and the report says so in its own caveats. The numbers are the story anyway, because no other frontier lab publishes anything like them.</p>

<h2>What the money is and is not</h2>
<p>The dollar figures are what the usage would cost an outside customer paying OpenAI's public API rates. They are not cash OpenAI spent, and they are not what any outside customer pays, because internal use is not billed. Run the arithmetic anyway, because the scale is the point: at list prices, the median researcher's October usage annualizes to about $876,000 a year, and the top tenth's to about $8.4 million a year, by ByteHerald's arithmetic from the daily figures. In September, the API-equivalent value of agent use across OpenAI's research organization was about twice its non-equity compensation for researchers. That comparison excludes equity, which OpenAI says generally accounts for 70 to 80 percent of a researcher's target pay, so the 2-to-1 ratio is against cash pay only, not total pay.</p>
<p>There is a second limit on the money story. Internal deployment, which combines internal Codex, ChatGPT, and API usage, accounts for only 2 percent of OpenAI's total research compute. The token bill is not the compute bill. Training runs still dominate the spending; the agents are a small slice of compute that happens to be growing fast.</p>
<h2>Why OpenAI published this</h2>
<p>OpenAI frames the report as evidence for a policy call: AI labs should be required to track and publicly report indicators of recursive self-improvement, the process by which AI accelerates the development of future AI systems. The company has made that call before, and this report is its second installment of the data it wants others to publish too. We audited the claims around recursive self-improvement, including the slowdown call and the thresholds OpenAI set for itself, in <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/the-loop-is-closing-rsi-slowdown-call.html">The Loop Is Closing: RSI, the Slowdown Call, and the Data</a>.</p>
<p>The report's own capability measure, the OpenAI ECI, rose 16.4 points a year since August 2025, against the 14-point-a-year trend Epoch AI found across the industry since late 2024. OpenAI notes its own models score higher on its version of the index than on Epoch's, likely because of differences in the benchmark mix, which is the honest caveat to carry with the number. The ideas behind the GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.2 Astra releases still came mostly from people: for both releases, posttraining and safety researchers said people came up with all the ideas they named, and models helped implement them. For GPT-6.2 Astra, one pretraining researcher credited Codex with identifying key insights while working through an idea together, and another said the main idea was theirs but only started working well after Codex suggested a change.</p>
<h2>The work is jagged</h2>
<p>The report's title is the finding that matters most. Agents do not help evenly. By task type, from August 1 to October 18, 2026, agents completed 79 percent of monitoring-training-run tasks without human help, and 72 percent of answering-technical-questions tasks, but only 35 percent of launching-training-run tasks, 29 percent of building evaluation, reinforcement-learning, and agent-environment tasks, and 25 percent of writing code for inference and serving stacks. By category, the autonomous success rate was 69 percent for communicating, 58 percent for analyzing, and 43 percent for building.</p>
<p>The researchers' own words are blunter than the numbers. Agents have poor taste in research ideas, code quality, data quality, and writing. They rabbit-holed on unnecessary steps, wrote what one researcher called AI slop pull requests, produced unusable data artifacts, and made invalid analysis assumptions. One researcher said that after being burned too many times by asking an agent to copy an existing task, the workflow is now to have the agent make one, review and edit it, and only then let it do the rest. The uplift is real and it is uneven, and the unevenness is where the bottlenecks live.</p>
<h2>How this compares with independent measurement</h2>
<p>OpenAI's numbers are self-reported, so the check is the independent measurement. METR, the independent evaluator, assessed in November that AI was speeding up AI research by about 1.9 times, with a wide uncertainty range, and disputed whether the field had crossed the 2-times threshold. We audited that assessment in <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/ai-is-building-ai-twice-as-fast-we.html">AI Is Building AI Twice as Fast. We Audited the METR Report</a>. OpenAI's report and METR's audit point in the same direction but measure different things: OpenAI counts its own usage and task completion, METR estimates the speed-up of the research itself. Neither proves that research progress, as opposed to research activity, is accelerating at the same rate. Activity is up about 11 times in agent workdays, but experiments per researcher are up only 1.68 times, and lines of code are up 7.45 times while code volume is not progress.</p>
<h2>The other lab's study</h2>
<p>Anthropic published the parallel measurement two weeks earlier, and it is the right comparison. Its study of 207,049 research sessions found AI could do 27 to 52 percent of a scientist's job, depending on the field, with a median of 38 percent. We read that study in <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/ai-could-do-27-52-of-scientists-job-we.html">AI Could Do 27-52% of a Scientist's Job. We Read the Study</a>. Two labs, two studies, the same question: how much of science can AI do, and how fast is that share growing? OpenAI's answer is measured in dollars and agent workdays; Anthropic's is measured in fields and tasks. Both are company-reported, and both need the same reading: the direction is clear, the exact speed is not.</p>
<h2>What it proves and what it does not</h2>
<p>What it proves is that OpenAI's researchers now lean on agents heavily, value them highly, and delegate more work to them every month. In a survey of 101 research staff, the median researcher said they would trade 40 percent of their team's headcount, or 30 percent of its compute, to keep frontier coding agents for three months, and the median said it would take three months to do a month's work without them. Twenty-seven of 28 respondents said they would rather work with the latest internal model than with a mid-career human research engineer and a 2024 model. Safety and Alignment work grew 77 percent from January to September, the largest absolute increase of any research area, with an inflection after the July Hugging Face incident.</p>
<p>What it does not prove is that research itself is getting faster at the same rate. OpenAI cautions that researchers may have changed which tasks they delegate within each duration band, so the rising success rates reflect both better models and changing choices about what to hand over. The report says the historical trends it reports, which involve significant human-AI collaboration, may not be predictive of future AI-driven progress. That is the sentence to keep. The token bill is real, the delegation is real, and the speed-up of science itself is still an open measurement, not a settled fact.</p>
