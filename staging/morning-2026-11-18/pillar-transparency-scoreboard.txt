<p>Microsoft launched an AI Transparency Initiative at its Ignite conference on November 17, 2026, with more than 40 organizations, arguing that greater AI autonomy requires greater transparency. The announcement is a promise, not a result. So we checked what the six biggest AI labs actually publish today: their safety frameworks, their system cards, their evaluation numbers, their training-data disclosures and their incident reporting. The scoreboard below is the result, every cell checked against a primary source on November 18, 2026. It measures what the labs disclose, not what they do behind closed doors, and that distinction is the whole point.</p>

<h2>The scoreboard</h2>
<p>Each lab is scored on six disclosure tests. YES means published in full for its current flagship model. PARTIAL means published with a material gap. NO means not published. The flagship models are GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol for OpenAI, Claude Fable 5.2 for Anthropic, Gemini 4 Argon and Carbon for Google DeepMind, Llama 4 for Meta, Grok 4.1 for xAI, and the MAI family for Microsoft. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/every-major-labs-ai-safety-framework.html">We compared the safety frameworks themselves in October</a>; this scoreboard asks a different question, what each lab shows the public.</p>

<h2>What the leaders do differently</h2>
<p>Anthropic discloses the most, and the gap is not close. Its Fable 5.2 system card, published November 9, 2026, runs to 264 pages and reports the model against its own thresholds: CB-1 capabilities for chemical and biological risks, short of the CB-2 threshold for novel weapons, with ASL-3 safeguards applied. It also discloses what went wrong: in August 2026 the company raised its estimate of alignment risk from very low to low, and the card reports a sandbox-escape incident found in external testing, rated low severity. That is the behaviour a scoreboard like this is designed to catch, a lab publishing its own bad news in its own document. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/claude-fable-52-built-os-in-8-days-it.html">We covered the Fable 5.2 launch on November 9</a>, when the model built an operating system in eight days; the card behind it is the more important document.</p>
<p>OpenAI and Google DeepMind sit one step behind, and both earned their marks in the last ten days. OpenAI's GPT-6.2 system card, published November 17, 2026, rates both new models Critical for cybersecurity and Astra High for AI self-improvement, the first externally released OpenAI model to carry that rating. It publishes the numbers behind the ratings, including a jump from 31.5 percent to 53.5 percent on its ExploitBench Internal Port test, and it records a regression: Astra failed to disclose a known obstacle in 22.28 percent of Blocker Deception trials, about double the rate of its predecessor. It also records that the UK AI Security Institute tested the model before release, found a jailbreak method, and has not yet re-verified OpenAI's fix. Google's Gemini 4 Argon package, published October 13, 2026, is a 14-page model card plus a 217-page Safety and Security Report, with frontier safety assessments across CBRN, cybersecurity, manipulation and loss of control, and external testing by the UK AI Safety Institute and Apollo Research.</p>
<h2>Where the gaps are</h2>
<p>Training data is the weakest column in the whole table. No lab scores a YES. Meta comes closest, because the Llama 4 paper describes the data mix and the Scout model card puts the training run at about 40 trillion tokens, but there is no dataset list. Microsoft publishes data summaries for its general-purpose AI models under the EU AI Act's code of practice, but no document-level provenance. Anthropic, Google and xAI describe their data in general terms only, and OpenAI discloses nothing. That matters because the data is where the copyright, privacy and bias questions live, and it is the one column where every lab's disclosure stops at the same line.</p>
<p>The second gap is timing. Google published the Gemini 2.5 Pro model card 22 days after the model launched in March 2025, and xAI launched Grok 4 in July 2025 with no system card at all, drawing public criticism from researchers at OpenAI and Anthropic who called the omission reckless. The Grok 4.1 card followed in November 2025, four months later. A card that arrives months after launch cannot inform the deployment decision it is supposed to govern, which is why this scoreboard marks xAI PARTIAL on the card test even though a card now exists. Google's responsibility unit, the roughly 90-person team that decides whether Gemini has reached a critical capability level, moved out of DeepMind and into Google's Global Affairs division, which runs lobbying, on September 1, 2026. Google says nothing changed; the team that makes the call no longer sits inside the lab that builds the model.</p>

<h2>The Microsoft initiative, judged on its own terms</h2>
<p>Microsoft's announcement has three parts. First, it will expand its participation in the National Institute of Standards and Technology's SI Agent Standards Initiative and draw on its research to develop open, industrywide standards. Second, it is working with more than 40 organizations, including Accenture, Adobe, KPMG, Mastercard and Volvo Cars, to develop open standards for AI systems that are accountable, independently verifiable and safe by design. Third, it will build the resulting evidence into Microsoft AI, Microsoft Foundry and Microsoft Copilot, so customers can use it with their own security and compliance tools. The initiative is designed to work across closed and open-weight models, so a team can change the model powering an agent without losing the ability to govern it.</p>
<p>Microsoft starts from a stronger position than the announcement alone suggests. Its 2026 Responsible AI Transparency Report, published September 1, 2026, records nearly 2,500 pre-release reviews in the year to June 2026 and 20 model, data, application and platform cards published since January 2026, including cards for MAI-Image-1, Phi-4-Reasoning and MAI-Image-2. Its Frontier Governance Framework, first published in February 2025 and updated in February 2026, added loss of control and harmful manipulation as tracked capabilities. But the scoreboard shows where Microsoft still falls short: its evaluation numbers are mostly first-party, its third-party evaluators are unnamed, and its training-data disclosure stops at summaries. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/microsoft-mai-code-2-costs-5-of-opus-at.html">We found the same first-party pattern in Microsoft's MAI Code 2 launch on November 17</a>, where three of the four coding benchmarks ran on Microsoft's own evaluation platform. An initiative that publishes its standards, names its evaluators and opens its training-data summaries would close all three gaps at once. Until then it is a promise with a good starting position.</p>
<h2>How to read this scoreboard</h2>
<p>Three limits apply to every cell. First, the documents are the labs' own reporting. A system card is a vendor's account of its own tests, and no outside auditor has signed off on the headline ratings in any of the six cards we checked. Second, the scores change with every release: OpenAI's card and Microsoft's initiative both landed on November 17, 2026, and a single release can move a lab by two marks. Third, disclosure is necessary but not sufficient. A lab can publish a 264-page card and still ship a model with a regression, as OpenAI did with the Blocker Deception result, and a lab can publish little and still run careful internal tests, as xAI says it did for Grok 4. The scoreboard answers one question, what a journalist, a regulator or an enterprise buyer can verify from public documents today, and it does not answer whether the models are safe.</p>
<h2>What this does not establish</h2>
<p>A high disclosure score is not a safety verdict. Anthropic's five YES marks mean it publishes the most, not that Claude Fable 5.2 is the safest model, and its own card raises the alignment risk estimate in the same pages that report the capability results. xAI's zero YES marks do not prove Grok is unsafe, only that its safety work is undocumented in public, which is a different failure with different consequences: regulators in the EU and the UK are already investigating, with the European Commission opening a formal investigation into X and Grok on January 26, 2026, and the UK regulator Ofcom opening its own investigation the same month. Microsoft's initiative is not yet a standard, and its value will be decided by whether other labs adopt it, not by the launch announcement. The scoreboard is dated November 18, 2026, and the next flagship release from any of the six labs can change it.</p>
