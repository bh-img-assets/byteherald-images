<p>Google introduced Gemini 4 Neon on November 18, 2026, a model that sits between the fast Gemini 4 Hydrogen and the heavyweight Gemini 4 Carbon, priced at $3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output tokens, with a one-million-token context window. Google says Neon scores 61 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index at its highest thinking level, third overall, and ranks first on the Artificial Analysis Coding Agent Index. It is rolling out today in the Gemini API in Google AI Studio, in Gemini for business, in Google Antigravity for paid Pro and Ultra subscribers, and in the Gemini app for Pro and Ultra subscribers.</p>

<h2>Where Neon sits in the family</h2>
<p>Neon is the fourth Gemini 4 model in seven weeks, according to Artificial Analysis, and Google describes it as the middle of the family: Carbon takes the hardest, long-horizon projects, Hydrogen handles fast everyday execution, and Neon balances depth and efficiency for complex work done often. The Gemini app picker now reads Auto, Everyday help (Gemini 4 Hydrogen), Deeper analysis (Gemini 4 Neon) and Code and complex work (Gemini 4 Carbon), according to 9to5Google, which also reports that Neon replaces Argon in the app. Neon also reaches Google AI Pro subscribers, not only Ultra, which matters because Carbon sat in the Ultra tier when it led the index at launch in October.</p>

<p>The price table carries the first catch. Until November 30, Carbon's introductory price is lower than Neon's per token, so Neon costs 50 percent more per token than Carbon today and becomes 25 percent cheaper per token only when Carbon's price doubles on December 1. Google's headline saving, 32 percent less per task than Carbon at the default high thinking level, is measured against Carbon's standard $4 and $20 pricing, not the discounted price in force today. Anyone comparing the two models this week has to pick a pricing basis first, because the answer changes with it.</p>
<h2>The benchmark scoreboard</h2>
<p>Google's announcement leans on third-party indexes rather than its own tests, and the list is long. Neon ranks second on the Vals Index at 70.9 percent, first on the Legal Research Bench and the Tax Agent Bench, and first on most Surge AI benchmarks across finance, accounting, spreadsheet modeling and complex constraint following. It scores 73.5 percent on Chartography for reading dense professional charts and 75.8 percent on Agents' Last Exam v1.1. On the agent side it debuts second globally with a score of 1336 on the Agent Arena Index across more than 4,200 real-world agent sessions, and ranks first on Zapier's AutomationBench at 56.3 percent and on Andon Labs' Vending-Bench 2 at $16,843, a long-horizon simulation of running a business.</p>

<p>The coding numbers deserve a closer look, because they are where Neon is meant to earn its keep. At its default high setting it scores 46.2 percent on FrontierCode 2.0, and on SWE-sweep it leads the mini harness at 12.8 percent and more than triples to 39.0 percent inside Google Antigravity, which Google presents as what its own harness unlocks. As an orchestrator it can direct parallel Hydrogen subagents, cut redundant file reads, share progress as it works and handle context compaction in long sessions, according to the announcement. Those are workflow claims, not benchmark scores, and only use will show whether they hold outside Google's own tooling.</p>

<h2>The December 1 price catch, worked out</h2>
<p>Google says Neon costs 32 percent less per task than Carbon at its default high thinking level. Artificial Analysis, measuring independently, puts Neon at $6.44 per task against Carbon at $7.76 at non-discounted pricing, about 17 percent less, and finds Neon takes about 21 minutes per task against Carbon's 36, about 40 percent less time. The two figures differ because they compare different thinking levels: Google's 32 percent is high against high, while the 17 percent compares Neon's highest thinking level against Carbon's high. Both use Carbon's standard $4 and $20 pricing as the baseline, which is the honest basis for a lasting comparison but not the price anyone pays this week.</p>
<p>On today's prices the math runs the other way. Carbon's introductory $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens hold through November 30, and Neon at $3 and $15 costs 50 percent more per token. At that ratio, Neon only wins on cost per task if it finishes the same job using fewer than two-thirds of the tokens Carbon would use, by ByteHerald arithmetic from the two published prices. From December 1, when Carbon rises to $4 and $20, Neon becomes 25 percent cheaper per token and wins at equal token use. There is one more wrinkle: Neon uses about 15 percent more output tokens per task than Carbon, according to Artificial Analysis, so its saving comes from the lower per-token price and a faster output speed, about 110 tokens per second against Carbon's 57, not from using fewer tokens. The December 1 rise was already on the calendar: we tracked the expiring discounts across ten frontier models in October, and Carbon's doubling was on the list, in <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/ai-got-75-cheaper-discounts-are-expiring.html">AI Got 75% Cheaper. The Discounts Are Expiring.</a></p>
<h2>The cyber variant, and the safety numbers</h2>
<p>Neon also ships in a cyber-tuned variant, Gemini 4 Neon for cyber, which rolls out to trusted defenders in the coming days as part of Google's Fairwind Program rather than to general availability. On AutoBaxBench it produces more secure code than Carbon across easy, medium and hard challenges and reaches state of the art on the hard tier at 76 percent. On WebExploitBench v1.2 it is state of the art at 51.26 percent at half the cost of competing large models. On SEC-bench Pro it reaches 90.5 percent at roughly half the cost per challenge, on ExploitGym it reaches frontier-level performance using half the tokens Carbon needs, and on CWE-bench v2 it reaches 70.7 percent, a 3.4 percent uplift over Carbon.</p>
<p>The safety numbers are unusually specific, and they cut both ways for a model built to run long tasks unsupervised. Neon matched Carbon with 0.0 percent cheating on Terminal-Bench 4.0 and took shortcuts on 2.45 percent of tasks on Google's coding cheating evaluation, the lowest rate of any Gemini 4 model. It recorded a 0.09 percent misaligned action rate on Google's agentic safety suite and a 99.2 percent pass rate on its agent honesty evaluation, reduced under-service by 43 to 56 percent and overreach by 15 to 35 percent against Carbon's baseline, and resisted every attack on Gray Swan's indirect prompt injection benchmark at its highest thinking level, a 0.0 percent attack success rate. It also declined 1.98 percent of tasks on Terminal-Bench 4.0 and none on Terminal-Bench-Science, which Google frames as declining far less legitimate work than Carbon while keeping the same protections against severe misuse and prompt injection. Those are Google's internal measurements, and no independent lab has published its own Neon safety audit yet.</p>
<h2>Which model to pick</h2>
<p>The picker in the Gemini app now makes the choice explicit: Auto, Everyday help for Hydrogen, Deeper analysis for Neon, and Code and complex work for Carbon. The rule that follows from the numbers is simple. Use Hydrogen for quick questions and everyday execution, where it is fastest and cheapest. Use Neon for deep work that repeats often, long documents, analysis, coding agents and multi-step jobs, especially after December 1, when its per-token price falls below Carbon's. Keep Carbon for the hardest, longest projects where the extra reasoning is worth the higher price and the slower pace. Before December 1, check the token math on your own workload: if Neon does not finish the job with substantially fewer tokens, Carbon's introductory price is still the cheaper option this week. Carbon led the index when it launched in October, but sat in the Ultra tier, as we reported in <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/gemini-4-carbon-leads-index-but-you.html">Gemini 4 Carbon Leads the Index, but You Cannot Use It</a>, and Neon's arrival on Pro as well as Ultra is the more practical change for most subscribers.</p>
