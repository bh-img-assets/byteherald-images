<p><a href="https://signal.org/blog/protecting-your-privacy/">Signal</a> said on November 20, 2026, that it will block screenshots inside its app on iPhones and Android phones, because AI assistants built into the operating system, Apple's Siri AI and Google's Gemini Intelligence, can read whatever is on your screen, including your private chats. The block is <a href="https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/signal-restricts-screenshots-block-ai-agents-reading-messages-2026-11-20/">on by default</a>. On a Mac, Signal says it cannot do the same, and its president calls the situation an <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/91646370/signal-blocks-screenshots-ai-agents-hostile-privacy-meredith-whittaker">existential threat</a> to the app.</p>

<h2>What changes on your phone</h2>
<p>On iOS 27, Signal blocks screenshots inside the app by default and adds a setting called <a href="https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/1038390/signal-blocking-ai-agent-screenshots-mobile-app">Block Siri AI</a>, which stops Siri from scanning Signal content and blocks screenshots in the app and in the App Switcher. On Android, Signal automatically switches on its Screen security feature when it detects an AI assistant trying to read the screen. If you want screenshots back, you can turn the protection off in Signal's settings, on either platform. Signal warns that doing so exposes your messages, and your contacts' messages, to the same assistants, because a screenshot of a chat you received shows the other person's words too.</p>
<p>When Signal detects a screenshot attempt or an assistant reaching for the screen, it <a href="https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/signal-restricts-screenshots-block-ai-agents-reading-messages-2026-11-20/">shows a pop-up with the option to disable the block</a>. The company is not hiding the control. It is making the trade visible: screenshots on, or privacy on, and the choice is yours each time it comes up.</p>

<h2>Why screenshots are the battleground</h2>
<p>End-to-end encryption protects messages while they travel between phones. It does nothing once a message is on your screen. An assistant that reads the screen never has to break the encryption. It captures the same text the recipient sees, and that copy can sit in a local database or move to whichever server the agent relies on, outside Signal's control. Signal's president, Meredith Whittaker, put it this way in <a href="https://signal.org/blog/protecting-your-privacy/">the company's blog post</a>: agentic AI integrations are changing the environment in a way that is making it increasingly hostile to privacy, and this is an existential threat to Signal and its ability to responsibly uphold its mission.</p>
<p>Signal says it spent months doing forensic work on Android and iOS to learn how these assistants grab app data, because the platform documentation offered too little detail. That is the sentence that explains why this took so long and why the fix is partial. The company had to reverse-engineer the behavior of the operating systems its app runs on, and it still cannot see everything the assistants do. It is asking Apple, Google, and Microsoft for two things: a documented way for developers to opt their apps out of AI data access and processing, across screen reading, notifications, and filesystem access, and more information about how the agents function, so developers can assess what data the assistants are accessing. Signal's move fits a pattern we have tracked all year: AI agents reaching private data through side doors, from accessibility settings to screen reading. We keep the running record of those incidents in <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/assume-breach-2026-ai-security-report.html">Assume Breach: The 2026 AI Security Report Card</a>, and this screenshot block is the latest defensive entry in it.</p>
<h2>What the block does not fix</h2>
<p>Signal is upfront that blocking screenshots is a partial defense. An assistant can still reach content already stored on the device through filesystem access, and the screenshot block does nothing about that route. On macOS, Signal says it lacks the tools to block screenshots reliably, so its advice for Mac users is to add Signal to the Excluded Apps list under System Settings, then Siri, so Siri AI cannot proactively read message content. <a href="https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/10/victory-you-can-now-exclude-apps-siri-ai">The Electronic Frontier Foundation</a> points to the same control on iPhones running iOS 27.2: Settings, then Siri, then Excluded Apps, and add Signal there.</p>
<p>The company also rejects the idea that confidential computing answers the problem. Running the assistant's processing in a sealed enclave still leaves the platform owner in charge of deciding how much privacy users get, and Signal argues that is the wrong place for the decision to sit. The fix it wants is not a better enclave. It is an opt-out the app developer controls.</p>
<h2>The week's screen-access story</h2>
<p>Signal's move is the third screen-access story in three days, and the three point in three directions. On November 18, <a href="https://www.reuters.com/investigations/meta-bets-big-muse-ai-agent-early-safety-mishaps-surface-2026-11-18/">Reuters reported</a> that Meta's Muse AI agent had mixed up private chats and bank balances, reaching into Signal chats through accessibility settings on a Mac and an Android phone. We covered that investigation in <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/metas-muse-mixed-up-private-chats-and.html">Meta's Muse Mixed Up Private Chats and Bank Balances</a>. On November 19, Google shipped computer use in the Gemini Mac app, letting Gemini fill in forms, repeat tasks across files, and build slideshows, while pausing for logins and payments. We covered that in <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/gemini-can-now-use-your-mac-but-only-on.html">Gemini Can Now Use Your Mac, but Only on Paid Plans</a>. Now Signal is blocking the screen altogether. One app reads your screen, one app drives your screen, and one app bars the door. The operating system is the same in all three stories, and that is the point: whoever controls what an assistant can see controls the privacy of everything on the phone.</p>
<h2>How to check what your assistant can see</h2>
<p>The controls already exist, scattered across three settings menus. On an iPhone running iOS 27.2, open Settings, then Siri, then Excluded Apps, and add any app you do not want Siri to read. Inside Signal, the Block Siri AI toggle in the app's security settings does the same job and also blocks screenshots. On Android, Signal's Screen security switches on by itself when it detects an assistant, and you can find it under the app's privacy settings. On a Mac, open System Settings, then Siri, and add Signal to Excluded Apps. None of these controls stops an assistant reading files already on the device, and none of them is a substitute for the opt-out Signal is asking the platform owners to build. But they are the controls that exist today, and they are worth setting before the next assistant asks for your screen.</p>
