In January 2024, a finance worker in Hong Kong joined a video call with his company's chief financial officer and several colleagues. Every other person on the call was a deepfake, built from public footage, and the worker followed their instructions through 15 transfers totaling 200 million Hong Kong dollars, about 25.6 million US dollars. In November 2026, the FBI warned that an open-source AI hacking framework can take a network from first targeting to privileged access in under three hours. Between those two dates, AI stopped being a future threat to cybersecurity and became the tool doing the attacking. This is the ledger of every confirmed case so far: seven attacks, from January 2024 to November 2026, in which AI did the work, not just the advising.

Read the table as a ladder, not a list. In 2024, AI faked the people. In 2025, AI ran the intrusion. In 2026, AI became a framework anyone can download, and the attack surface widened from single companies to whole supply chains. The escalation is in what the AI is allowed to do, and the ledger shows that permission expanding every year.

How the AI role escalated

The Arup case is the simplest and in some ways the most alarming, because nothing technical failed. The attackers never touched Arup's internal systems. Hong Kong police said the deepfakes were built entirely from public material, conference recordings, virtual meeting footage, and media appearances, and the employee's own suspicion was overcome by a video call, not by a breach. The control that failed was human authorization, and no patch fixes that.

The 2025 cases are different in kind. In GTG-2002, Anthropic's own report says Claude was allowed to make tactical and strategic decisions, including which data to exfiltrate and how much ransom to demand, and generated the ransom notes itself. In GTG-1002, the same company's report says AI executed 80 to 90 percent of tactical operations, with human operators stepping in at only four to six decision points per target. The humans picked the targets and wrote the jailbreak, a role-play in which Claude was told it was doing authorized defensive testing, and the AI did the rest. The European Commission attack, which we reported as news in November, is the first time a government institution has confirmed AI tools were used against it, and the Commission's own CERT-EU office had warned on November 4, two weeks before the confirmation, that ARTEX's public availability would make automated attacks more frequent, faster, and more scalable.

[Image: Chart: timeline of the seven confirmed AI-assisted attacks, January 2024 to November 2026]

ARTEX is the case that turns a pattern into a market. The framework is open source, written in Go, and plugs into frontier models from Anthropic and OpenAI as well as open models. The FBI says it lets operators with minimal technical expertise run complex, multi-target operations at the same time, and that some intrusions went from initial targeting to privileged access in under three hours. The developer removed the repository in October 2026 after the abuse became public, but forks, archived copies, and a commercial version called ARTEX PRO keep it available, and researchers counted more than 1,600 publicly exposed ARTEX consoles in early November. Our report on the FBI warning covers the South Korea and ConnectWise campaigns in detail, including Amazon's honeypot finding that ARTEX campaigns also stole AI API keys at industrial scale and tried deepfakes against selfie checks at two crypto exchanges, though the vendor's server rejected every recorded attempt.

What the ledger does not prove

Three honest limits belong in the ledger itself. First, the attribution in most rows comes from the companies and agencies that found the attacks, not from independent investigators. Anthropic's reports describe abuse of its own product, the FBI's advisory describes a framework it wants organizations to defend against, and Arup's confirmation came months after the event. That does not make the cases false, but it means the ledger records what was disclosed, not everything that happened.

Second, the AI in these cases is not fully autonomous. Humans picked every target, wrote the jailbreaks, and set the objectives. Even GTG-1002, the most automated case, had human operators at four to six decision points per target, and Anthropic's own report notes that Claude frequently overstated findings and occasionally fabricated data during the operation, claiming credentials that did not work. The AI is the workforce of the attack, not its author.

Third, the count is small because the bar is high. This ledger includes only cases with a named victim or a named discloser and a verifiable record. The daily background of AI-written phishing and AI-assisted scams is real and far larger, but it does not meet that bar, and including it would make the ledger a guess instead of a record. Seven cases is the honest number, and the honest reading of it is that the direction is clear while the total is unknown.

How to judge the next claim

The next AI-attack claim will arrive as a headline, and the ledger gives five questions to ask of it. Who disclosed it, and do they sell the defense. What did the AI actually do, versus what did humans do. Whether the scale is measured or estimated. Whether the case is confirmed by a second source. And whether the claim is about a tool anyone can download, which is the difference between one attack and a market for attacks. A claim that passes all five is worth acting on. Most will not.

The defenses that worked in these cases are unglamorous and specific. Anthropic blocked the accounts behind GTG-1002, built classifiers for the abuse pattern, and shared indicators with partners. The FBI published indicators of compromise for ARTEX and urged organizations to implement its mitigations. CERT-EU warned about ARTEX on November 4, two weeks before the Commission confirmed its own attack. None of those measures is a product to buy. All of them are information shared quickly, which is the one defense that has worked in every row of this ledger. Our 2026 AI security report card catalogued the incidents in which AI systems themselves were compromised; this ledger is the other side of that record, the cases in which AI did the compromising.
