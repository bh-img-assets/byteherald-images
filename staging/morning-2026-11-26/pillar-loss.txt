<p><a href="https://openai.com/index/perplexity-and-intelligence/">OpenAI published base-model log-perplexity for 13 models on November 22, 2026, measured on a fixed held-out dataset from its internal codebase</a>. <a href="https://metr.org/blog/2026-11-24-interpreting-a-recent-acceleration-in-pretraining-loss/">METR fitted that curve on November 24, 2026 and found loss falling about seven times faster since early 2026 than from 2022 through 2025</a>. The scoreboard below compiles the published anchors, the chart values, and the one cross-lab reply. It also states what the ruler cannot prove.</p>

<h2>The scoreboard</h2>
<p>Log-perplexity measures how well a base model predicts the next token in text it has not seen. Lower means stronger prediction. Pretraining is the first stage, where a model trains as a compressor on a large corpus before later tuning elicits specific skills. OpenAI states two anchors in essay text: GPT-3.5 at about 0.99 and GPT-4 at about 0.91. The essay also states that losses below 0.8 brought transformative capabilities in cybersecurity and mathematics, that a model near 0.76 produced groundbreaking mathematics results, and that further transformative capabilities should be expected in the 0.68 to 0.70 window. OpenAI published a chart, not a data table. Values marked as chart reads come from that chart and carry reading error. Perplexity in the third column converts log-perplexity with the exponential function. A value of 2.14 means the model spreads belief across about 2.14 effective options per token on this dataset.</p>

<h2>The acceleration math</h2>
<p>METR fitted two segments with a breakpoint in February 2026. Loss fell about 0.022 per year from 2022 to early 2026, then about 0.156 per year. The ratio is 7.1 to 1. At the faster pace, the drop from GPT-3.5 to GPT-4.1 that took three years would take about six and a half months. Standard scaling laws predict the opposite shape. As loss approaches an unknown floor, each tenfold compute increase buys a smaller drop. The observed curve steepens where the default expectation flattens. That tension drives the dispute.</p>
<p>The gaps shrink fast at the top. GPT-6 Astra to GPT-6.2 Astra spans 0.022, equal to one full year at the old pace. GPT-6.2 Astra to the internal model spans 0.029. The internal model sits 0.032 above the top of the expected window at 0.70 and 0.052 above the bottom at 0.68. At 0.156 per year, arithmetic gives about 2.5 months to 0.70 and 4.0 months to 0.68. At 0.022 per year, reaching 0.70 would take about 17.5 months. Those month counts assume the fitted pace holds and the chart reads are exact. METR states no consensus that either assumption holds.</p>
<h2>Three readings, no consensus</h2>
<table><thead><tr><th>Reading</th><th>What it claims</th><th>What would support it</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Data overstates progress</td><td>Overfitting or leakage inflates the drop on one internal codebase holdout</td><td>Loss on other holdouts, such as books, falls more slowly</td></tr><tr><td>Benchmarks are the endpoint</td><td>Perplexity is an input, and task scores measure real capability</td><td>Epoch ECI and task evals keep showing no matching acceleration</td></tr><tr><td>Perplexity runs deeper</td><td>Compression gains precede broad capability gains that benchmarks miss</td><td>Real-world task performance accelerates within about six months</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>METR lists three missing inputs. Loss on other held-out datasets would test robustness beyond code. Pretraining end dates would separate training progress from release timing, since base models can finish months before launch. Compute, labor, and experiment spending would separate a one-time spike from compounding AI-assisted research. OpenAI lists matching limits. Labs use different datasets and mixtures. Perplexity does not name a task buyers care about. Chain-of-thought reasoning raises capability without lowering perplexity. The floor stays unknown, so smaller future drops may carry larger capability gains. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/ai-proves-70-of-theorems-but-solves.html">Our theorem-proving scoreboard showed why task endpoints still matter</a>: strong benchmark scores coexisted with near-zero open-problem solves. A loss ruler and a task ruler measure different things. Forecasts need both.</p>
<p>Use the scoreboard with its method attached. State the dataset, the date, and whether a value comes from essay text or a chart read. Pair any capability claim with a task evaluation on the same model. Treat the 0.68 to 0.70 window as OpenAI expectation, not a measured threshold. The next decisive evidence is simple to name: another lab publishing loss on a shared holdout, OpenAI publishing loss on a non-code holdout, or task scores bending upward to meet the loss curve. Until one arrives, the acceleration is a fitted fact about one ruler and an open question about capability.</p>
