<p>OpenAI says it cut the success rate of attacks that steal a frontier model's reasoning from 54 percent to 0.2 percent. The number comes from the company's own red-team tests, published on November 20, 2026, and it measures something narrower than the headline suggests. This is the audit: what OpenAI built, what the number proves, and what it does not.</p>

<h2>The problem: a model can be copied without its conscience</h2>
<p>Distillation is a normal machine-learning technique. One model learns from data produced by another: its answers, its reasoning, or its judgments about which answers are better and why. Adversarial distillation is the same technique used without authorization, to train a second model with similar capabilities and weaker safeguards. The safety risk is the point. A model that helps defenders find vulnerabilities in software could also help an attacker exploit them. A model that assists with biological research could also help someone develop a harmful pathogen. If those capabilities move to a model without the original's refusal behavior and monitoring, the new model may provide help the original would have refused.</p>
<p>The most valuable thing to steal is not the answer. It is the reasoning. OpenAI calls it "protected reasoning": the intermediate steps a model generates internally while working toward an answer, known as its chain-of-thought. Those traces can contain information the final answer leaves out, which makes them especially valuable as training data for another model. That is why OpenAI's post, <a href="https://openai.com/index/combatting-adversarial-distillation/">"Combatting adversarial distillation"</a>, published on November 20, 2026, focuses on protecting reasoning traces rather than outputs in general. It is also why this matters beyond OpenAI: <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/opencodes-distillation-attack-what.html">the OpenCode distillation attack we covered in October</a> showed the other side of the same problem, an attack that worked. This post is the defense.</p>
<h2>The defense, in four layers</h2>
<p>OpenAI defends at two levels that reinforce each other. At the request level, model behavior, classifier checks, and API controls limit how much reasoning can be extracted in a single interaction. At the campaign level, the company looks for patterns across accounts, sessions, infrastructure, and time that reveal coordinated extraction. Attacks uncovered in investigations become training examples for the safeguards, so each campaign makes the next one harder to run.</p>

<p>The serving-stack layer deserves attention because it is the one a prompt alone cannot beat. System prompts can be talked around; controls in the inference stack can interrupt output, restrict traffic, or suspend accounts even after an adversarial prompt persuades the model to start producing protected material. OpenAI says it closed a replay path described in its September disclosure, in which encrypted reasoning was moved from one conversation into another and the model was asked to reveal it in readable form. With GPT-6.2 Astra and GPT-6.2 Sol, protected reasoning is tied to the conversation history that produced it, so rewriting an earlier message drops the reasoning that followed it, and older model families with weaker safeguards cannot read GPT-6.2 reasoning at all.</p>
<h2>The Moonshot campaign</h2>
<p>The defense was built from a real investigation, and OpenAI has now described it twice. Its September 30, 2026, <a href="https://openai.com/index/disrupting-a-coordinated-model-distillation-campaign/">disruption report</a> says the earliest activity appeared in the first week of July, with high-volume spikes on July 24 and 25 consisting of 16,000 requests using a relevant extraction pattern, from over 4,000 users. Related prompt-pattern activity ran across a cluster of more than 15,000 users, and the campaign was fully disrupted by July 28. The operators did not break the encryption, compromise a database, or gain direct access to stored conversations. They manipulated model interactions so protected reasoning could be reproduced in visible form. On attribution, OpenAI says it attributes a core cluster of the activity to individuals associated with Moonshot AI, the developer of Kimi, while adding that it is unclear whether all the operators observed came from a single actor. That is the scale that makes the campaign layer necessary: no single request in such a campaign looks like an attack. Only the pattern across accounts does. When OpenAI identifies violations, it says it enforces against the network of accounts, shares indicators of compromise with other model providers, and feeds what it learned back into training and testing. The attribution to Moonshot is OpenAI's, not independently confirmed, and the company has not published the underlying evidence. What is verifiable is the shape of the response: a provider that finds a coordinated campaign now treats it as an investigation, not a moderation queue.</p>
<h2>The cyber study: why exact reasoning matters</h2>
<p>OpenAI's post includes an internal study on how much cyber capability actually transfers when a student model is trained on a teacher's outputs. Two training runs started from the same pretrained student. One learned from exact teacher reasoning traces; the other learned from the user-visible baseline of final responses, reasoning summaries, and tool interactions. The fine-tuning data consisted primarily of Astra-level teacher trajectories on coding and defensive cybersecurity tasks. After supervised fine-tuning, the exact-trace student generally outperformed the summary-trained student. After a round of reinforcement learning, the exact-trace student still scored higher at the largest inference budget tested on both reported benchmarks, while the summary-trained student scored higher at lower budgets.</p>
<div class="separator" style="clear: both; text-align: center;"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bh-img-assets/byteherald-images/main/img/pillar-distillation-chart2.png" alt="Chart: timeline of the distillation defense, July 2026 Moonshot campaign, September 2026 encrypted reasoning disclosure, November 20 2026 defense post" /></div>
<p>The result is narrower than it sounds, and OpenAI says so. The study shows that protecting exact traces can limit cyber capability transfer even when the underlying task is allowed under the company's usage policies, because exact traces teach skills that generalize beyond the tasks the teacher performed. But the benchmarks are not named in the public summary, the student models are not named, and the company says it will keep studying how much transfers across different student models and training recipes. The honest reading is that OpenAI has evidence the traces matter, not a measurement of how much a real attacker would gain. That distinction is the difference between a research finding and a security guarantee.</p>
<h2>The catch: what 0.2 percent does not prove</h2>
<p>The headline number comes from an internal adaptive evaluation. Automated red-teamers, six attacker models in the test, adjusted their approach based on how the system responded, seeing only what an external user would see. They tried to recover protected reasoning the model had generated while answering an earlier prompt. The average success rate fell from 54.0 percent against GPT-6 Astra with its original safeguards to 0.2 percent against GPT-6.2 Astra with its updated safeguards. For extraction from the current request, the corresponding average fell from <a href="https://www.unite.ai/openai-strengthens-gpt-6-2-astra-against-reasoning-extraction/">22.9 percent to 0.9 percent</a>. Those are OpenAI's numbers, from OpenAI's test, against OpenAI's attackers. No independent lab has reproduced them.</p>
<p>Three limits follow. First, the figure measures successful extraction attempts, not the resulting student model's capabilities. A 0.2 percent extraction rate says almost nothing about what an attacker could build from the 0.2 percent that succeeds, or from the final responses and summaries that remain available. Second, the evaluation is adaptive but still OpenAI's: the attackers are automated systems the company built, and a human adversary with time, money, and patience is a different opponent. Third, the defense protects reasoning, not outputs. Distilling final answers is still possible, and for many capabilities the final answer is enough. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/the-misalignment-audit-how-labs-catch.html">Our audit of how labs catch models that game their own safety tests</a> found the same pattern across the industry: detection methods are real, they catch real attacks, and none of them is a proof.</p>
<p>None of that makes the post unimportant. A frontier lab publishing its defense layers, its campaign data, and its failure rates is more transparency than the industry offered a year ago, and the encrypted-reasoning fix closes a hole that an <a href="https://openai.com/index/combatting-adversarial-distillation/">August 2026 study</a> showed could be reused across the Claude, Gemini, and GPT ecosystems. The right way to read the 0.2 percent is as a claim about one layer of one system, tested by its owner, on the day it was published. Five questions decide whether any vendor's version of that claim deserves trust: who ran the test, what exactly was measured, what the attackers could see, what the number leaves out, and whether anyone independent can repeat it. OpenAI answers the first four in the post. The fifth is still open.</p>
