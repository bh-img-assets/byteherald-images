<p>President Donald Trump said on Friday, November 20, 2026, that American companies building AI data centers in other countries will face a "tremendous tax". He gave no rate, no mechanism, and no list of companies, and said he had not yet told his own officials about the plan. The threat lands on a buildout that is already moving abroad, because land, power, and permits are cheaper and faster outside the United States than inside it.</p>

<h2>What he said, word for word</h2>
<p>Trump spoke at a <a href="https://www.reuters.com/technology/trump-says-us-penalize-firms-building-data-centers-abroad-2026-11-20/">White House news conference</a> on Friday, after returning from a three-day trip to the Philippines and China, his third to Asia in his second term. The press conference ran nearly 90 minutes and covered wars, outages, and technology. On data centers, his words were blunt. "We are not going to allow companies to build data centers in other countries," he said. "If they do that, they can. But we are going to put a tremendous tax on them." He added: "We are not allowing data centers to go into other countries because China will take all of them, and we are not allowing that to happen."</p>
<p>He also urged Americans to welcome data center construction in their own communities, and to extract concessions from the developers. "You ought to take advantage of data centers," he said. "Make them pay. Make them pay your communities, but don't shut them off." And he named the places he would rather see the building: "If they will not go in Texas or they will not go in California, they are going to go in West Virginia." The framing is consistent with how his administration talks about AI, which it calls "super intelligence": whoever wins that race, wins.</p>
<h2>The buildout already abroad</h2>
<p>The companies Trump named in general terms have already announced specific overseas plans. Alphabet said earlier in November that it will invest 15 billion dollars in data center infrastructure in Malaysia in 2027 and 2028. Oracle has spent the past year expanding abroad: four of the seven gigawatts of power it plans to bring online in the next two years are tied to US hyperscaler campuses, and the rest will be spread across smaller or international sites. CoreWeave already has a significant presence in Europe and Canada, and plans to expand to Australia, India, Indonesia, Japan, South Korea, Singapore, and the United Arab Emirates, according to company materials cited by <a href="https://www.morningstar.com/news/marketwatch/20261120183/trump-is-trying-to-stop-big-techs-push-to-build-data-centers-outside-the-us">MarketWatch</a>. Reuters notes that several major American data center developers, including Equinix and Digital Realty, already build and operate facilities outside the United States.</p>

<p>The table is the point. A tax on building abroad would not hit a future plan. It would hit projects already announced, financed, and in some cases under construction. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/ais-power-and-water-bill-datacenter.html">The resource ledger we compiled in October</a> shows why the buildout is so large in the first place: announced datacenter projects now draw power in gigawatts and water in volumes that strain local grids, which is exactly why communities push back and why companies look abroad. The financing behind it is the subject of <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/how-ai-buildout-is-paid-for-november.html">our November financing ledger</a>, which tallied the instruments paying for the buildout.</p>
<h2>Why companies build abroad</h2>
<p>Three forces push construction overseas, and none of them is mysterious. The first is cost: land, labor, and electricity are cheaper in many of the countries on the expansion lists. The second is speed: permitting battles in the United States have slowed projects, and politicians and residents in many communities oppose new datacenters. A Pew Research Center survey published on September 22, 2026, found that 54 percent of US adults believe AI data centers are bad for the environment, up from 39 percent in January 2026. <a href="https://www.wionews.com/world/trump-issues-stern-warning-to-us-tech-giants-looking-to-build-data-centres-overseas-vows-tremendous-penalties-1795196488559/amp">Gallup found</a> that 71 percent of Americans oppose a data center being built in their area, with 48 percent strongly opposed. The third is the customer: a datacenter in Malaysia or Singapore serves Asian users faster than one in Texas.</p>
<p>The administration is already acting on the China angle through a channel that has a legal mechanism. On Monday, Washington moved to close a loophole that let Chinese firms rent AI computing power from data centers in Southeast Asia and elsewhere running on US hardware not available for export to China. The new export controls require data centers anywhere in the world to hold a US license to provide large volumes of computing power from advanced chips to companies headquartered in China, Macau, or other territories under US embargoes. That is a rule with a text, an enforcer, and a penalty. A tax on building abroad has none of those yet.</p>
<h2>Can he actually do it</h2>
<p>The honest answer is that nobody knows, including, by his own account, his own officials. <a href="https://www.wsj.com/politics/policy/trump-to-hold-white-house-press-conference-following-trip-to-asia-25c8ecc0">The Wall Street Journal notes</a> that it is not clear how the administration could penalize companies for building international projects, and Google, Oracle, and CoreWeave did not immediately respond to requests for comment. A tax would normally need Congress, which has just moved in the opposite direction on process: <a href="https://www.usatoday.com/story/tech/nation-now/2026/11/20/trump-tax-ai-data-centers/92881622007/">the Senate approved legislation on November 19</a> to ease the approval of data centers as part of a broader overhaul of permitting. Tariffs, which a president can impose more freely, apply to goods crossing a border, not to a building in Malaysia.</p>
<p>Three things will tell us whether this becomes policy or stays a warning. The first is a formal proposal: a rate, a definition of "American company", and whether existing projects are covered. The second is the reaction from the companies, which have billions already committed abroad and will lobby hard against a retroactive charge. The third is the China test Trump himself named: he also teased a new agreement on international AI regulations with President Xi, saying "President Xi and I agree that we are going to do some joint guardrails." A president who taxes his own companies for building abroad while negotiating joint rules with Beijing is running two different China strategies at once. Which one wins will decide whether the overseas buildout slows, moves, or simply gets more expensive.</p>
