<p><a href="https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/amazon-decart-ai-optimization">Amazon announced on November 23, 2026, that it has signed an agreement to purchase the proprietary technology behind the Decart Optimization Stack, and to bring Decart's three co-founders and their team of researchers and engineers to Amazon</a>. Bloomberg reported the price at about $7.5 billion, though Amazon did not disclose the financial terms. What Amazon is buying is software that makes AI models run faster and lets them switch between chips from Nvidia, Amazon, and Google. What it is not buying is Decart's models, including the live-video editor Lucy and the interactive world generator Oasis. Amazon paid a reported $7.5 billion for the plumbing, not the product, because the plumbing is what decides whose chips the models run on.</p>

<h2>The price, per person</h2>
<p>The numbers only make sense when they are divided. Decart was founded in 2023 and employs about 100 people, according to Morningstar, so $7.5 billion works out to about $75 million per employee. PitchBook data cited by Finimize put Decart's last valuation near $4 billion, which makes the reported price about 1.9 times what the company was last worth. The three founders, chief executive Dean Leitersdorf, his brother Orian Leitersdorf, and Moshe Shalev, hold about 64 percent of the company between them and stand to share about $4.8 billion, according to Globes, which also reported that the deal is structured in shares and tied to performance targets over five years.</p>
<div class="separator" style="clear: both; text-align: center;"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bh-img-assets/byteherald-images/main/img/hot2-decart-chart.png" alt="Chart: Decart deal price 7.5 billion dollars against its last valuation near 4 billion dollars, November 2026" /></div>
<p>The deal's shape matters as much as its size. This is an asset purchase plus hiring, not a takeover of the company: Amazon gets the optimization stack and the people who built it, while Decart's models stay behind. The agreement was signed on Sunday night after two months of negotiation, according to Calcalist, and follows failed talks with Anthropic and Nvidia. Completion is subject to customary closing conditions, including regulatory clearance. For scale, <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/six-ai-raises-added-up-232m-for-ai-that.html">six AI funding rounds added up to $232 million in our October ledger</a>, so a single asset deal for one startup's software is about 32 times that entire ledger, which is the scale of a chip-strategy purchase, not a startup purchase.</p>
<h2>Why Trainium, why now</h2>
<p>Decart has worked with Amazon since 2025, delivering performance results on Trainium through a full-stack approach spanning software and hardware, according to Amazon. The technology will run through Annapurna Labs, Amazon's chip unit, and will be used to improve performance on Trainium and Inferentia, Amazon's custom AI chips. The timing is the tell: Amazon has so far used Trainium chips in its own data centers, and it is now preparing to sell them to customers as a competitor to Nvidia, according to TechCrunch. A chip nobody can easily move workloads onto is a chip nobody buys, and Decart's software is the portability layer that makes the move easy. In its latest quarterly update on October 30, Amazon said revenue commitments for its Graviton and Trainium chips had each increased more than 20-fold year over year. The demand is already there. What was missing was the software that lets a customer leave Nvidia without rewriting everything.</p>
<p>That is also why the deal belongs in the same frame as the fight over who controls the AI stack. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/who-controls-open-ai-stack-hugging-face.html">We mapped that control fight in November: whoever owns the hub, the chips, and the distribution decides what everyone else can build</a>. Amazon already owns the distribution through AWS. What it lacked was the optimization layer that makes its own silicon a credible alternative, and Decart is the purchase that fills the gap. Nvidia's moat was never just the chip; it was CUDA, the software that made the chip easy to use. Amazon just bought a piece of the answer to that.</p>
<h2>Asset deal, not acqui-hire</h2>
<p>It is worth naming the structure correctly, because the two get confused. An acqui-hire buys a team and winds the product down. An asset deal buys a specific technology and the team that built it, and leaves the rest of the company standing. Amazon's deal is the second kind: the Decart Optimization Stack and its builders move to Amazon, while the models and whatever remains of Decart stay behind. The distinction matters for anyone reading the price. Amazon is not paying $7.5 billion for 100 people. It is paying $7.5 billion for a software layer that, if it works as advertised, turns Trainium from a chip Amazon uses into a chip Amazon sells, with the people who know how it works included in the price. Whether that arithmetic holds depends on one thing the announcement does not answer: how much faster Decart's stack actually makes Trainium on real customer workloads, a number Amazon has not published.</p>
