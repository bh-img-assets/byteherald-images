<p><a href="https://www.reuters.com/business/anthropic-breaks-below-83-ipo-price-debut-rally-unravels-2026-11-24/">Anthropic shares fell below their $83 IPO price on Tuesday, November 24, 2026, trading as low as $75.61, down nearly 14 percent from Monday's close</a>. Four trading days after debuting at $111.50, the biggest IPO in history has given up every dollar of its first-day gain and then some. The stock missed Monday's Nvidia-led rally entirely, then fell again on Tuesday while Nvidia gave back more than 7 percent by midday. The round trip took four days; the reasons for it were in the IPO's own paperwork.</p>

<h2>The round trip, in four days</h2>
<p>Anthropic priced its IPO at $83 a share and debuted on Nasdaq on Thursday, November 19, 2026. The first trade was $111.50, 34 percent above the IPO price, and the stock reached $115.10 on its opening day. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/anthropics-ipo-pop-is-gone-nasdaq-lost.html">By Friday the pop was already gone: our November 20 post recorded a Thursday close of $97.17 and a Friday low of $83.05, five cents above the IPO price</a>. Monday brought the Nvidia rally, when Jensen Huang's reinstatement sent the chipmaker up more than 30 percent, and Anthropic closed at $87.64, missing the rally. On Tuesday the stock broke below $83 in premarket trading, was last down 6.5 percent at $82 in the early session, and fell as low as $75.61 by midday, down nearly 14 percent from Monday's close.</p>

<p>The valuation math checks out against our own earlier reporting. Reuters reported that the fully diluted valuation briefly topped $2 trillion at the open. Our November 22 post counted the seven founders at about $141 billion combined at the $83 IPO price, about 1.3 percent each, on 240 to 244 million shares apiece. Seven founders at 1.3 percent each is 9.1 percent of the company, which implies roughly 18.6 billion shares outstanding. At the $111.50 open, that is about $2.07 trillion, matching Reuters. One figure does not fit and we are not using it: a secondary headline put the listing valuation at $119.5 billion, which cannot be reconciled with the founders' share count, so it stays out of this article.</p>
<h2>The supply overhang was in the paperwork</h2>
<p>The debut demand met immediate supply, and the supply was disclosed before the first trade. Anthropic's final prospectus made up to 315 million shares immediately eligible for sale, with further staggered releases tied to earnings announcements. The IPO's lock-up provisions allow certain employees and insiders to sell portions of their holdings from the first trading day, rather than after the usual months-long lock-up. Filings late on Monday showed the founders used that freedom at once: chief executive Dario Amodei and president Daniela Amodei each sold 3,378,378 shares at $83 to the IPO's underwriters, worth more than $280 million each. Those sales happened at the offering price rather than on the open market, but they still put stock in underwriters' hands a day after the debut, and the prospectus also disclosed $900.9 billion in compute obligations, the fixed cost the company must pay whether or not its revenue keeps growing.</p>
<p>That combination is what a falling market punishes first. A stock with 315 million shares free to sell on day one and nearly a trillion dollars of committed compute spending has no cushion when sentiment turns. The broader market gave it no cover either. The S and P 500 and the Dow ended last week in confirmed correction territory, and our November 21 scoreboard counted about $6 trillion of market value lost in 45 days. Anthropic is the most exposed name in that selloff because it is the newest: it has no earnings history as a public company, no dividend, and no track record for investors to fall back on when the price breaks.</p>
<h2>How fast is this, historically</h2>
<p>Falling below the IPO price is a familiar embarrassment, but doing it in four days is rare. The Wall Street Journal compared Anthropic with the biggest listings on record and found it the second-fastest to surrender its offer price among companies worth $75 billion or more on their first trading day. Facebook, now Meta, slipped below its offer price on its second trading day in 2012. SpaceX, the previous record IPO in June, took about a month to fall below its offer price. Anthropic took four trading days. Reuters notes the same caveat the Journal does: an early break below the offer price is not necessarily a guide to longer-term performance, and Facebook's history is the proof of that. The speed matters for a different reason. It measures how fast debut enthusiasm faded, and four days is unusually fast.</p>
<h2>What breaking below the IPO price means</h2>
<p>Underwriters typically price an offering at a discount to the value they expect the market to put on the stock, so the first trade is supposed to open higher. Falling below the offer price means the market values the company below the level its own bankers set as a discount. That is why TechCrunch calls the slide a worrying sign for AI startups: Anthropic is the first frontier AI lab to go public, and its performance is being read as a barometer for how public investors value the sector. If its shares keep trading poorly, the slide could put pressure on private startup valuations and weaken IPO prospects for other AI companies. The company raised $81.3 billion at pricing, or $93.5 billion after underwriters exercised their option to buy additional shares, the biggest IPO on record either way, ahead of SpaceX's June listing. The money is banked. The question now is what the market thinks the company is worth, and on Tuesday it thought less than $83 a share.</p>
<h2>The counterpoint</h2>
<p>Not every signal points down. Oppenheimer started coverage on Tuesday with an outperform rating and a $150 price target, roughly double the Tuesday low. Index funds may provide some support: index rebalance analytics firm Intropic estimates passive funds could buy up to 30 percent of the shares offered by year-end, and MSCI will add Anthropic to its global standard indexes as of the November 30 close. Index inclusion forces buying regardless of what active managers think, and it arrives within a week. Whether that buying is enough to hold the $83 line is the test for the rest of the month. The stock has now spent four days proving that debut demand was thinner than the first trade suggested. The next four days will show whether index demand is thicker.</p>
