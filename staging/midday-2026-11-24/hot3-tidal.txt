<p><a href="https://support.tidal.com/hc/en-us/articles/48031883413521-AI-Policy">Tidal will begin removing wholly AI-generated music on December 9, 2026, and expects about 29 million tracks to be gone from its catalog by the start of 2027</a>. The Block-owned streaming service announced the ban on Tuesday, November 24, escalating a policy that since June has only labeled AI-generated tracks and blocked them from earning royalties. From December 9, any wholly AI-generated track submitted to Tidal will be rejected, and tracks already in the catalog will be removed. Tidal is the first major streaming service to ban fully AI-generated music outright.</p>

<h2>The flood, in Tidal's own numbers</h2>
<p>Tidal's announcement is built on numbers the company has been collecting since it started labeling AI-generated tracks in July. Forty-three percent of all tracks distributed to Tidal over the past 90 days were detected as wholly AI-generated. About 27 million tracks in the catalog, 6 percent of everything Tidal carries, already carry the AI badge. Ninety-four percent of those badged tracks are attributed to Suno, the AI music generator. Ninety-six percent of AI-badged tracks got zero streams in the last 90 days. And about 66,000 AI tracks have already been taken down automatically for suspected artist-name impersonation. The ban adds two more numbers: an estimated 80,000 to 85,000 songs rejected every day once the policy takes effect, and about 29 million tracks deleted by the beginning of 2027.</p>

<p>The 27 million and 29 million figures look contradictory and are not. About 27 million tracks carry the AI badge today. About 29 million will be deleted by the start of the year, because the deletions include tracks detected between now and then plus the 80,000 to 85,000 songs rejected every day across the December 9 to December 31 window, roughly 1.8 million more. That reconciliation is ours, from Tidal's own figures; the company announced both numbers without explaining the gap. The 96 percent zero-stream figure is the one that explains the business logic. A catalog of 29 million tracks that almost nobody plays costs Tidal storage, moderation, and fraud review while earning nothing, and the tracks that do get played are disproportionately the ones impersonating real artists.</p>
<h2>The line Tidal drew</h2>
<p>The ban covers wholly AI-generated tracks only, and Tidal's own policy page is careful about what that word means. A wholly AI-generated recording is one generated entirely using generative AI. Because detection technology has limits and false positives are a real risk, Tidal says it will only take action on wholly AI-generated recordings for now, and will expand its actions to recordings that are substantially AI-generated as detection improves. It will take no action on AI-generated compositions. Tracks that were assisted by AI tools but are not the sole output of those tools will still be allowed. The detection comes from Ircam Amplify, the commercial arm of the French audio research institute IRCAM, plus metadata screening: a track is removed if its distributor discloses that it was made solely with generative AI, or if Tidal's own systems identify it as fully AI-generated. Artists who believe a track was wrongly identified can appeal through Tidal's support form, and listeners can already block AI-labeled recordings in their settings.</p>
<h2>From label to ban: the escalation</h2>
<p>December 9 is the third step in a policy Tidal has tightened every few months. In June 2026, Tidal introduced its AI policy: wholly AI-generated tracks were tagged in the app and blocked from earning royalties, with demonetization effective June 29. In July, the listener-facing AI badge and fraud removals followed, on July 15, and Tidal began excluding wholly AI-generated recordings from direct-to-fan sales. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/every-streaming-platforms-ai-music.html">Our October 14 scorecard compared six streaming platforms' AI music rules and found Tidal the strictest on royalties, while Deezer's flood chart showed AI tracks rising from 10 percent to 52 percent of uploads</a>. The December ban is the same policy with the last step added: the tracks do not stay up, unlabeled or labeled, paid or unpaid. They go. Tidal's lead, Robert Andersen, put it bluntly on X: "Bye AI music."</p>
<h2>What the other platforms do</h2>
<p>Tidal is now the outlier, and the comparison shows how far. Spotify allows music made with AI and says it will not down-rank tracks for disclosing it; since mid-September 2026, artist profiles whose public identity is AI-generated carry an AI Persona badge and are excluded by default from editorial and algorithmic recommendations, a rule about the artist's identity rather than how the music was made. Apple Music made its ai_transparencies metadata tag mandatory in August 2026 wherever AI generated a material portion of the content, with listener-facing Made With AI labels to follow later in 2026. Deezer detects and tags fully AI-generated tracks itself, removes them from algorithmic recommendations and editorial playlists, and since July 2026 deletes AI tracks tied to fraud or unplayed for six months. YouTube Music labels songs made primarily with generative AI, a policy that began October 14, 2026. Bandcamp has barred wholly AI-generated music since January 2026, written into its Acceptable Use Policy on June 10. Only Tidal and Bandcamp refuse to carry the music at all, and only Tidal is deleting 29 million tracks to prove it.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/every-forum-and-platform-that-banned-ai.html">That puts Tidal at the far end of a spectrum our October 31 pillar mapped across forums and platforms, from labeling to demonetization to outright bans</a>. Music is the first industry where a major platform has crossed from the middle of that spectrum to the far end, and the reason is the flood: no other platform reports 43 percent of its incoming supply as wholly AI-generated.</p>
<h2>Why Tidal can do this and Spotify cannot</h2>
<p>The ban costs Tidal almost nothing in listening, and that is the point. Ninety-six percent of its AI-badged tracks got zero streams in 90 days, so deleting them removes almost no listening and almost no royalty expense, since the tracks were already demonetized in June. What it removes is the fraud surface: 66,000 impersonation takedowns so far, and a catalog where fake artists can siphon streams meant for real ones. Spotify cannot copy the move at the same scale because nothing on Tidal's scale has been reported for Spotify, and its business is scale: it monetizes the long tail, including the AI part, and its answer is to hide AI identities from recommendations rather than delete the music. Tidal is small, artist-positioned, and owned by Block, and its pitch to artists is that human music is the product. The test of the ban is not whether 29 million tracks disappear. It is whether distributors keep sending 80,000 AI songs a day to a platform that now rejects them, or send them somewhere else. If they go elsewhere, Tidal's flood becomes Spotify's and Deezer's problem, and the ban becomes the first domino rather than the last word.</p>
