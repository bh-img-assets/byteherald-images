<p><a href="https://github.com/openai/math/tree/main/preprints/VP-and-VNP-are-different-November-23-2026">Anthropic posted a Claude-written proof on November 23, 2026, claiming that VP differs from VNP, the algebraic version of P versus NP that Leslie Valiant posed in 1979</a>. About five hours later, OpenAI posted its own proof of the same claim, saying its model had obtained the result on November 6, more than two weeks earlier. Neither proof has been checked by a human mathematician. Both labs published the machine-checked formalizations alongside the papers, so the mathematics community can now do the checking the labs did not.</p>

<h2>What VP and VNP are</h2>
<p>VP and VNP are classes of polynomials, not decision problems. VP is the class of polynomials that can be computed by small arithmetic circuits, circuits built from addition, subtraction, and multiplication gates. VNP is the class of polynomials whose terms are easy to describe even when there are exponentially many of them to add up. Valiant showed that the permanent, a close cousin of the determinant, is the hardest polynomial in VNP, so VP differing from VNP amounts to saying that no shortcut for computing the permanent will ever be found, even approximately. Both papers claim exactly that, over every field, and in the stronger border sense: even circuits that only approximate the permanent must be large. This is a 47-year-old conjecture, and it is not the P versus NP problem. Both labs say so plainly. The Clay Institute's $1 million prize for P versus NP remains unclaimed, and OpenAI's paper states that its result is not a separation for decision problems in the Boolean model.</p>
<h2>The two proofs, side by side</h2>
<p>The papers arrived within hours of each other and differ in almost every dimension except the claim. Anthropic posted first, at 20:04 UTC on November 23, a 70-page paper credited to Claude, with a Lean 4 formalization. Its README says the result was discovered by an internal research Claude model, that the paper and the formalization were written by AI models, and that no human mathematician has checked the proof. OpenAI followed at about 01:20 UTC on November 24, a 30-page paper credited to OpenAI, in the November 23 edition, with its own Lean formalization. Its changelog says the result was obtained on November 6. Its README describes the manuscripts as produced by an unnamed internal OpenAI model and best understood as raw research data rather than polished academic papers, and it defers to the mathematics community completely on matters of credit, precedence, and concurrent work.</p>

<p>The bounds are not directly comparable, and neither lab pretends they are. Anthropic's bound is stated in terms of gates for an m by m permanent. OpenAI's is stated in terms of n, the size of the explicit VNP family, and its paper gives the stronger border-circuit bound while its Lean formalization covers the narrower exact-circuit case. A reader who wants to compare them has to do the conversion work, and no outside mathematician has published that comparison yet. What can be compared is the size of the machine-checked evidence: OpenAI's Lean folder holds more than 3,100 files and roughly 440,000 lines, about six times the size of Anthropic's, and 442,736 lines were added in the single commit that merged the manuscript.</p>
<h2>What Lean checked, and what it did not</h2>
<p>The formalizations are the most important part of both deposits, and they are also the part most easily misunderstood. A Lean proof is a machine check that a formal statement follows from stated axioms. It is not a check that the formal statement says what the paper claims it says. Anthropic's README is explicit about this: Lean checks the proofs, but no machine checks that the file says what is claimed, and experts still have to compare the Lean statement file with the paper's appendix. OpenAI's deposit has the same gap in a different place: its Lean proof covers the separation for exact arithmetic circuits, while the paper claims the stronger bound for border circuits, which approximate the permanent rather than computing it exactly. The stronger claim sits in the paper; it is not the part Lean has checked. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/two-riemann-proofs-in-one-week.html">The same question decided the Riemann proofs a week earlier, when Lean and Mathlib certification arrived on November 21 and 22 and the claims moved from announced to machine-checked</a>. For VP and VNP, the machine check exists for the narrower statements, and the human check of whether the statements match the claims has not happened yet.</p>
<h2>The precedence question</h2>
<p>OpenAI obtained its result on November 6 and posted it five hours after Anthropic. Anthropic posted first and does not say when it obtained its result. That asymmetry is the whole precedence dispute, and both labs handled it differently. OpenAI dated its result in its changelog, a deliberate precedence marker, and then deferred the credit question to the mathematics community in its README. Anthropic said nothing about when its model found the result and said nothing about OpenAI's. The race is now about verification, not posting order: both labs published Lean files, and outside review of both papers is still to come. New Scientist's Matthew Sparkes collected outside reaction on November 24 and found it strong and split, which is what an unverified claim of this size should produce. The labs' own language invites that split. Anthropic calls its deposit a proof; OpenAI calls its manuscripts raw research data. Neither description settles anything.</p>
<h2>The October lineage</h2>
<p>Neither proof came from nowhere. In the last week of October 2026, both labs posted AI-written papers claiming the Exponential Time Hypothesis is false, by giving a subexponential algorithm for 3-SAT, the classic hard satisfiability problem. OpenAI's VP and VNP paper adapts a grid method from the Claude paper that refuted the Exponential Time Hypothesis, so the new proof partly builds on a technique Claude discovered. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/openai-claims-three-science.html">Our October 27 ledger audited OpenAI's three claimed breakthroughs from that same week, local smoothing, Kakeya and Stein, the ETH refutation, and the QCD and Yang-Mills existence claim, and found the math and computer science formalizations public while the physics artifacts were withheld</a>. The pattern is now established: the labs deposit AI-written mathematics in public repositories, with Lean formalizations, and the verification happens in public afterward. The VP and VNP deposits are the largest test of that pattern yet, because the claim is the oldest and the two labs made it on the same night.</p>
<h2>What would settle it</h2>
<p>Three things, in order. First, outside mathematicians read both papers and say whether the arguments hold, starting with the grid method both papers share. Second, someone compares Anthropic's Lean statement file with its paper's appendix, and OpenAI's Lean exact-circuit proof with its paper's border-circuit claim, and reports whether the formalizations prove what the papers say they prove. Third, the two bounds get converted to a common scale so the community can say which proof is stronger and whether either is complete. Until then, the honest description is the one both labs' own documents support: two AI-written claims of a 47-year-old result, posted five hours apart, machine-checked in their narrower forms, and not yet checked by any human mathematician.</p>
