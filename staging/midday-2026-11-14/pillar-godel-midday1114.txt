<p>In September 2026, a Finnish logician handed Anthropic's Claude the transcriptions of 1,009 pages of Kurt Gödel's unpublished notebooks, written in an obsolete shorthand in the early 1940s. Within days the model returned a 650-page explanation and a list of 663 theorems. About 400 of them passed a computer proof checker exactly as stated. About 200 needed repair. About 50 were false. The numbers are the story, and so is what they do not settle.</p>

<h2>The notebooks, and the man who spent nine years reading them</h2>
<p>Kurt Gödel's two incompleteness theorems, published in 1931, changed mathematics and fed into the ideas behind computing. After the 1930s he published little, but he left tens of thousands of pages of unpublished work. Since 2017, the Finnish logician Jan von Plato has led a project at the University of Helsinki on 16 notebooks Gödel wrote in the early 1940s, known as the workbooks. They contain 1,009 pages of mathematics and logic, according to the figure von Plato gave The Guardian, much of it written in Gabelsberger, a German shorthand that was already obsolete when Gödel used it. <a href="https://www.helsinki.fi/en/researchgroups/godel-enigma/project-description">The Helsinki project's own page</a> describes the same 16-notebook series, the Arbeitshefte, as some 1,200 shorthand pages. The two counts come from different tallies, and this article uses the Guardian's 1,009 for the material Claude read.</p>
<p>Von Plato taught himself to read the shorthand, and says he is one of only six people who can. Progress was slow: about a page a week. That is the baseline the AI result should be compared with. The project, funded by a 2.5 million euro European Research Council grant, aims to obtain a full picture of Gödel's role in the birth of the information society and to exploit his hidden discoveries, including what <a href="https://www.helsinki.fi/en/researchgroups/godel-enigma/project-description">the project page calls</a> "his 1942 proof of independence of the axiom of choice." Nine years of one page a week is what stood between the notebooks and the world. Claude crossed it in days.</p>
<h2>What Claude was asked to do</h2>
<p>In August 2026, von Plato attended a conference in France on AI and theorem proving, where computer scientists suggested he try Claude. Back in Helsinki in September, he showed the model his transcriptions of the workbooks, six books he had written about Gödel, and other secondary literature. Within a couple of days he saw a 650-page explanation of the material. He told The Guardian it had to be controlled page for page, and that it is "basically a work that is impossible to do for human beings," because people read one page at a time and forget parts. His argument is about scale: the model can hold the connections across a thousand pages at once.</p>
<p>This is long-context reading used as a research assistant, not a new proof engine. The model did not read Gödel's shorthand directly. It worked from von Plato's transcriptions, plus his books and other literature. That distinction matters, because every claim that follows rests on two layers of human work first: the transcription, and the checking that came after.</p>
<h2>The audit: 663 theorems, three buckets</h2>
<p>Von Plato then asked Claude to extract every theorem in the workbooks where Gödel had a proof, a sketch of one, or an idea for one. The model found 663. After the formal check, von Plato reported three buckets, as <a href="https://explainx.ai/blog/claude-godel-notebooks-von-plato-663-theorems-coq-what-is-verified-2026">the claims audit by ExplainX, working from The Guardian's report</a>, sets out in full.</p>

<p>Two honesty notes belong in the table's shadow. First, the three figures are von Plato's rounded estimates, and they add to about 650, not 663. That is what rounding looks like when the underlying counts are approximate, and it is not a discrepancy to hide. Second, the false bucket is not a surprise. Von Plato notes that Gödel himself sometimes wrote "falsch!" or "schlecht!" in the margins when rereading his own work. A notebook is a place to think, and thinking includes being wrong. The notebooks also contain dozens of anticipations of results not known until the 1970s and 1980s, and 14 themes in logic that have not been pursued, according to the Guardian report. Those 14 themes are the part of the story that has nothing to do with Claude at all: they are new research directions, sitting in a drawer since the 1940s.</p>
<h2>The axiom-of-choice near miss</h2>
<p>The first task von Plato gave Claude was historical. In 1966, Paul Cohen won the Fields Medal for showing that the continuum hypothesis and the axiom of choice are independent of standard set theory. Gödel reacted by saying he had proved the result in the 1940s. When peers pushed for the proof, he could not reconstruct it from his notes. Von Plato asked whether the workbooks held a proof of the independence of the axiom of choice. The answer, as he reports it, was yes, almost. One definition was wrong, but Gödel had used the correct definition two years earlier. Von Plato ran the reconstructed proof through Coq, a program that checks mathematical proofs, and it passed.</p>
<div class="separator" style="clear: both; text-align: center;"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bh-img-assets/byteherald-images/main/img/pillar-godel-midday1114-chart2.png" alt="Timeline: 1931 incompleteness theorems, 1942 workbooks written, 1966 Cohen wins the Fields Medal, 2017 Helsinki project begins, September 2026 Claude reads 1,009 pages, November 13, 2026, 663 theorems reported" /></div>
<p>Von Plato believes that, had Gödel published it, he would have won the 1940 Fields Medal. That is an opinion, not a verified fact, and it should be read as one. Joel David Hamkins, a logic professor at the University of Notre Dame, has not seen Claude's reconstruction. He says that if it stands up, it is "remarkable and historically interesting," and adds that how much comes from Claude and how much from Gödel is a job for historians. He also draws a line on credit. Resemblance to Cohen's proof, he says, shows Gödel was on the path, not that he had it. "You don't get credit for work you don't tell anyone about," in his words. That caution is the right frame for the headline. Hundreds of proved theorems describes what the notebooks are said to contain, and the open question is which parts are Gödel's ideas and which are Claude's repair work.</p>
<h2>The verification ladder: what Coq proves and what it does not</h2>
<p>The reason this story deserves more trust than most AI-for-science announcements is the ladder it climbs. Each rung does a different job, and no rung does the next rung's job.</p>
<table>
<tr><th>Rung</th><th>Who does it</th><th>What it establishes</th><th>What it cannot establish</th></tr>
<tr><td>Extract</td><td>Claude, from von Plato's transcriptions</td><td>A list of 663 theorem candidates</td><td>Whether any of them are true</td></tr>
<tr><td>Repair</td><td>Von Plato, by hand</td><td>Precise statements with the assumptions made explicit</td><td>Whether Gödel meant the repaired version</td></tr>
<tr><td>Check</td><td>Coq, the proof assistant</td><td>Whether the formal statement follows from its premises</td><td>Whether Gödel believed it, or proved it first</td></tr>
<tr><td>Judge</td><td>Historians of mathematics</td><td>Whose work it is, and what it changes about Gödel's legacy</td><td>Nothing yet, because the work is not published</td></tr>
</table>
<p>Coq verifies the formal statement, not the history. The 50 false theorems are the proof the checker earns its place: without it, the false items would sit in the list looking exactly the same as the 400 true ones. That is the difference between this project and the AI proof announcements that drew a backlash from mathematicians earlier this year. When OpenAI's AI wrote 722 mathematics manuscripts in a single dump, the response from the field was a boycott call, because the volume arrived without a checking step anyone could trust: <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/mathematicians-vs-machines-722-proof.html">our account of the 722-proof revolt</a> has the full story. The Gödel project is the counter-case. It pairs the generator with a checker, and it reports three buckets instead of one accuracy figure, which is why the 400 verified theorems are believable in a way a bare headline count would not be.</p>
<h2>What is still unknown, and what would settle it</h2>
<p>No paper or data release has been reported, so outside logicians cannot rerun the extraction. The 650-page digest has been checked by von Plato, but not by independent reviewers that we know of. The Guardian report does not name the Claude model version or give the cost. The Coq results cover the formal statements. They do not settle whether Gödel held the proof in his head. Further notebooks, including physics and cosmology, are planned, and von Plato says he will start those after his current logic projects.</p>
<p>The test that would settle the open questions is simple to state and hard to do: publish the Coq files and the 650-page digest, so outside logicians can check the extraction, the repairs, and the verification for themselves. Until that happens, the right way to describe the result is the way this article has described it throughout: von Plato's figures, as reported by The Guardian, checked by one man and one machine, and not yet by the field. That is still a remarkable result. It is not yet a settled one, and the difference between those two things is the whole point of the audit.</p>
<h2>Why this is the right way to use AI on science</h2>
<p>Four practices made this project credible, and they are worth copying. First, pair the generator with a checker. The strongest part of this story is the Coq step. The model proposes, the proof assistant disposes, and the same shape works for code and tests. Second, give the model your own context. Von Plato supplied his transcriptions and six books, not just the scans, and domain material changed the output from a summary into a research digest. Third, review at the page level. He controlled the digest page for page, and long outputs need a human pass. Fourth, report three buckets. Verified, repaired, and false is more honest than one accuracy figure, and it is the same discipline we applied when we audited OpenAI's claimed breakthroughs one by one: <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/openai-claims-three-science.html">our verification ledger for OpenAI's three claimed breakthroughs</a> treated each claim the same way, and found the physics artifacts withheld while the mathematics was public.</p>
<p>The deeper point is about what the machine actually did. It did not discover mathematics. It recovered mathematics Gödel had already worked out and buried in a code almost no one alive can read. That is a smaller claim than the headlines suggest, and a more important one. A thousand pages of unread notebooks is not a curiosity. It is a backlog, and backlogs like it exist in every field with a long paper trail: letters, lab books, field notes, and drafts that no living expert has time to read. The Gödel project shows what happens when a model reads the backlog and a checker sorts the result. The theorems were there all along. Someone finally read them.</p>
