<p>Anthropic says its Claude Mythos Preview model got Claude running on AMD GPUs in about three days, with basically no human intervention, porting or writing almost 100 percent of the kernels. The claim comes from the company's IPO roadshow video, released November 10, 2026, and reported by Tom's Hardware on November 16. The footnote on Anthropic's own slide narrows the claim, and the narrowing is the story.</p>
<div class="separator" style="clear: both; text-align: center;"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bh-img-assets/byteherald-images/main/img/hot2-mythos-hero.png" alt="Editorial graphic: the headline Mythos Moved Claude to AMD in 3 Days, November 2026" /></div>
<h2>What Anthropic actually said</h2>
<p>The claim comes from Tom Brown, Anthropic's co-founder and chief compute officer, in the compute chapter of the 42-minute roadshow video. Anthropic started using AMD GPUs for the first time this year, Brown said, and bringing up a new chip platform used to take dozens of engineers months. "This time we had Mythos try it out," he said. "It got Claude running on AMD in just three days with basically no human intervention. Claude ported or wrote almost 100 percent of the kernels." Kernels are the small, heavily tuned programs that do a model's math on one specific chip. They are the code that used to chain a model to Nvidia's CUDA software platform, and rewriting them for a new chip is the slow, specialist work that kept labs locked to one vendor.</p>
<p>HSBC analysts flagged the line to clients during Nvidia's earnings week, and the market heard it the same way. If porting takes days instead of months, the chip underneath a model starts to look interchangeable, and Nvidia's software moat starts to look thinner. That is the claim. Here is the footnote.</p>
<h2>The claim vs the footnote</h2>
<div class="separator" style="clear: both; text-align: center;"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bh-img-assets/byteherald-images/main/img/hot2-mythos-chart.png" alt="Chart: the Mythos claim versus the footnote, four rows covering time, scope, baseline, and proof" /></div>
<p>An on-screen footnote in the roadshow video limits the three days to bringing up prompt processing, the stage where a model reads its input before answering, for one Anthropic model on AMD's MI355X GPUs, at what the company calls a useful level of performance. Prompt processing is one part of running a model, not all of it. Tom's Hardware notes it is unclear whether response generation, the stage where the model produces its answer, was ported too, and how the model's speed on AMD compares with Nvidia. The company has not named which model was ported, and it has not published the kernels, the code, or any throughput or latency numbers. Nobody outside the company has independently verified the claim.</p>
<p>The six to twelve month comparison is also Anthropic's own account of its past experience, not a measured benchmark. It is an estimate drawn from the company's history of bringing up new accelerators, and the comparison was made in a video aimed at IPO investors. Anthropic's own SEC prospectus, filed November 12, 2026, makes the same point in quieter language, saying only that its teams can now bring new chip platforms into production more quickly, with no number attached. A claim made to sell shares deserves a harder look than a claim made to a peer reviewer, and this one has not yet had either.</p>
<h2>The counterpoint: OpenAI is doing the same thing</h2>
<p>Anthropic is not the only lab reporting this direction. OpenAI said on November 18 and 19, 2026, that its models found a year's worth of AMD GPU design gains in under five months, and that its models improved AMD's flagship GPU design by up to 14 percent. Taken together, the two claims point the same way: frontier models are now doing the low-level chip work that used to take specialist teams months. But OpenAI's claim is also company-reported, with no published designs or code, and neither company has published the designs or the code behind its claim. Two sales pitches do not add up to a benchmark. If a second company confirms a porting timeline measured in days with published code, the three-day claim stops being a sales pitch and becomes the new going rate. Until then it is a signal of direction, not a result.</p>
<h2>Why it matters even if the number is soft</h2>
<p>The reason the claim traveled so far is that the prize is real. Anthropic now trains and serves Claude across four chip families, Amazon's Trainium, Google's TPUs, Nvidia's GPUs, and AMD's GPUs, and it treats them as one fungible pool, matching each chip to the job it does best. Its first AMD MI450 chips are expected in the first half of 2027. If kernel porting really does take days, a lab can move work between vendors as prices and supply change, and the leverage shifts from the chipmaker to the lab. That is the prize, and it is why the footnote matters as much as the headline. A three-day port of one model's prompt processing is a demonstration. A three-day port of a full training stack, with published numbers, would be a break in the moat.</p>
<p>This is the next claim for the scoreboard we built on November 19, when <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/ai-is-designing-chips-now-every.html">we scored every verified AI chip-design result so far</a>. Mythos belongs on that board, but as an unverified vendor claim, not as a measured result, and the board will stay honest only if the two stay separate.</p>
<h2>What would settle it</h2>
<p>Three things would turn the claim into a result. First, publish the kernels, so anyone can run them and check the work. Second, publish throughput and latency on the MI355X against the same model on Nvidia hardware, so the "useful level of performance" has a number. Third, let an independent team repeat the port on a different model and a different AMD chip. Until one of those happens, the honest summary is short: Anthropic says its model ported a model's prompt processing to AMD in three days, the footnote says the scope was narrow, and no one outside the company has checked. That is a story worth reporting, and a claim worth waiting on.</p>
