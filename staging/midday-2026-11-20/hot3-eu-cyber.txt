<p>The European Commission confirmed on Thursday, November 19, 2026, that a cyberattack using artificial intelligence targeted the European Union in October, the first time the 27-member bloc has reported a cyberattack using an AI tool. The attack was detected on October 22, ran for at least 10 days, and left some of the bloc's digital tools still offline almost a month later.</p>

<h2>What happened</h2>
<p>Cybersecurity experts have been dealing with the attack for almost a month, an official confirmed on Thursday, with some of the bloc's digital tools still offline. The official did not say who could have been behind it. European Commission spokesperson Thomas Regnier said the EU first detected the incident on October 22 and launched an investigation while imposing preventative measures. Politico first reported the incident and said the attack lasted at least 10 days and affected translation and reimbursement tools as well as databases used for salaries and timesheets. Regnier declined to confirm that report.</p>
<div class="separator" style="clear: both; text-align: center;"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bh-img-assets/byteherald-images/main/img/hot3-eu-cyber-chart.png" alt="Chart: timeline from the October 22 detection to the November 4 ARTEX warning to the November 19 confirmation, 28 days" /></div>
<p>"We temporarily took down several applications, and most of them have since then been restored," Regnier said. So far, he added, "we have not detected any evidence of a data breach affecting commission staff as a whole." The attack targeted the headquarters of the EU's executive branch. No similar incidents were reported by the EU's two other main institutions, the European Council and the European Parliament. The EU agency CERT-EU oversees the digital security of the bloc's various institutions, which together employ about 40,000 people just in Brussels.</p>
<h2>The warning came first</h2>
<p>The confirmation landed two weeks after the warning. On November 4, CERT-EU posted a blog warning about threats from a specific tool called ARTEX. While designed to use AI to identify vulnerabilities in digital systems, ARTEX has been linked to recent attacks across the globe. "We assess that ARTEX's public availability will likely accelerate adoption by threat actors, resulting in more frequent, faster, and more scalable automated attacks with a higher success rate," the office said. The October attack on the Commission is the first confirmed case to follow that warning, and it arrived fast: the warning went up on November 4, and the confirmation came 15 days later.</p>
<p>We have tracked ARTEX since it surfaced. On November 6 we reported that <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/artex-815-ai-hacking-tools-left-open-to.html">815 AI hacking tools were left open to the internet</a>, and on November 16 we reported that <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/fbi-artex-ai-tool-can-breach-network-in.htm">the FBI warned the Artex AI tool can breach a network in under 3 hours</a>. The EU attack does not prove ARTEX was the tool used: the Commission has not named one, and the official would not say who was behind the attack. What it proves is the pattern CERT-EU warned about, that an AI vulnerability-finding tool in public hands gets used, and that the first confirmed institutional target was the EU itself.</p>
<h2>What AI-assisted means here</h2>
<p>Regnier's own words are the clearest guide to what happened. "AI-assisted tools can be used and especially misused to detect vulnerabilities," he said. "Indeed we can confirm that this was done with AI tools." That is a narrower claim than it sounds, and the distinction matters. An AI tool that finds a weakness is not the same as an AI that runs the whole attack. The Commission has confirmed the first part, that AI tools were used to find or exploit vulnerabilities. It has not said the attack was autonomous, and it has not named the tool. The honest reading is that AI did the scouting, and humans, or human-run automation, did the rest. That is still a first: no EU institution had previously reported a cyberattack using an AI tool at all.</p>
<p>The gap between scouting and full automation is where the next warning will come from. ARTEX is designed to identify vulnerabilities, and the FBI's warning was about speed: under 3 hours to breach a network. The EU attack ran for at least 10 days, which suggests a longer operation than a single automated breach, and the Commission's own response, taking applications down and restoring them one by one, suggests the damage was contained to availability, not data. "We have seen many global cyberattacks recently, and of course no one is immune to this," Regnier said. The sentence reads like a shrug. It is also the most honest summary of where institutional cybersecurity now stands.</p>
<h2>What to watch</h2>
<p>Three things will decide how big this becomes. The first is attribution: whether the EU names a culprit, which it has not done, and whether the attack is linked to ARTEX or to a different tool. The second is scope: whether the European Council or the European Parliament report similar incidents, which they have not, and whether the staff-wide data breach the Commission says it has not found stays unfound. The third is guidance: whether CERT-EU turns its November 4 warning into new rules for EU institutions, now that the warning has a confirmed case behind it. Until then, the ledger stands at one confirmed AI-assisted attack, one named tool in the warning, and no named attacker.</p>
