<p>Two claimed proofs of the Riemann Hypothesis landed in the same week. One comes from Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, who says he found it working with Claude. The other comes from Alessandro Masullo, a Stanford postdoc, who says he found it alone over twelve years. Neither proof is verified yet. This is the ledger of what is claimed, what is checked, and what is still open.</p>

<h2>The two claims, side by side</h2>
<p>The Riemann Hypothesis, proposed by Bernhard Riemann in 1859, conjectures a hidden structure behind the appearance of prime numbers. It is one of the Clay Mathematics Institute's seven Millennium Prize Problems, each carrying a 1 million dollar award, and the most famous unsolved problem in mathematics. Two people now say they have solved it, or its generalization, in the same week. The table below is the whole story in one place.</p>

<p>The contrast is the point. One claim was built in six weeks with an AI model, and its evidence is machine code no human has read in full. The other was built over twelve years by one person, and its evidence is a draft its author wanted a year to polish. Masullo wrote in his introduction that if his work sends any message to "the mathematician who feels discouraged by the pace and tone of the current climate, it is this: we are still here. We are very much alive. We are not going anywhere." Vakil posted the draft with the note that "it is all human, with no AI." The two proofs are not rivals in the usual sense. They are two different answers to the same question: who gets to prove the great theorems now, and how.</p>
<h2>What Alpöge actually did</h2>
<p>Alpöge posted a four-page note on his own website on November 20, 2026. "I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis," he wrote. "In fact, the method easily extends much further, resolving the so-called Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, and I expect, with more time, the Extended Riemann Hypothesis too. Claude and I have also formalized the arguments without assumptions." The note is the only readable account so far. The proof itself exists only as Lean code, in 14 archive parts on his site, and Alpöge writes that he decided to only present Lean, because the full written argument would run to more than 250,000 pages.</p>
<div class="separator" style="clear: both; text-align: center;"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bh-img-assets/byteherald-images/main/img/pillar-riemann-chart1.png" alt="Chart: timeline of Alpöge's proof from the October 6 Stepanov determinant to the November 20 announcements" /></div>
<p>The method, as Alpöge describes it, turns on an old idea used in a new way. Stepanov's argument, which resolved the Riemann Hypothesis over function fields, uses a divisor involving multiplicity. Multiplicity is hard to find over the integers, because it corresponds to something like differentiating whole numbers. Alpöge's line, the one he says will make fellow mathematicians fall out of their chairs, is that chaining theta lifts can mimic multiplicity, and that for a von Mangoldt sum of length X, Claude's suggested length of chain was the square root of X. He guided the work to the right setting, he writes, and then Claude "flew, and danced, and sparkled near that finish line." He is candid about the limits of his own grasp: he writes that he has not mastered the neighbourhood around the argument, that he may have baked strange and suboptimal choices into it, and that he expects massive simplification, likely immediately. He also writes that the timing is horrible, that he messaged Anthropic chief executive Dario Amodei late on Sunday night to ask how to announce it, and that he felt he could not live if he waited even longer. Alpöge and NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster also released a proof related to the Navier-Stokes equations alongside it.</p>
<h2>How a Lean proof gets checked</h2>
<p>Lean is a proof assistant: a language in which a theorem and its proof are written formally, and a kernel then checks every step mechanically. A Lean proof that compiles is a proof whose logic has been machine-checked. That is a stronger test than asking a person, or another AI, whether an argument looks convincing. But it only checks the statement it is given. The four steps below are the whole verification path, and each one has an open question.</p>
<div class="separator" style="clear: both; text-align: center;"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bh-img-assets/byteherald-images/main/img/pillar-riemann-chart2.png" alt="Chart: four steps of checking a Lean proof, from stating the claim to humans reading the argument" /></div>
<p>The first outside check came quickly. MIT mathematician Andrew Sutherland independently compiled Alpöge's result in Lean and called it "an historic moment," according to Scientific American. Mathematician Elliot Glazer posted on X that the formalized statement in Alpöge's drop is in fact the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, and put the chance of a bug in Lean itself at under 1 percent. That addresses one item on the list, that the theorem in the code matches the famous problem, but it does not settle the others. The full compilation was still running on Friday afternoon and was expected to take hours, and mathematicians were going through the code on the Lean community's Zulip forum. Chris Birbeck, a number theorist at the University of East Anglia, posted a detailed reading of the cohomology bound, and twenty readers reacted with the mind-blown emoji. TechCrunch reported that most in the discussion appear confident the formalized Lean result will hold. Confidence is not verification. The proof is claimed, not verified, and the honest status is exactly that.</p>
<h2>Why the 1 million dollar prize is not in play yet</h2>
<p>The Riemann Hypothesis carries a 1 million dollar Millennium Prize from the Clay Mathematics Institute, but the prize has rules, and neither claim meets them yet. A personal website post and a code archive do not start the clock.</p>
<table><thead><tr><th>Prize rule</th><th>What it requires</th><th>Where the claims stand</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Publication</td><td>A qualifying peer-reviewed outlet</td><td>Neither claim is published; Alpöge posted Lean code, Masullo posted a draft</td></tr><tr><td>Waiting period</td><td>At least two years after publication</td><td>The clock has not started for either claim</td></tr><tr><td>Acceptance</td><td>General acceptance in the math community</td><td>Neither claim has it yet</td></tr><tr><td>Prize</td><td>1 million dollars</td><td>Not in play</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>Clay's own Riemann Hypothesis page still listed the problem as unsolved as of November 20, 2026. The only Millennium Prize problem ever declared solved is the Poincaré conjecture, awarded to Grigori Perelman in 2010. A Lean archive, however large, is not a peer-reviewed publication, and a draft posted to move fast is not general acceptance. Both claims have years of scrutiny ahead of them before the prize is even a question.</p>
<h2>The five-question test for any AI math claim</h2>
<p>The claims will keep coming, and the test for judging them is now stable enough to write down. We used the same method on October 27, when <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/openai-claims-three-science.html">we audited OpenAI's three claimed science breakthroughs claim by claim</a>, and it applies here without change. Ask these five questions of any AI math claim, in order.</p>
<p>First, is the claim published as code, a paper, or both? Alpöge published code only, no paper. Masullo published a draft paper only, no code. A claim with neither is a rumor; a claim with one is half a claim. Second, has anyone outside the authors compiled or checked it? Sutherland compiled Alpöge's code, which is the strongest single check so far, and the full compilation was still running. Nobody has reported checking Masullo's draft. Third, does the formal statement match the famous problem? This is the most common failure in formalization, and it is the one Glazer's post addresses for Alpöge. For Masullo, the draft can be read directly, so the statement is visible. Fourth, is there a readable argument, or only machine code? Alpöge has no readable argument yet and says so himself. Masullo has a draft he wanted a year to polish. Fifth, what would falsify it, and who is trying? For Alpöge, a failed compilation, a wrong formal statement, or a flaw found on Zulip would falsify it, and the Zulip discussion is where that trying is happening in public. For Masullo, falsification is slower: peer reading over months, not a compiler run over hours. A claim that passes all five is a result. Neither claim has passed all five yet.</p>
<h2>The history says: wait</h2>
<p>The mathematics community has been here before, and the last time it ended in embarrassment for the claimant. In September 2018, Michael Atiyah presented a claimed proof of the Riemann Hypothesis at the Heidelberg Laureate Forum. Mathematicians found gaps, and the claim was not accepted. That is the most famous recent false alarm, and it is why the community's first instinct is to check the code before celebrating the headline. The skepticism is not hostility to AI. It is the same skepticism the community showed this summer, when the race between frontier labs and mathematicians to solve outstanding problems produced both real results and real backlash, a story we covered in <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/mathematicians-vs-machines-722-proof.html">the 722-proof revolt</a>, in which mathematicians pushed back against AI-generated proofs and the systems that produce them. The revolt was not about whether AI can do mathematics. It was about whether mathematics done at machine speed, in machine code, still counts as mathematics anyone can understand. Alpöge's own note is, in its way, an answer to that revolt: he released the code, admitted he does not yet understand all of it, and committed to writing it up properly. That honesty is the strongest thing in his claim.</p>
<h2>What happens next</h2>
<p>Four things will decide this story. The Lean compilation will finish, and the community will learn whether the code compiles cleanly and whether the statement it proves is the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis. The Zulip discussion will either find a flaw or fail to find one, and the failure to find one, sustained over weeks, is itself evidence. Alpöge will write the readable paper he has promised, and the simplifications he expects will either arrive or not. Masullo's draft will be read, and the year of polishing he wanted will either confirm the argument or expose its gaps. We will update this ledger as the verification proceeds, and we will correct it if any claim in it is shown to be wrong. Until then, the ledger stands: two claims, one week, zero verified proofs, and the most interesting question in mathematics suddenly moving at the speed of software.</p>
