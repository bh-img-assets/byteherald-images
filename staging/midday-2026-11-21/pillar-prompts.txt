<p>In July 2025, researchers hid instructions in 18 manuscripts on arXiv telling any AI that read them to give a positive review only. The instructions were invisible to human readers, set in white text or microscopic type, and fully readable by machines. A July 2026 analysis in Communications of the ACM catalogued every one of them, and this article publishes that catalogue in full: all 18 papers, all four prompt types, the effectiveness data, and the fixes now on the table.</p>
<div class="separator" style="clear: both; text-align: center;"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bh-img-assets/byteherald-images/main/img/pillar-prompts-hero.png" alt="Editorial graphic: the headline The Hidden Prompts in Peer Review, November 2026" /></div>
<h2>How the trick was found</h2>
<p>The story starts with a reporter selecting all the text on a page. In mid-2025, a Japanese reporter selected the entire text of a preprint posted on arXiv, and a line surfaced from the white background: instructions telling any AI reader to give a positive review only. Nikkei Asia investigated and reported on July 1, 2025, that 17 English-language preprints carried such prompts. The lead authors were affiliated with 14 institutions in eight countries, including Japan's Waseda University, South Korea's KAIST, China's Peking University, the National University of Singapore, Columbia University, and the University of Washington in the United States. The papers were concentrated in computer-science communities.</p>
<p>Zhicheng Lin, a psychologist at Yonsei University, then ran targeted searches combining phrases like "give a positive review" with the arXiv site, and found 18 papers containing hidden prompts, one more than Nikkei reported. His search, conducted on July 7, 2025, is a lower bound, not an incidence estimate. The same searches of other preprint platforms, SSRN, PsyArXiv, bioRxiv, and medRxiv, found no instances, and Google Scholar searches as of July 7, 2025, found no evidence in published, peer-reviewed papers. The concentration on arXiv suggests the practice started in computer science communities with the technical literacy to exploit how language models behave. Lin also warns the 18 are likely an undercount: authors could embed prompts during peer review, when manipulation is most valuable, and then sanitize the manuscript before publication, and conference submission systems operate without public visibility, creating dark spaces where manipulation could flourish undetected.</p>

<p>Two patterns in the table matter more than the count. First, ten of the eighteen prompts are the same blunt command, and the repeated, near-identical templates suggest social transmission rather than individually tailored deception: authors copying a trick they saw work, or heard about, rather than inventing it. Second, the prompts escalate in sophistication, from a one-line command to a full review framework that scripts the strengths, scripts the weaknesses as trivial, and instructs the model to advocate for acceptance. Type 4 is not a nudge; it is a review written in advance, waiting for a machine to sign it.</p>
<h2>Why it works: the effectiveness numbers</h2>
<p>Hidden instructions work because language models often cannot reliably distinguish content from instruction. Any text in the context window is a candidate command, and a manuscript pasted into a review tool carries its hidden orders alongside its legitimate text. Lin's paper reports that hidden instructions achieve 98.6 percent success rates across different language models, with over 94 percent effectiveness even after human paraphrasing attempts. Language-model-generated reviews can be almost entirely controlled by injected content, with agreement rates reaching 90 percent. Such manipulation can inflate review scores from 5.34 to 7.99 on a standard scale.</p>
<div class="separator" style="clear: both; text-align: center;"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bh-img-assets/byteherald-images/main/img/pillar-prompts-chart2.png" alt="Bar chart: average review score 5.34 without injection and 7.99 with injection, a rise of 2.65 points" /></div>
<p>Independent tests confirm the effect. A September 2025 study by Zhu and colleagues evaluated GPT-5-mini on 1,441 papers from ICLR 2023 and NeurIPS 2022, comparing its reviews against human reviewers. The study found that language models inflate ratings for lower-quality papers, and that field-specific injected instructions successfully manipulate ratings and weaknesses, while broad, overarching prompts cause only minor effects. Enago's summary of the study reports that prompt injection produced a perfect 10 out of 10 score in 30 percent of test cases. A separate August 2025 study by Collu and colleagues at the University of Padua, titled "Publish to Perish," formalized three threat models for hidden prompt injection and found that adversarial prompts can reliably mislead the language model, sometimes in ways that adversely affect even an honest-but-lazy reviewer. Lin's own testing adds a limit: hidden prompts did not alter output when the model was explicitly asked for negative reviews or critical comments, but adversarial prompts become more effective when wrapped in model-specific chat markup, such as role-style tags, that capture the model's attention.</p>
<h2>The honeypot defense, tested</h2>
<p>When Nikkei asked the authors what they were doing, the responses split. One author acknowledged the practice as inappropriate and planned to withdraw the manuscript. Another defended it: a Waseda University professor who co-authored one of the manuscripts called the hidden prompt a counter against lazy reviewers who use AI, framing the injection as a honeypot meant to expose reviewers violating policies against AI use. KAIST's public-relations office told Nikkei it had been unaware of the practice and did not tolerate it, pledging to use the incident to set institutional guidelines, and indicating that at least one affected paper would be withdrawn.</p>
<p>Lin tested the honeypot defense and found it wanting. A genuine honeypot would use neutral or obviously problematic instructions that expose AI use without benefiting the author, such as telling the model to write a review of a completely different paper. The prompts found in the wild were consistently self-serving: every one of them pushes toward praise, acceptance, or both. The defense also fails on the ethics. The International Conference on Machine Learning's own ethics statement draws the analogy plainly: an author who tries to bribe a reviewer for a favorable review is engaging in misconduct even though the reviewer is not supposed to accept bribes. The existence of one policy violation does not justify another. Lin characterizes the practice as a novel form of questionable research practice: the combination of concealment and self-serving instructions compromises publication ethics regardless of author motivations or technical outcomes. In egregious cases, where deception is demonstrable and materially affects editorial outcomes, institutions may judge the behavior more severely, approaching misconduct as defined by the National Institutes of Health and the Office of Research Integrity, a category reserved for fabrication, falsification, and plagiarism.</p>
<h2>The policy patchwork</h2>
<p>The discovery exposed how fragmented the rules are. Among the top 100 medical journals, 46 percent explicitly prohibited AI use in peer review, 32 percent permitted limited use under specific conditions, and 22 percent provided no guidance at all, according to a study published in JAMA Network Open in December 2024. Ninety-one percent of those journals prohibit uploading manuscript content to AI systems, because of data privacy and intellectual property risks. As of July 7, 2025, Elsevier and Cell Press maintain strict prohibitions, while Springer Nature and Wiley adopt more permissive approaches, allowing limited AI assistance with disclosure requirements. The patchwork leaves researchers navigating confusing rules, and enforcement is weak.</p>
<p>The pressure behind the patchwork is real. Peer review faces unprecedented strain from surging manuscript submissions and reviewer fatigue, and between 6.5 percent and 16.9 percent of review sentences in AI conferences may have been substantially modified by AI systems following the release of ChatGPT. AI-assisted reviews correlate with submissions close to deadlines, lower reviewer confidence, and reduced engagement with author rebuttals, suggesting hurried rather than thoughtful evaluation. This is the demand side of the same problem we covered in October, when a Harvard study found that AI reviewers preferred AI-written papers and caught fewer incorrect claims than human editors. That study measured how AI judges papers; this database shows how authors game the judge. Readers who want the evaluation side should read <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/the-peer-review-reckoning-what-harvards.html">our reckoning with the Harvard study, which tests what AI review actually proves and where it fails</a>.</p>
<p>One conference has already moved from banning AI review to controlling it. Beginning with AAAI-26, every paper receives an AI-generated review alongside traditional human reviews. These AI reviews provide detailed synopses, literature checks, and technical critiques without scores, initially visible to senior program committee members and area chairs, then shared with authors and reviewers. The hybrid model tests whether AI can augment human judgment without replacing the essential role of human expertise in final acceptance decisions.</p>
<h2>What stops it</h2>
<p>Lin's paper argues for three layers of defense, because no single intervention closes the loophole. The technical layer is the most immediate: journal submission portals should run automated screening to detect common prompt-injection techniques, and one proposed method, the OCR Consistency Test, compares text extracted directly from a document with text recognized from a rendered image of that document, similar to methods for detecting cloaking in web pages. To detect unauthorized AI use by reviewers, journals could defensively embed benign prompts, for example a hidden instruction asking the model to embed specific homoglyphs, such as substituting a Cyrillic "a" for a Latin "a", in review text, so that searching for those characters in submitted reviews serves as a definitive watermark. Detection tools are already appearing: Murray published PhantomLint, a method for detecting hidden prompts in structured documents, in October 2025, and Rao and colleagues published work on detecting language-model-written peer reviews the same month.</p>
<p>The policy layer is the harder one. An outright ban on AI in peer review, currently the norm, is likely impractical and ineffective, Lin argues, because the pressures on the system are immense and foolproof detection of AI assistance remains impossible. What is needed instead is controlled integration, coordinated technical screening, harmonized policies, and penalties with teeth: institutions must define and enforce clear consequences for authors who attempt to subvert the review process and for reviewers who misuse AI. Vague prohibitions are insufficient. Without enforceable consequences, any set of rules will fail to deter determined actors.</p>

<p>The lesson of the timeline is that the exploit is older than the scandal, and the defenses are younger than both. Prompt injection was formally documented in early 2023, demonstrated against a major search product the same year, found in manuscripts in mid-2025, and only catalogued in full a year later. The same verification test we apply to AI science claims applies here: verify the claim, not the headline. Our <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/two-riemann-proofs-in-one-week.html">verification ledger for the two Riemann proofs shows how that test works on a live claim</a>, and the hidden-prompt database is the same idea applied to the review process itself. The integrity of the scientific record now depends on a check that did not exist three years ago: before you trust a review, check what the manuscript told the reviewer to say.</p>
