<p>Akamai is turning away some potential AI infrastructure business because it does not have the power to run it. Chief Financial Officer Ed McGowan said so at an RBC conference on November 17, 2026, in remarks reported on November 21, even as the company signed 14 billion dollars of large deals this year. His summary of the constraint was blunt: power is the most important, scarcest element today.</p>

<h2>What McGowan said at the RBC event</h2>
<p>McGowan told the RBC event, hosted by software analyst Rishi Jaluria, that Akamai's pipeline is the largest he has seen in its history. Demand is coming from enterprises, foundation-model developers, startups, and other AI companies. But he said power constraints make it difficult to take on additional large projects that need capacity in 2027. Availability should improve in the second half of 2028 and into 2029, he said, which puts roughly a year and a half between today's demand and the power to serve it.</p>
<p>The turnaway is selective, not a retreat. Akamai is already supporting workloads from central cloud to the edge, including post-training work, and McGowan pointed to latency-sensitive uses for its distributed infrastructure, including robotics and other forms of physical AI. What it does not plan to do is build gigawatt-scale facilities for large-scale model training. The company will supply the hardware customers need, with substantial demand for CPUs alongside GPUs in AI applications, but it is not chasing the biggest training builds.</p>
<h2>The deal stack behind the turnaway</h2>
<p>The deals McGowan is protecting are the largest in Akamai's history. The anchor is the Anthropic agreement announced September 25, 2026: an 11.6 billion dollar, seven-year commitment for Akamai Cloud infrastructure and software to support Anthropic's CPU workloads, with room to expand by up to 9 billion dollars more. Before that deal, Akamai had already signed more than 2.8 billion dollars of multi-year cloud infrastructure commitments this year, bringing total signed commitments to about 14.4 billion dollars.</p>

<p>The power math behind the stack is the story. Akamai estimates its signed commitments will need roughly 95 to 105 megawatts of power, and the Anthropic buildout alone requires about 5.5 billion dollars of capital spending, including about 1.7 billion dollars in the fourth quarter of 2026 to secure critical components. Revenue from the Anthropic agreement is not expected in 2026 at all. It begins in the second half of 2027 at roughly 150 to 300 million dollars for the year, with about 3.1 billion dollars of capital spending planned for fiscal 2027 and another 700 million dollars in fiscal 2028, before reaching roughly a 1.7 billion dollar annual run rate by the end of 2028. The cloud infrastructure business that carries all of this generated just 99 million dollars of revenue in the second quarter, up 39 percent year over year. The deals are enormous; the revenue is years away; the power has to be secured now.</p>
<h2>Why power, not chips, is the constraint</h2>
<p>This is the part of the AI buildout that does not show up in a chip benchmark. A data center is a power contract with a building around it, and the grid connections, permits, and megawatts arrive on a slower clock than the purchase orders. When we mapped the resource ledger in October, the trajectory was already steep: the International Energy Agency put total data center electricity demand on a path from 485 terawatt-hours in 2025 toward about 950 terawatt-hours by 2030, with individual campuses measured in gigawatts. Akamai's turnaway is what that trajectory looks like from inside one company: the demand is real, the contracts are signed, and the next projects cannot start because the power is not there yet. Readers who want the full ledger, campus by campus, will find it in <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/ais-power-and-water-bill-datacenter.html">our October resource ledger, which tracks megawatt draw, water use, and grid-strain cases for the announced projects</a>.</p>
<p>The constraint also changes who gets served first. McGowan said the roughly 13 percent internal rate of return calculated for a large infrastructure contract included data center fit-out and network connection costs, plus spare capacity, and assumed revenue only for the contract's duration, though the company may keep using the equipment afterward. He described the return as a floor, and noted that a customer committing to a deal of that size receives Akamai's best pricing. In plain terms, the biggest customers get the best price and the first megawatts, and smaller projects wait for the second half of 2028.</p>
<h2>The money side: funded through 2028, profitable later</h2>
<p>Akamai can afford to wait, for now. McGowan confirmed that the company closed a 2.5 billion dollar offering the prior week and is funded for its committed deals without returning to debt markets through 2028. What happens after that depends largely on how quickly it chooses to grow. He said Akamai holds investment-grade ratings from Fitch and Moody's, while S and P downgraded the company, a split he attributed to the agencies' different assumptions about future leverage and growth, and that Akamai expects to deleverage over the next two years as it pays down debt and expands margins. He declined to set a firm date for a sustainable return to positive free cash flow, saying faster cloud infrastructure growth would require more capital spending before revenue arrives, and that the company could reach that point in 2028 or later. The financing question is the same one hanging over the whole buildout, and <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/how-ai-buildout-is-paid-for-november.html">our November financing ledger adds up the instruments, from Oracle's 20 billion dollar raise to SpaceX's 40 billion dollar GPU lease, that are paying for it</a>.</p>
<p>There is also a timing wrinkle worth watching. McGowan said three large customer projects that had been expected to start in the fourth quarter are now generating revenue, and that data center construction, customer-specific fit-outs, and equipment issues can shift the precise date revenue begins. He called that spreadsheet risk: the money is real, the quarter it lands in is not guaranteed.</p>
<h2>What to watch next</h2>
<p>Three things decide whether this is an Akamai story or an industry story. First, whether power availability actually improves in the second half of 2028, as McGowan expects, or slips further. Second, whether other infrastructure providers start turning away work too, which would show the constraint is systemic rather than one company's pipeline. Third, whether the revenue ramp holds: 150 to 300 million dollars in the second half of 2027, then roughly a 1.7 billion dollar run rate by the end of 2028. If the power arrives and the revenue follows, the turnaway looks like discipline. If either slips, the 14.4 billion dollars of signed commitments becomes a promise the grid has to keep.</p>
