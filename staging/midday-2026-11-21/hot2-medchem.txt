<p>OpenAI's GPT-6.2 Astra scored 84.5 percent of available credit on MedChemBench, a new medicinal chemistry benchmark released on November 20, 2026. The headline number is real, and it is also the least interesting number in the release. On the two task types that decide which compounds advance toward a drug, selecting compounds and predicting their numerical properties, the same model earns only about half the credit.</p>

<h2>What MedChemBench actually tests</h2>
<p>MedChemBench is a test of reasoning drawn from published research, not a test of discovering new drugs. It contains 99 problems drawn from 117 papers and patents, covering 761 distinct molecular structures. For most problems, the model receives the context available at a chosen point in a research program: the biological target, the researchers' goals, the compounds studied so far, and selected experimental findings. It must then explain results, compare compounds, and predict their properties.</p>
<p>The benchmark grades four components: reconstructing molecular structures, with correct atom connections and stereochemistry; scientific reasoning, with explanations supported by the supplied evidence; ranking and selection, with compound choices that meet the stated requirements; and property predictions, judged against the numerical values and categories reported in the source material. That four-way split is the point of the benchmark. Medicinal chemistry is a tradeoff discipline: improving one property of a molecule can make another worse, so a compound might act strongly against a disease-related protein but be poorly absorbed or break down too quickly in the body. A model that can draw the molecule but cannot weigh those tradeoffs is not helping a chemist decide what to try next.</p>
<p>One problem shows the tradeoff directly. It draws on research into inhibitors of PRMT5, a protein studied in cancer drug discovery. The model must predict how three related compounds will behave: how strongly they block PRMT5 in cells, how readily they cross a cell barrier, and how quickly liver enzymes break them down. Later experiments, withheld from the model, showed the tradeoff in full: compound 23 blocked PRMT5 in cells at a lower concentration, while compound 24 crossed the cell barrier more readily and broke down more slowly in the liver-enzyme assay. Predicting those properties together is the central challenge of the field, because stronger activity against a target does not necessarily come with better permeability or slower breakdown.</p>

<p>OpenAI evaluated eleven GPT models at different reasoning-effort settings, producing 51 combinations across nine models from GPT-5.6 onward. Higher reasoning effort generally improved scores, while large differences between models remained. The gap between generations is striking: GPT-5.6 Sol trails the leader by more than 26 points, which is the distance between a model that can barely do the job and one that can do most of it. The gap between the top three is less than 3 points, which is the distance between models that are, on this test, nearly the same machine.</p>
<h2>The gap inside the headline</h2>
<div class="separator" style="clear: both; text-align: center;"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bh-img-assets/byteherald-images/main/img/hot2-medchem-chart.png" alt="Bar chart: GPT-6 Astra earns 95.4 percent on molecular structures and 83.3 percent overall, but about 50 percent on compound selection and property prediction" /></div>
<p>Break the headline into components and the story changes. GPT-6 Astra earned 95.4 percent of the available structure credit at xhigh reasoning, and in one problem it reconstructed an 18-atom ring correctly in all ten responses. GPT-5.6 Sol produced a smaller, 15-atom ring, and GPT-5.6 Luna split the molecule into two disconnected fragments; both failed across all ten responses. Those errors change the identity of the molecule being analyzed, so the structure test is a foundation test: a model that cannot draw the compound cannot reason about it.</p>
<p>But the foundation is where the strength ends. OpenAI says Astra earned 95.4 percent of the available structure credit at xhigh, but only about half the credit for selecting compounds and predicting numerical properties. Those two categories are the ones that gate real decisions in a lab: which compound to make next, and what it will do. A model that draws molecules beautifully and picks the wrong ones is a drafting tool, not a discovery partner. That is the honest reading of the 84.5 percent: the headline averages a near-perfect drafting score with a coin-flip decision score.</p>
<p>The same pattern shows up in the drug business around the benchmark. Anthropic is building a life sciences operation on the claim that AI agents can design antibodies and advance drug programs, with six partners and about 35,000 antibody designs in about a month for one food-allergy program, and no drug yet. MedChemBench is the measurement side of that same promise, and <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/anthropics-drug-business-mapped-six.html">our map of Anthropic's drug business separates what the partners have actually shipped from what is still a press release</a>. The benchmark and the business agree on one thing: the capability is real, and the distance to a drug is still measured in years.</p>
<h2>What the score does not prove</h2>
<p>OpenAI publishes the ceiling alongside the score, and it deserves quoting in full. These results do not establish whether models can discover new drugs or improve laboratory decisions. Prior exposure to the published sources cannot be ruled out, and each response was a single attempt without tools, feedback, or new experiments. The benchmark evaluates reasoning from historical evidence rather than real-world drug discovery. That is three separate caveats, and each one matters: the 99 problems come from papers and patents the models may have read during training, the models got one shot with no tools, and no new compound was ever tested in a lab.</p>
<p>There is also a dating wrinkle. The underlying paper is dated November 9, 2026, and it reports GPT-6 Astra at 83.83 percent at max effort and 83.33 percent at xhigh. The GPT-6.2 Astra result of 84.5 percent was added in the November 20 release, not in the paper. That does not make it wrong, but it means the newest number has not been through the same documentation as the rest. Readers who want to judge benchmarks the way they judge models should read <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/how-intelligence-index-v5-rewrote-ai.html">our audit of the Intelligence Index version 5, which shows how a benchmark's composition, not just its leaderboard, decides what a score means</a>. The test for MedChemBench is the same: ask what the components say, not what the headline says.</p>
