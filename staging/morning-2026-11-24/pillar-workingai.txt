<p><a href="https://www.anthropic.com/research/working-with-ai">Anthropic spent months asking 58 of its own enterprise customers a simple question: what is AI actually doing to your workforce</a>. The answer, published November 23, 2026, in a study called "Working with AI", is more careful than either the doomsayers or the optimists would like. Researchers Nathan Wilmers, Maxim Massenkoff, and Peter McCrory conducted 66 face-to-face interviews across manufacturers, law firms, hedge funds, trucking companies, healthcare companies, and startups. The headline finding is not a wave of layoffs. Only two of the 58 companies had seen AI automation fully eliminate a role and cost someone their job. The real story is quieter: about two-thirds of the companies are hiring less because of AI, and the highest-value uses of the technology came not from automating a workflow but from reorganizing the work around it.</p>

<h2>The hiring scoreboard</h2>
<p>The employment numbers are the study's most citable finding, and they cut against both familiar narratives at once. Layoffs driven by AI automation were rare, but the hiring slowdown was widespread, and contractors took the first hit. Two companies saw AI fully eliminate a position. Five pushed out employees who would not adopt AI. Ten hired more, mostly into AI implementation and IT support roles. About two-thirds reported hiring less, and a third of those declines came from simply not backfilling roles when people left. Nine companies cut contractors, including offshore developers, customer service representatives, legal counsel, and back-office staff, work the researchers describe as modular tasks that can be handed off separately. One finance company downsized well over 100 offshore data and developer contractors. One operations leader said the company's legal bill had just completely disappeared once AI took over contract reviews. The pattern is not replacement. It is a freeze at the edges: fewer new hires, fewer contractors, and a workforce that grows by buying tokens instead of people. As one engineering leader put it, "We've added a lot of capacity by buying tokens instead of hiring people."</p>

<h2>Where the value actually comes from</h2>
<p>Most of the nearly 300 AI applications the researchers mapped added value by increasing output rather than cutting costs. About half of those output gains came from speeding up work companies already did; the other half came from improving products and services or creating new ones. A medical group gave patients an up-front estimate of after-insurance costs. A dental company introduced an after-hours AI answering service, so patients' midnight calls no longer went to voicemail. In one-tenth of the use cases, the value went directly to employees, who did more enjoyable work or less work altogether. One construction manager said work "can be a joy again instead of a difficult, painful thing". The software the companies built with AI tells the same story in numbers: of 100 pieces of AI-built software the researchers could categorize in detail, 80 percent automated work previously done by hand, like spreadsheet updates, meeting notes, and document chasing, while only 20 percent replaced existing software. Coding agents did the most visible work, putting software skills into the hands of nontechnical staff: an accountant vibe-coded an app that audits whether 401(k) payments land in the right fund, a job that previously took 80 hours of matching records across dozens of systems. A former airport operations manager built a vehicle tracking app and said he was "wielding AI like Excalibur". The value was real. It just did not arrive as a smaller payroll.</p>
<h2>Three ways companies deploy it, and only one that scales</h2>
<p>The study's organizing finding is that companies deploy AI in three ways, and the first two stall. Top-down automation picks a workflow, automates it, and rolls it out. It produces the sharpest employment effects and the sharpest resistance. Managers described terminating developers who "just kind of shut down instead of trying to embrace it", and one financial services firm put it bluntly: "You either get on board or don't." Experts asked to build the systems that could replace them pushed back; one engineer said, "If I'm open to this, I'm essentially building my own coffin." A company that automated an approval workflow intended to double reviewer output and cut expected reviewer hires by more than 90 percent, then found the reviewers balked at doing double the cases and considered offering gift cards to get them to comply. Bottom-up experimentation hands out the tools and waits. It produces vivid individual wins and organizational mess: power users flooding colleagues with AI-written instructions, "Claude grenades" lobbed across teams, zombie apps nobody maintains, and a 401(k) audit prototype that sat unused for months because no vendor would implement it. The third way, reorganization, is where the highest-value uses sat. Companies broadened roles so one person or small team owned a whole workflow, redeployed people toward the bottleneck tasks AI cannot do, and built shared data infrastructure so nontechnical staff could build safely. One startup founder employed only individual contributors, each owning a distinct part of the product. The six startups that gave estimates said they would need, on average, four times as many employees without AI, and one chief operating officer said 90 percent of his operations budget went to AI tokens and 10 percent to headcount. The through line is the study's own summary: the highest-value applications involved meaningful reorganization, and companies that reorganized were reorganizing to take advantage of ongoing model improvements rather than settling on a fixed approach. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/89-of-enterprises-redesigned-ai-scale.html">That is the same mechanism our scale-trap pillar found from the other direction: 89 percent of enterprises redesigned AI work over token costs, and the redesign, not the model, decided what survived</a>.</p>

<h2>The bottlenecks are inside the building</h2>
<p>What slows AI down, in these interviews, is mostly not the technology and not the customer. The bottlenecks companies named were experts resisting deployment, tasks that AI does not accelerate, and the review problem: as coding speeds up, code review, sales, and strategic decision-making have to keep pace, and they often cannot. One agent added an errant letter to an API endpoint and broke a carefully planned feature release. One company built a customer service rating system on a two-year-old model that managers no longer take seriously. Customer skepticism and regulation were rarely cited as bottlenecks at all. The bulk of the friction comes from the process of adopting AI inside the organization itself. That is also where the study's warning sits: in top-down deployments, automation and surveillance threaten to undermine employees, and in bottom-up ones, uneven velocity creates inequality between teams. The companies that reorganized addressed both problems at once, by giving the people closest to the work the authority to redesign it, and by sharing the gains, using productivity improvements to lighten tedious work rather than to cut heads. The researchers are explicit that none of this is a guarantee against AI-induced job loss, and that it will not replace a strong policy response if disruption arrives at scale. What the interviews show is that employers have some say in how AI changes work, and that the choice is visible in the numbers: two layoffs, five push-outs, and a hiring freeze, or a reorganization that puts people where the models cannot go yet. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/ai-job-displacement-what-data-actually.html">Our earlier synthesis of seven job studies put the same question to the wider literature, and found the same split: the displacement concentrates in entry-level and offshore work, while the aggregate job loss stays small</a>.</p>
<h2>What the study cannot say</h2>
<p>The limits are stated in the study itself, and they matter to every number above. The 58 companies are Anthropic enterprise customers, selected for range across industry, size, and AI usage, not a random or systematically representative sample, so the findings cannot support economy-wide estimates. The value assessments are primarily qualitative descriptions from interviewees, not A/B tests or other quantitative assessments. The interviews were anonymous, and company names are pseudonyms. Read with those limits, the study is a map of how adoption actually happens inside firms that use AI seriously, not a forecast of what AI will do to employment overall. Its most useful number may be the smallest one: two companies, out of 58, where AI fully eliminated a role. The layoff wave has not arrived in these interviews. The hiring slowdown has, and the reorganization that captures the value is still, for most companies, a choice they have not made yet.</p>
