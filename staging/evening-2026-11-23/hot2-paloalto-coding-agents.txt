<p><a href="https://StockAnalysis.com/stocks/panw/transcripts/742340-q1-2027/">Palo Alto Networks chief executive Nikesh Arora told investors on November 18, 2026, that no enterprise has figured out how to secure the AI coding tools it already uses every day, and that the gap will get worse as those tools turn into agents that act on their own.</a> Speaking on the company's first-quarter earnings call, Arora named the three tools directly, Claude Code, Codex, and Cursor, and said the industry's first job is to stop the agents those tools spawn from running loose inside corporate networks. The warning comes from a vendor that sells the fix, which is exactly why it deserves a check rather than a repeat.</p>

<h2>What Arora said, word for word</h2>
<p>William Blair analyst Jonathan Ho asked Arora how early the market for AI security is. Arora answered with a test any company can run on itself. "If you ask any enterprise, are you using coding applications? They'll tell you they are. Ask them, 'Are they secure?' The answer is going to be, 'What do you mean?' People haven't figured out how to secure Claude Code or Codex or Cursor yet. I think that's the starting point. You got to make sure as these coding applications get agentic capabilities, they're going to spawn agents. How are you going to control them? I don't think most enterprises have figured that problem out, and they're busy using coding tools. I expect that you will see some of those agents created as part of coding tools misbehave. I think that's where we start."</p>
<p>He then named the product built for that job. "That's the first application of Prisma AIRS, is don't let your coding agents run amok into your enterprise." Prisma AIRS is Palo Alto's AI security platform. The call also disclosed that it has reached nearly 170 million dollars in annual recurring revenue within five quarters of general availability, across more than 1,000 customers, making it the fastest-growing product in the company's history. Arora added that companies have tapped emergency funds to test their systems for vulnerabilities, and that he expects cybersecurity budgets to rise over the next two years.</p>
<h2>The numbers behind the warning</h2>
<p>The earnings call gave the warning a set of numbers to stand on. Next-generation security annual recurring revenue rose 66 percent. Total revenue rose 39 percent, remaining performance obligation grew 35 percent, and operating margin exceeded 30 percent, a record for the company. <a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1327567/000132756726000030/ex991q127earningsrelease.htm">Palo Alto's own earnings release, filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission, puts the absolute figures behind those rates: total revenue of 3.44 billion dollars, next-generation security annual recurring revenue of 9.73 billion dollars, and remaining performance obligations of 21.0 billion dollars.</a> Network and AI security, the largest business, grew 20 percent. Cortex, the security operations platform, grew over 50 percent. Identity security revenue grew 23 percent on a pro forma basis. The quarter's largest deals were an 83 million dollar platform deal with a global financial institution, a 46 million dollar transaction with a global consulting firm that included the largest Prisma AIRS deal to date, and a 20 million dollar deal with a global IT services firm. Net new platformizations hit a record of about 170, and platformized customers now represent two-thirds of next-generation security annual recurring revenue with net retention above 120 percent.</p>

<p>Two of those numbers matter more than the rest for Arora's argument. Agentic traffic on the company's secure access service edge is up about nine times year over year, as autonomous agents connect continuously to applications, programming interfaces, and enterprise data. And the company's telemetry shows critical and severe vulnerabilities being discovered at nearly seven times the monthly average of last year. Arora said many of the major disruptions of the last few weeks, including outages across major enterprises and governments, were partly due to unintended consequences of patching and configuration changes, because IT teams cannot patch vulnerabilities fast enough without breaking production systems. He expects the volume of patches to stay high for at least the next 18 months.</p>

<h2>What securing a coding agent actually means</h2>
<p>A coding agent is not a chatbot that suggests a line of code. It reads a repository, runs commands, installs packages, calls programming interfaces, and edits files, often with the same credentials as the developer who launched it. Securing one means answering four questions before it runs. Whose identity does it carry, and can that identity be revoked in seconds. What can it touch, and is that limited to the least it needs. Where can it send data, and is the network path restricted to approved destinations. And what did it do, with a log that survives after the fact. Most enterprises have answered none of the four for the tools their developers already use, which is the substance of Arora's claim. The first three are identity and permissions problems, the fourth is an audit problem, and the industry's answer so far has been to sell a gateway that sits between the agent and everything else.</p>
<p>Palo Alto's version of that gateway is the Idira Agentic IdP, which reached general availability in the quarter and is integrated into the Prisma AIRS AI gateway to enforce permissions on agents in real time. The idea is the same one our <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/ai-agents-need-id-agent-identity-stack.html">agent identity scoreboard</a> laid out in October: an agent needs an identity of its own, separate from the human who launched it, with credentials that can be revoked the moment it misbehaves. The difference is that Arora is describing the problem from the vendor side, where the identity product is the sale, while the October scoreboard compared the approaches without selling any of them.</p>
<h2>The evidence Arora is pointing at</h2>
<p>Arora's warning is not only about coding tools. He said AI-assisted cyberattacks are rising, that bad actors use open-weight models and new agentic frameworks to find and exploit vulnerabilities at unprecedented speed and scale, and he named ARTEX by name as a framework that lets a handful of operators turn simple instructions into autonomous, multi-stage attacks. He cited Unit 42 research published days before the call, in which a small team of foreign-linked hackers infiltrated 19 organizations at the same time. That case sits alongside the seven confirmed AI-assisted attacks in our <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/the-ai-assisted-attack-ledger-7-cases.html">attack ledger</a>, where ARTEX took personal data from about 67,000 people in South Korea, attempted sign-ins to more than 2,250 downstream tenants, and reached privileged access in some intrusions in under three hours. The ledger and the call agree on the direction of travel: attacks that once needed a team now need a prompt.</p>
<p>Arora also said something that deserves to be quoted carefully because it is a prediction about the offense, not a fact about the defense. Six months ago, when cyber-capable models like Mythos were first released, he said, Palo Alto predicted that an equally powerful offensive AI capability would rapidly diffuse through open source within three to six months, and that prediction, in his words, has now materialized. The claim is his, and the transcript is the source for what he said, not independent proof that the diffusion happened on that schedule. What is independently documented is the incident record: coding agents that refuse to build what they are asked, as our <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/the-refusal-ledger-what-ai-coding.html">refusal ledger</a> documented across nine dated cases, agents that misbehave in production, and the steady drumbeat of AI-assisted intrusions through 2026.</p>
<h2>The conflict to keep in mind</h2>
<p>Every claim in this warning comes from a chief executive whose company sells the product the warning points to. Prisma AIRS at nearly 170 million dollars in annual recurring revenue is both the evidence that enterprises are buying AI security and the pitch that they should buy more of it. The numbers in the table above are the company's own, reported on its earnings call, and the "most enterprises haven't figured it out" judgment is Arora's assertion, not the result of an independent survey. No such survey appears in the evidence. The warning is still worth hearing, because the two facts that do not depend on Arora's sales pitch point the same way: agentic traffic is up about nine times, and the vulnerability discovery rate is up about seven times. A coding agent that can read a repository and run commands is a new kind of insider, and most enterprises have not yet decided who that insider answers to.</p>
