<p><a href="https://www.datacenterwatch.org/report">Data Center Watch, a project tracking local opposition to data-center construction across 28 states, lists 16 projects that were blocked or delayed in the last two years, worth 64 billion dollars between them.</a> The list runs from a 14 billion dollar campus in Arizona that the developer withdrew after residents objected, to a 24.7 billion dollar Virginia gateway still tied up in court. The database below is the first time those 16 projects have been compiled in one table, with the value, the developer, the status, and the grounds cited in each case, plus what the November 2026 ballot results added after the list was compiled.</p>

<h2>The database: 16 projects, blocked or delayed</h2>
<p>Data Center Watch defines a project as blocked when its development has been definitively halted, either by permit denial or by the developer withdrawing the proposal in response to local opposition, and delayed when the original timeline has been disrupted but the developer is still seeking approval. The research covers May 2024 to March 2025, and the statuses below are as of March 2025. Three delayed projects have no disclosed investment value and are excluded from the value sums.</p>

<h2>What the rows add up to</h2>
<p>The six blocked projects sum to 17.8 billion dollars, and the seven delayed projects with disclosed values sum to 46.7 billion dollars, for a total of 64.5 billion dollars across the 13 rows with values. Those sums match the report's own rounded figures of 18 billion dollars blocked and 46 billion dollars delayed, or 64 billion dollars in total, and the small difference is the three delayed rows with undisclosed values, which are left out of the arithmetic rather than guessed at. The single largest row is the Prince William Digital Gateway at 24.7 billion dollars, a delayed project that alone accounts for more than a third of the counted value. The largest blocked row is Tract's 14 billion dollar Arizona campus, which the developer withdrew in May 2024 and then replaced with a second project near the Buckeye airport, announced in August 2024 after engaging with authorities and securing the mayor's support.</p>

<h2>Virginia is the center of the backlash</h2>
<p>Nine of the 16 rows sit in Virginia, and they carry 46.8 billion dollars of the 64.5 billion dollars counted, or about 73 percent of the value. That concentration is not an accident of the sample. Northern Virginia is the largest data-center market in the world, and it is also where opposition has become most organized: Data Center Watch counts 42 activist groups in Virginia alone, and the Data Center Reform Coalition, founded in 2023, coordinates 41 organizations across the region. The pattern in the Virginia rows is consistent. Projects are challenged at the planning commission, the board of supervisors reconsiders, and the developer either withdraws, shrinks the plan, or waits out a lawsuit. In Warrenton, every town council member who supported the Amazon project subsequently lost re-election, and in November 2024 the remaining supporters lost their seats too. In Cascade Locks, Oregon, voters recalled two port authority officials in June 2023 for supporting a 100 million dollar project, and the new board canceled it the next month.</p>

<h2>Why projects get blocked</h2>
<p>Counting the grounds cited across the 16 rows, noise appears in eight, environmental impact, wildlife, or rural preservation in six, power demand or grid strain in five, water in three, property values in two, and traffic in two. The counts are ours, tallied from the table, and they show that the opposition is not one argument but a bundle of local ones. Power is the ground that connects this database to the rest of the buildout story: the same grid strain that has AI labs buying their own power, as our <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/the-grid-cant-keep-up-so-ai-labs-buy.html">grid pillar</a> documented, is the ground neighbors cite when a substation or a transmission line is proposed next to their homes. The report also notes that opposition is bipartisan in a way few infrastructure fights are: among elected officials who took public positions against projects larger than 50 megawatts, 55 percent were Republicans and 45 percent were Democrats, with Republicans more often citing tax incentives and grid strain and Democrats more often citing environmental impact and resource use.</p>
<h2>What November 2026 added</h2>
<p>The database stops in March 2025, and the eight months since have made the backlash larger, not smaller. On November 3, 2026, voters in roughly 40 cities and towns weighed measures on data centers, and at least 33 voted to rein them in, with only one town in North Dakota voting the other way, according to USA Today. Centuri counted about 980 active local moratoriums or restrictions, about 80 percent of them enacted since April 2026, and 44 local ballot measures that passed restrictions across 12 states, led by Ohio, where 18 communities all approved restrictions. The same count put the number of activist groups at 833 across 49 states, and polling put opposition at about three-quarters of voters in one survey and 70 percent in Gallup's. Our <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/data-centers-lost-33-of-40-ballot.html">ballot-fight report</a> compiled those figures the day they landed. The industry's response, so far, has been three concessions: Microsoft, Amazon, and OpenAI say they will stop using non-disclosure agreements in negotiations with local governments, technology companies are offering to pay for more electricity infrastructure, and new projects are pivoting from evaporative cooling to closed-loop systems that use less water.</p>
<h2>How to read this database</h2>
<p>Three cautions belong with the table. First, the rows describe the opposition wave through March 2025, not a live tracker, and several statuses will have moved since; the Prince William gateway, the Culpeper campus, and the King George project were all unresolved at the last update. Second, Data Center Watch itself warns that attributing delays exclusively to opposition oversimplifies a landscape that also includes permitting, utility readiness, incentives, and market conditions, and each project's circumstances must be assessed individually. Third, the value sums cover only the 13 rows with disclosed values, and the report's 64 billion dollar headline is a rounded total, not a precise audit. What the database does, and what the headline counts cannot, is name the projects. A reader who wants to know which data center was blocked, where, by whom, and on what grounds, can now find the answer in one place, and a journalist covering the next ballot fight can start from the row that matches it.</p>
