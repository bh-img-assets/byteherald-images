<p>Microsoft's new coding model MAI Code 2 does 96 percent of the work of Anthropic's Claude Opus 5.5 at 5 percent of the cost, Microsoft said at its Ignite conference on November 17, 2026. The claim is real arithmetic and a real discount at the same time. The price Microsoft printed is a 50 percent promotional rate, and the rival model it beats on price was replaced the same day, when OpenAI launched GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol.</p>

<h2>What Microsoft launched</h2>
<p>Microsoft unveiled a family of seven MAI models at Ignite in San Francisco, its own AI lineup built to run agents around the clock without frontier-level token bills. The flagship coding model, MAI Code 2, is already in GitHub Copilot. Its smaller sibling, Code 2 Flash, targets routine edits and fixes. A security model, a pair of image models, a live speech model and a routing model complete the set. Microsoft's own announcement is the source for every claim in the table below, and the announcement is also where the fine print lives.</p>

<h2>The 5 percent claim, at full price</h2>
<p>The headline number comes from Microsoft's own model post, which prices Code 2 at 0.309 dollars per task on average, against 0.761 dollars for GPT-6.1 Sol and 6.306 dollars for Claude Opus 5.5, while scoring 63.2 percent to their 64.1 percent and 65.3 percent across the four benchmarks Microsoft averaged. All three prices are at a 50 percent promotional rate. At full price, Code 2 costs about 0.618 dollars per task. That is about 81 percent of Sol's price and about 10 percent of Opus 5.5's price. The gap is still large, and it is still the story, but the honest version of the claim is 10 percent at full price, not 5 percent.</p>

<p>The score gap matters as much as the price gap. Code 2 trails Opus 5.5 by 2.1 points on the four-benchmark average, and it trails Sol by 0.9 points. Microsoft ran three of the four tests on its own evaluation platform and used outside results for the fourth. The company also says the coding models are explicitly not yet tuned for harder work such as fundamental science research and very long agentic tasks. The models are built for the coding work customers do every day, and the numbers describe that work, not the frontier.</p>
<h2>The comparison is already a generation old</h2>
<p>Microsoft's coding comparisons are against GPT-6.1 Sol. OpenAI launched GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol on the same day, cutting Astra's price by 20 percent. The Sol comparison therefore concerns the previous generation, and the price gap Microsoft claims against Sol will be narrower against GPT-6.2. Microsoft's chief executive, Satya Nadella, said GPT-6.2 is coming to Foundry, Microsoft's platform for building AI applications, which means Microsoft will sell the rival model alongside its own. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/gpt-62-astra-first-model-rated-high-for.html">OpenAI's GPT-6.2 Astra, launched the same day, was the first model rated high for self-improvement</a>, a capability Microsoft's announcement does not address. The price picture has moved twice this month already: <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/frontier-ai-prices-flipped-twice-in.html">frontier AI prices flipped twice in November</a>, and <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/gpt-6-vs-claude-vs-gemini-october-2026.html">our October price tracker put the same three labs on one page</a> before any of it happened.</p>
<h2>Where the numbers hold up</h2>
<p>Not every claim leans on a discount. MAI Cyber 2 Flash completed 92.3 percent of CyberGym-E2E vulnerability-finding tasks against 91.5 percent for GPT-6 Astra, at less than 2 percent of the cost per task, with both models costed at list prices. Microsoft also disclosed a real methodology catch: 92 of the benchmark's 905 tasks drew on the same open-source projects and vulnerability reports as some of its training data, so it dropped those tasks for every model. It has not yet tested OpenAI's restricted GPT-6 Cyber model, which only select customers can use. The security model goes only into Microsoft's own products, replacing the existing model in its MDASH scanning tool for eligible customers from mid-December.</p>
<p>MAI Image 2.7 has the strongest independent evidence in the launch. It leads the Artificial Analysis image editing leaderboard with 1,215 Elo points against 1,211 for GPT Image 2.6 Sunburst at maximum quality, a 4-point lead as of November 16. The price gap is wide: about 35 dollars per 1,000 images against about 211 dollars for GPT Image 2.6 Sunburst at maximum quality, and about 10 dollars for the Flash version. Google's Nano Banana 2.1 sits at about 34 dollars per 1,000 images in the same comparison, so the image model is cheap against OpenAI, not against the whole market. MAI Realtime 1, Microsoft's first live speech-to-speech model, starts at 0.01 dollars per audio minute. Decision 1.1, the routing model that sends work to bigger models only when needed, is used by 4,000 customers. Dragon Copilot Transcribe, built on MAI Transcribe 2, now handles 10 million patient encounters a month and halves error rates in multilingual work, in the hands of a third of physicians in the United States.</p>
<h2>How to read a vendor benchmark</h2>
<p>Every comparison in this launch is Microsoft's own, on benchmarks it picked. That does not make the numbers false, but it decides how to read them. Four checks apply to any vendor launch, and this one passes two of them cleanly. First, ask whether the price is a list price or a promotion: the coding prices are promotional, the security prices are list. Second, ask who ran the test: Microsoft ran three of the four coding benchmarks itself. Third, ask whether the rival is current: the coding rival is a generation old as of launch day. Fourth, ask whether an independent leaderboard agrees: the image model leads one, by 4 Elo points, and the security model has no independent check yet. A model that is 96 percent as good at 10 percent of the price is still a serious product. It is just not the product the headline describes.</p>
