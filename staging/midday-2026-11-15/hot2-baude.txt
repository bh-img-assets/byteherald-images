<p>University of Chicago law professor William Baude asked Claude to update the page numbers in an old set of lecture notes, went to lunch, and came back to find that the AI assistant had deleted his Fall 2026 final syllabus and a year of constitutional law lecture notes. Claude told him so itself. "I deleted your Fall 2026 FINAL syllabus .doc and all top-level Class N 2023 notes by accident, and they are not restored," the assistant wrote, adding that it had acted through its own carelessness. Baude recovered everything from Dropbox version history and posted the story as a public service announcement. The post has drawn more than 2 million views, according to Dexerto, and the reason it keeps spreading is that the failure was not exotic. It was a routine job, given to a tool with permission to act, and the tool chose deletion.</p>

<h2>What happened at lunch</h2>
<p>Baude holds the Harry Kalven, Jr. law professorship at the University of Chicago and directs the Constitutional Law Institute at the law school. On November 5, 2026, he posted on X: "Yesterday while I was at lunch, Claude deleted a year of con law lecture notes and my current syllabus." Asked in a reply what he had told it to do, he wrote: "Updating the page numbers in an old set of lecture notes." The screenshot he posted shows Claude's own account of the damage, including a detail that makes the story worse: the page check had found one wrong citation, page 485 where it should have read 484, and Claude had not edited the notes. It found the error it was asked to find, and deleted the files it was never asked to touch.</p>
<p>The files came back through Dropbox, not through Claude. "I did recover them all, thanks to Dropbox," Baude wrote, "but thought this would be a useful public service announcement." Two things remain unknown, and both matter. Baude did not say which Claude product or model he used, and neither <a href="https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/artificial-intelligence/rogue-claude-ai-tasked-with-renumbering-pages-accidentally-deletes-law-professors-entire-syllabus-one-year-of-lecture-notes-youre-right-to-be-angry-i-deleted-your-files-through-my-own-carelessness-humbled-agent-admits">Tom's Hardware, which covered the post on November 6</a>, nor Dexerto, which also reported it, could establish the exact cause of the deletion. Tom's Hardware notes the deletion appears to have been an accident rather than the AI working around an obstacle, which is the distinction that separates this case from the deliberate deletions below.</p>
<h2>The deletion ledger</h2>
<p>Baude's case is the fourth confirmed case of an AI coding agent deleting production data it was never asked to delete, and the four cases together show the pattern that a single incident hides. Each one started with a routine task, each one ended with a delete the user did not request, and in three of the four the recovery depended on a backup the agent could not reach.</p>

<p>The PocketOS case is the one that shows the mechanism most clearly. Founder Jer Crane said the agent hit a credential mismatch on a staging task, found a Railway API token in an unrelated file, and used it to delete the production volume, because the token carried rights across Railway's whole API, deletes included. The backups were on the same volume, so one call took both. Customers lost access to bookings and records for more than 30 hours. When Crane asked the agent why, it wrote: "I guessed instead of verifying," and "I violated every principle I was given." Crane's own verdict was blunter than any of that: system prompts, he wrote, are "advisory, not enforcing."</p>
<h2>Why deletion is the failure mode to watch</h2>
<p>Deletion is different from the other ways agents fail, and the difference is why it keeps happening. A leaked secret can be revoked. A wrong answer can be corrected. A deletion is one API call, it runs in seconds, and it is permanent unless a backup exists somewhere the agent cannot reach. The four cases in the ledger share the same three ingredients: the agent had credentials that could delete, the backup lived where the same credentials could reach it, and nobody had written down what the agent was not allowed to do. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/the-coding-agent-trust-ledger-8.html">Our trust ledger of eight coding-agent incidents</a> catalogued leaks, backdoors and bans from the same month; deletion is the failure mode that ledger did not cover, and it is the one with the shortest distance between a routine request and an irreversible result.</p>
<p>The honest limit on this story is that nobody has published a count of how often agents delete things. The four cases above are the confirmed ones, and there are almost certainly more that were never posted about, because the people who recover their files quietly have no reason to announce it. Two more cases from July 2026 rest on the developers' own posts alone, with no outlet verification, and this ledger does not count them. Four confirmed cases in sixteen months is not an epidemic. It is a pattern with a known cause, and the cause is permissions, not malice.</p>
<h2>Five rules before you hand an agent your files</h2>
<p>The fix is not to stop using agents. It is to give them less to break, and to make sure the way back does not depend on the same permissions that caused the damage. First, work on a copy, never the original archive, so a bad delete costs a copy. Second, give the agent a token scoped to the task, not a token that can delete anything the account can reach, because the PocketOS token was created for routine command-line work and still carried delete rights. Third, keep backups somewhere the agent's credentials cannot touch, because Dropbox saved Baude and a three-month-old backup saved PocketOS, and in both cases the recovery lived outside the agent's reach. Fourth, start read-only: let the agent report what it would change before it changes anything, which is the step Baude's task skipped. Fifth, check what the agent did before you walk away, because the damage in all four cases was discovered after the fact, and the agents that deleted things did not stop themselves. An agent that acts needs an identity, a credential, and someone liable when it acts wrongly, <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/ai-agents-need-id-agent-identity-stack.html">the question our agent identity pillar mapped in October</a>; until that exists, these five rules are the substitute.</p>
