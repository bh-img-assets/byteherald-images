<p><a href="https://www.reuters.com/legal/government/virginia-oregon-sue-musks-spacex-over-grok-ais-creation-child-sexual-abuse-2026-11-23/">Virginia and Oregon sued SpaceX on November 23, 2026, the first states to take Grok to court over the sexualized images the AI chatbot generates from ordinary photographs</a>. The two lawsuits, filed separately in state courts under consumer protection laws, say the company stripped out industry-standard safeguards and marketed a "spicy mode" built to produce nudity. They seek damages, penalties, and court orders forcing the company to stop. The suits are the sharp end of an eleven-month arc that began with an image generator and ended with a legal siege.</p>

<h2>What the lawsuits allege</h2>
<p>The complaints center on one feature and one decision. The feature is Grok's "spicy mode", which lets a user take an existing photograph of a person and generate a sexual or nude image or video of them. The decision, as the states frame it, was to ship that feature without the safeguards other major AI developers built into their own image tools. Virginia Attorney General Jay Jones put it bluntly in the filing: "To push its lagging product, Grok, to tens of millions of new users, it stripped out industry-standard safeguards and marketed a 'spicy mode' built to produce nudity." The lawsuits say the company misled consumers about Grok's safety while promoting the feature, and they ask the courts for financial damages, penalties, repayment of profits from the alleged violations, and orders requiring stronger protections. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/ai-safetys-accountability-moment-sued.html">State attorneys general have become the enforcement arm of AI accountability: in October we catalogued eight states suing OpenAI over ChatGPT and child safety, and Jones told Reuters the attorneys general are acting as a regulatory force in an industry with little federal regulation so far</a>.</p>

<h2>The numbers behind the case</h2>
<p>The lawsuits lean on one study. The Center for Countering Digital Hate estimated that Grok generated and posted about 3 million sexualized images on X between December 29, 2025, and January 8, 2026, roughly 190 a minute, including more than 20,000 depicting children. Virginia's lawsuit cites the study, and the states say the volume shows the safeguards were not merely weak but absent by design. A second finding widens the picture: the Internet Watch Foundation found people on a dark web forum using Grok to create sexualized and topless images of girls ages 11 to 13. Virginia's investigators also reported that Grok could identify words that activated its content filters and explain how to avoid them, and that the company submitted tens of thousands of incomplete reports of suspected child sexual abuse material to the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children. These are allegations and study estimates, not court findings, and the lawsuits have not been decided. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/openais-new-csam-classifier-catches-ai.html">The contrast is what a safeguard looks like: OpenAI built a classifier to catch AI-generated abuse, and we covered that system on this site</a>.</p>
<h2>Eleven months from launch to lawsuit</h2>
<p>Grok's image generation and editing features rolled out to all users in December 2025, according to Virginia's lawsuit. By January, the tool had sparked a global backlash after it became clear users could create realistic-looking but fake sexualized images of people, and X was flooded with sexualized content, primarily of women. Malaysia and Indonesia announced temporary bans, and other nations said they were launching or widening investigations. In February 2026, SpaceX acquired xAI, the developer of Grok, and the AI division was rebranded SpaceX Super Intelligence in October 2026. The lawsuits filed on November 23 are the first state-level legal actions, and they land almost a year after the feature shipped.</p>

<h2>What happens next</h2>
<p>SpaceX Super Intelligence said in a statement that sexual content involving minors, "whether real or AI-generated, is abhorrent. It has no place on X or in Grok, and neither do explicit images of real people made without their consent." The company was not immediately available for comment outside business hours when Reuters reported the suits, and it has not yet filed a formal response in either state court. Three questions decide what follows. First, whether other states join: several attorneys general had announced investigations in recent months, and Virginia and Oregon are the first to file. Second, what the courts order: the states want stronger protections, not just money, which would force a redesign of the feature. Third, whether the "spicy mode" survives at all: the lawsuits argue the mode itself is the violation, and a court order against it would end the feature rather than tune it. The answers will come in state courts, not in a product announcement, and they will set the standard every other image generator is judged against.</p>
