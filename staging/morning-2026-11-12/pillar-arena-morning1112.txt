<p><a href="https://arena.ai/leaderboard/agent">Agent Arena</a>, the leaderboard that grades AI models on real work instead of exam questions, published its latest board on November 11, 2026, built from 2,822,562 real sessions across 59 models. Claude Fable 5.2 at maximum effort leads it at 30.48 percent, about 1.6 times the next model, Claude Opus 5.5 at 19.51 percent. But the number most coverage will quote is not a success rate, and the gap between first and second is the only gap in the top ten that is clearly real. This is the first compiled reading of the full board, with the cost of every point and the confidence intervals that decide which ranks are ties.</p>

<h2>What the numbers actually mean</h2>
<p>Agent Arena is run by Arena, the organization behind the LMArena leaderboards, and it works differently from every exam-style benchmark. In Agent Mode, every real session is routed to a randomly chosen model, and the model works through a multi-step task with tools: running shell commands, searching the web, fetching pages, writing files. Users interact with one agent in a long thread, sometimes over hundreds of turns, and the board scores what happened from the session traces, using explicit feedback and implicit behavioral signals, with a <a href="https://arena.ai/blog/agent-arena-methodology/">method called causal tracing</a> instead of the head-to-head voting that powers the chat leaderboards. That disclosure-first design is what <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/who-grades-ai-agents-benchmark.html">our audit of seven scripted agent benchmarks</a> found separates a trustworthy test from a marketing slide.</p>
<p>That design is why the headline number is a net improvement, not a score out of 100. Each signal is a treatment effect: how much better or worse a behavior becomes when this model is substituted for the average model, expressed in percentage points. Fable 5.2's 21.66 percent on task confirmation does not mean users confirm the task is done 21.66 percent of the time. It means users confirm done 21.66 percentage points more often with Fable 5.2 than with the average model on the board. The headline rank is a weighted average of the five signals, with Bash Reliability carrying half the weight of each other signal. A score near zero means about average, a negative score means below average, and the little plus-or-minus after each number is the 95 percent confidence interval: when two models' ranges overlap a lot, treat them as basically tied. That rule is printed on the board itself, and it is the rule most coverage ignores.</p>
<p>The five signals each capture one dimension of doing the work well. Confirmed Success counts how often users explicitly confirm the task is done. Praise vs Complaint measures whether users say more explicitly positive things than negative ones, separate from button clicks. Steerability measures whether the very next response actually lands when a user pushes back or corrects the model. Bash Reliability measures how reliably the model's shell commands run without failing, judged per command, with failures outside the agent's control excluded. Tool Hallucination measures how often the model calls a tool that does not exist, where lower is better, and the board always colors the good direction green so the sign never needs decoding.</p>
<h2>The lead is real. Ranks 3 to 6 are a tie.</h2>
<p>Apply the board's own confidence-interval rule and the top ten splits into three groups. Fable 5.2's headline range is 27.41 to 33.55 percent, and Opus 5.5's is 17.47 to 21.55. There is no overlap, so the lead is real, and at about 1.6 times the next model by ByteHerald arithmetic, it is the largest gap on the board. A fixed task set agrees: <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/ai-finished-41-of-real-freelance-jobs.html">the Remote Labor Index gave Fable 5.2 a 41.30 percent completion rate on 240 real Upwork projects</a>, a different measurement with human grading. Fable 5.2 also more than doubles its predecessor: Fable 5.1 sits at 14.62 percent, so the new model improved on the old one by 15.86 points in two days of board time, since Fable 5.2 <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/claude-fable-52-built-os-in-8-days-it.html">launched on November 9</a> and the board is dated November 11.</p>
<p>The second group is a tie. Gemini 4 Carbon's range is 16.22 to 17.58, which touches Opus 5.5's lower bound at 17.47, and Claude Sonnet 5.5's range is 13.26 to 18.88, which covers Carbon's range entirely and most of Fable 5.1's and Astra's ranges. Ranks 3, 4, 5, and 6 cannot be separated on this board, and any headline that ranks them as a fixed order is reading precision the data does not have. Carbon is the most precisely measured of the four, with the tightest interval at plus or minus 0.68 and the largest sample in the top ten at 61,473 sessions, against 7,841 for the leader, which is the smallest sample in the top ten and the reason Fable 5.2's interval is the widest at the top. As more sessions arrive, the intervals will narrow, and the tie may resolve, or it may not.</p>
<p>The per-signal leaders tell a different story than the headline. Fable 5.2 leads three signals outright: task confirmation at 21.66 percent, praise at 74.41 percent, and steerability at 32.91 percent. The praise gap is the widest on the board: the next model, Carbon, is at 40.91 percent, and even at the bottom of Fable 5.2's wide interval, 61.71 percent, it stays clear. Bash Reliability belongs to Claude Sonnet 5 at the High effort level, at 15.10 percent, ahead of Opus 5.5 at 15.03 and Fable 5.2 at 14.42. Tool Hallucination is a ten-way tie at the 0.99 percent floor, which says the signal is rare enough that the board cannot separate the leaders, not that ten models are equally careful.</p>
<h2>The price of a point</h2>
<p>The board also prices every model, and the price changes the ranking completely. Within the top ten, the median cost per task runs from 71 cents for Gemini 4 Argon to 7.74 dollars for Claude Fable 5.1, with the leader at 7.40 dollars. Divide cost by net improvement and the cheapest point on the board is Argon's 6.6 cents, followed by Terra at 7.5 cents, Sol at 8.5 cents, and Carbon at 8.6 cents. Fable 5.2 costs 24.3 cents per point, nearly three times Carbon's, and Fable 5.1 costs 52.9 cents per point, the worst in the top ten. Astra, at 41.9 cents per point, is the second most expensive point on the board. The leader buys its margin with money and time: 16.6 minutes and 106,600 tokens per task, against 9.9 minutes and 48,000 tokens for Carbon.</p>

<h2>How to use this board</h2>
<p>Agent Arena is the best public evidence we have about how models behave on real work, and it is also the easiest board to misread, because it looks like a score out of 100 and is not. Five checks keep the reading honest. First, read the intervals before the ranks: a gap smaller than the overlap is a tie. Second, remember every number is measured against the average model, so when a strong new model joins, every other model's number can fall without its behavior changing, and the board says so itself. Third, check the session count: a model with 7,841 sessions is less certain than one with 61,473, and the newest model on the board is always the least measured. Fourth, separate the effort levels: the top spots are all maximum-effort runs, so the board measures capability at maximum spend, and a cheaper model at a lower effort level may be the better buy for your task. Fifth, ask what the board cannot see: users choose their own tasks, set their own difficulty, and judge their own outcomes, so a model that attracts harder tasks can look worse than one that attracts easy ones, and the board does not control for that. One more limit to keep in mind: the board measures general agentic sessions, so coding-specific ability still needs coding-specific tests.</p>
<p>That is also why this board belongs next to the other agent benchmarks, not above them. Agent Arena's disclosure is unusually good: the method, the signals, the weights, and the intervals are all published. Three different measurements, three different methods, one leader. That agreement is worth more than any single number on any single board, and it is the reason the lead is believable even though the sample is still small.</p>
