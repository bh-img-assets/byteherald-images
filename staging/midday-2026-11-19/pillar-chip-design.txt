<p>In five years, AI has moved from arranging the blocks on a chip to rewriting the circuits inside them. Google's AlphaChip placed chip blocks in hours in 2021. In November 2026, OpenAI's models found improvements worth up to 14 percent on AMD's flagship GPU silicon, and Anthropic's Claude ported software to new AMD hardware in about three days. This pillar puts every verified result in one place: what was claimed, what was measured, and what is still only a claim.</p>

<h2>What each result actually was</h2>
<p>AlphaChip is the oldest result and the only one with peer review. In June 2021, Nature published "A graph placement methodology for fast chip design" by 20 Google-affiliated researchers. The system is a reinforcement learning agent that places large circuit components, called macros, on a chip floorplan, one at a time. It was pre-trained on 20 earlier designs before being fine-tuned on a specific chip. Google DeepMind says AlphaChip-generated layouts were used in TPU v5e, TPU v5p and the sixth-generation Trillium accelerator, and in Axion data-center CPUs, and that the technique generated layouts for a larger share of the floorplan with each generation. The claims were disputed: a 2023 critique argued the published comparisons were not reproduced as described, and Google does not publish a controlled comparison of the same chip designed with and without AlphaChip. The production use is attested by Google; the size of the whole-chip benefit is not established by a public controlled test.</p>
<p>AlphaEvolve is the closest thing to a language model writing chip logic. Announced by Google DeepMind on May 14, 2025, with a white paper on arXiv on June 16, 2025, it pairs Gemini models with automated evaluators in an evolutionary loop. For hardware, it proposed a Verilog rewrite that removed unnecessary bits in a heavily optimized arithmetic circuit inside a TPU matrix-multiplication unit. Google's designers validated the change and slated it for an upcoming TPU. The honest caveat comes from the paper itself: downstream synthesis tools independently caught the same simplification, so AlphaEvolve's contribution was surfacing the change earlier in the design flow, in engineers' native Verilog, not discovering an optimization the tools could never find. The same system sped up a matrix-multiplication kernel used in Gemini training by 23 percent on average, which Google says cut Gemini's overall training time by about 1 percent.</p>
<p>Jalapeno is OpenAI's first custom inference chip, and the first place its models touched production silicon. OpenAI started using GPT-o3 mini to help design it in early 2025. Across the broader effort, the team estimates model-proposed changes reduced standard-cell area by about 13 percent in the vector unit, which manages parallel computing tasks, and 10 percent in the matrix unit. The examples are concrete: one multiplier redesign used about 15 percent less cell area in synthesis while reducing latency from two clock cycles to one, and a separate adder redesign reduced cell area by about 15 percent while keeping its two-cycle latency. The optimizations were silicon-proven during Jalapeno's bring-up, and OpenAI's August 2026 silicon update put the chip from design start to tapeout in nine months.</p>
<p>The AMD work, reported by OpenAI on November 18, 2026, is the newest and the largest single number in the table. Working with AMD, OpenAI's models rewrote SystemVerilog RTL, the code that describes the chip's circuits, and achieved up to 14 percent improvement in power, performance and area on flagship GPU IP, against baselines AMD engineers had already heavily optimized. OpenAI calls that roughly a year of progress achieved in less than five months. AMD has already included some of these changes in a recent design it sent for manufacturing, and added others to its production design files for future versions. AMD also verified single-digit percentage PPA gains across key blocks of its upcoming next-generation flagship CPU, using designs written by the model. The full detail is in <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/openais-models-found-14-in-amds.html">OpenAI's Models Found 14% in AMD's Flagship GPU Design</a>.</p>
<p>To measure how chip-design capability changes from one model generation to the next, OpenAI tested five model generations on two open-source 32-bit RISC-V cores, PicoRV32 and Ibex, running 9,881 scored runs across both tasks, both cores and all token budgets. At a comparable budget of about 112,000 tokens per run, GPT-6.2 Astra had the highest average score on both cores, and GPT-6.1 Sol also outscored GPT-5.6 Sol on both. The model behind those scores is the one we covered in <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/gpt-62-astra-first-model-rated-high-for.html">GPT-6.2 Astra: First Model Rated High for Self-Improvement</a>. OpenAI itself flags the limit: models designing from written requirements could omit features found in the original cores, such as performance counters, which did little for throughput on the test workloads. These are 32-bit embedded cores, not flagship silicon, and the difference between those worlds is the subject of our comparison in <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/risc-v-vs-arm-vs-x86-for-ai-workloads.html">RISC-V vs ARM vs x86 for AI Workloads: Engineer's Guide</a>.</p>
<p>Claude Mythos is the fastest claim in the table and the least verified. Anthropic co-founder and chief compute officer Tom Brown told investors in a November 10, 2026 roadshow video that the Mythos preview model completed the software work needed to run Claude on AMD Instinct MI355X accelerators in about three days, with almost no human intervention, writing or porting nearly 100 percent of the kernels. The baseline Anthropic cites is six to twelve months of work by dozens of engineers. Two limits matter. The three days covered bringing prompt processing, the prefill phase, for a single model up to usable performance; whether the response-generation phase was included has not been disclosed. And the figures are company claims from investor materials, not independently verified. What is independently confirmed is narrower: AMD chair and chief executive Lisa Su recounted that when she asked whether AMD engineers were assisting Anthropic, staff confirmed Anthropic needed no help, and Anthropic's S-1/A filing of November 10, 2026 confirms Claude is trained and served across AWS Trainium, Google TPUs, Nvidia GPUs and AMD GPUs. Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang has pushed back, saying Nvidia already uses AI on its own kernels and calling Anthropic's case "a unique instance, not a trend". The next test is dated: Anthropic's first AMD MI450 chips are due in Helios racks in the first half of 2027.</p>

<h2>How to read the claims</h2>
<p>Three numbers in this table look comparable and are not. PPA, power, performance and area combined, is a design-quality score, not a speed figure. Cell-area cuts are about silicon footprint, not whole-chip performance. Wirelength cuts are block-level placement quality, not a faster processor. A 14 percent PPA gain does not mean a 14 percent faster graphics card, and a 6.2 percent wirelength cut does not mean a 6.2 percent faster TPU. Each number is real inside its own metric, and misleading outside it.</p>
<p>The verification ladder is the other thing to read. A peer-reviewed paper with a reproducibility dispute sits above a company blog post with partner confirmation, which sits above a roadshow claim with no independent check. Production silicon is the strongest evidence in the table: Jalapeno's changes ran on real chips, and AMD put some of OpenAI's changes into a design sent for manufacturing. The weakest is also the most dramatic: three days against twelve months, from the company selling the story, with the scope limited to one phase of one model. That does not make it false. It makes it the claim most in need of the repeat test, which has a date: the first half of 2027, when MI450 chips arrive in Helios racks.</p>
<h2>What comes next</h2>
<p>OpenAI is working with Synopsys to train GPT-Synopsys, a model meant to use Synopsys electronic design automation tools the way expert chip designers do. That partnership is the clearest sign of where this goes next: from one-off demonstrations on a vendor's hardware to a model trained inside the tools every chip team already uses. If that works, the question stops being which lab can show the biggest percentage and becomes how much of the design flow a model can be trusted with, and who checks its work. The scoreboard above is the baseline for that question, and we will update it as new results land.</p>
