<p>In 2026, the services the AI economy runs on kept going dark. A tracker that reads operator status pages counts 39 major cloud and internet-infrastructure incidents this year, through November 11. We read the postmortems and status pages behind the biggest ones and built the ledger: 13 major outages, nine with fully timed durations that add up to about 88 hours, and one cause behind most of it.</p>

Four rows have no closed duration, because the operator never published one or the incident was still open at our cutoff, and those rows are excluded from the 88-hour total. The total is our own arithmetic, and we label it as such wherever it appears.</p>

<p>Two rows need a note. The ledger has 13 rows, and the table above shows 13. Nine of them carry a closed duration, and those nine add up to 87.9 hours, which we round to about 88. The other four, Claude in March, Claude in August, Microsoft 365, and Claude on November 13, are listed with the duration the operator actually gave, which is none, or "still open." Adding an estimate for them would make the total a guess, so we did not. One row needs a note on method: Cloudflare's postmortem states a total of 6 hours 7 minutes from 17:48 UTC, while its event table runs 17:56 to 23:03. We use the operator's headline total, as we do for every row.</p>

<h2>The pattern: power and cooling</h2>
<p>Four of the nine timed rows share one cause, and those four rows account for most of the year's downtime. A thermal event in AWS us-east-1 in May ran 21 hours 30 minutes to majority recovery. A three-millisecond sag on a utility feed in Google Cloud's europe-west4-a region in July tripped breakers, dropped a chiller controller offline, and left a server hall at 44 degrees C for 14 hours 55 minutes. Lightning locked the cooling out of Azure West US 2 the same month for 22 hours 6 minutes, the longest single outage in this ledger. And a power event in one London zone took Google Cloud down for about 7 hours 52 minutes on October 20, hitting banks, couriers, and court live streams. Together, power and cooling account for 66.4 of the 87.9 timed hours, or about three quarters of it.</p>
<p>That is not a coincidence. It is what happens when compute density rises faster than the power and cooling around it. The July Google incident is the clearest example: one diesel rotary backup took the load and the other did not, two server rows moved to the surviving feed, the third went dark, and the spare chiller was out for construction. Every layer had a backup except the one that failed. The October London incident is the same story at smaller scale: a power event in one of three zones, mitigated in about five and a half hours, resolved for most customers in about eight.</p>
<div class="separator" style="clear: both; text-align: center;"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bh-img-assets/byteherald-images/main/img/pillar-outage-ledger-midday1113-chart2.png" alt="Bar chart of timed outage hours by cause: power and cooling 66.4 hours, config and automation 6.1, shared dependency 5.6, maintenance and break-fix 5.0, cause not yet stated 4.9" /></div>
<p>The rest of the timed hours split almost evenly. Config and automation accounts for 6.1 hours, the February Cloudflare incident in which a cleanup task asked for prefixes pending deletion, passed an empty value, and received every prefix instead, deleting about 1,100 of them before an engineer stopped it. Shared dependency accounts for 5.6 hours: the September 3 failure that took four AI services down together, and Cloudflare's Durable Objects errors on November 12. Maintenance and break-fix accounts for 5.0 hours, the July Azure West US incident in which a repair on one optical device expanded to every optical device leaving a datacenter. And 4.9 hours carry no stated cause at all, OpenAI's November 3 file-upload errors, for which no incident review had been read at the time the tracker logged it.</p>
<h2>The week that broke the pattern's back</h2>
<p>November 12 and 13 packed three ledger rows into two days, and they were not independent. Microsoft 365 went down on November 12, knocking out Outlook, Teams, and Copilot for users in 74 countries, and stayed down into November 13, stretching into Asia-Pacific business hours. Claude went down twice on November 13, first an authentication failure from 09:58 to 11:58 UTC, then degraded performance from 13:09 UTC, resolved at 15:38 UTC. And the third problem was the link between them: Claude's Microsoft 365 connector, the piece that lets Claude read mail, calendars, SharePoint, and Teams chats, has been failing since 17:52 UTC on November 12, because of the Microsoft 365 incident itself. We published <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/microsoft-365-outage-hits-74-countries.html">the full Microsoft 365 timeline</a> and <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/claude-went-down-twice-in-one-day-here.html">the full Claude timeline</a> on the morning of November 13, and this ledger adds the year's context those timelines could not carry.</p>
<p>Cloudflare was degrading the same week, with Durable Objects errors in North America and Workers API availability issues on November 13. The year's worst week for outages was also the week in which the services most visibly depend on one another. That is the ledger's second pattern, and it is the one that should worry a buyer more than the hours do.</p>
<h2>The shared-dependency lesson</h2>
<p>September 3 is the shortest row in the ledger and the most instructive. Claude, ChatGPT, Codex, and Grok failed within the same half hour, and the cause was not any one AI company. It was a problem at Cloudflare, the infrastructure in front of many websites and apps. Switching providers did not help, because the providers shared the same dependency. The lesson the morning's Claude post drew from November 13 is the same lesson, stated the other way: keep a second provider available for time-sensitive work, because the fallback can fail on the same day as the primary.</p>
<p>That is the test this ledger is really running. Every outage has a cause, and most causes are boring: power, cooling, a config push, a repair that went wide. The question that decides how much an outage costs you is not what broke. It is what else breaks with it. A buyer who runs two AI providers behind one Cloudflare account, or two cloud regions behind one power feed, has one provider, not two. The ledger's rows keep proving it, and the proof keeps arriving in the same week as the promise that this time the redundancy is real.</p>
<h2>Three checks before you depend on one provider</h2>
<p>First, read the status page, not the marketing page. A provider's status history shows how often it fails, how long it takes to recover, and whether it publishes a postmortem. Anthropic's status page, for example, showed 15 Claude incidents in the last 30 days, about 37 hours of disruption, before November 13 added two more. That number is more useful than any uptime percentage, because it counts what actually happened.</p>
<p>Second, ask what your fallback shares with your primary. If both go through the same content delivery network, the same identity provider, or the same region, they will fail together. September 3 proved it for four AI services at once. November 12 proved it for Claude's Microsoft 365 connector, which failed because the service it connects to failed.</p>
<p>Third, ask what the provider has not said yet. Four rows in this ledger carry no stated cause, and the Google Cloud London postmortem was still unpublished when ThinkReview checked on October 21, 2026. An unpublished cause is not a resolved cause. It is a risk you are carrying without a price on it.</p>
<h2>What is missing from this ledger</h2>
<p>This ledger covers the 13 biggest outages of 2026 by blast radius, not all 39 major incidents the tracker counts. Sentry, GitHub, Render, DigitalOcean, Discord, Netlify, Notion, Figma, DENIC, and npm all appear in that 39, and their incidents matter to the developers who depend on them. We did not include them because this ledger is about the cloud and AI services the AI economy runs on, and because each row needs a duration and a cause from a primary source, which the smaller incidents often lack.</p>
<p>One date trap deserves a correction in plain sight. The famous October 20 AWS outage, the DynamoDB DNS failure that hit 140 services and generated more than 11 million Downdetector reports, happened on October 20, 2025, not 2026. It is not in this ledger. The October 20, 2026, event is the Google Cloud London power cut, one year to the day later. Search results mix the two, and any ledger that does not say so will be read as claiming both.</p>
<p>Our method is public: we count only what operators and trackers published, we exclude rows without a closed duration from the total, and we label our arithmetic as ours. The full method lives on <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/p/methodology.html">our methodology page</a>, and any correction to this ledger will be logged publicly there.</p>
