<p>The newest models from four of China's top AI labs carry a fingerprint in their published weights, and a forensic analysis published November 12, 2026, says that fingerprint matches Nvidia's Blackwell chips, hardware the United States does not allow to be exported to China. The evidence is technical, it is strong, and the report itself says it proves no law was broken.</p>

<h2>How a fingerprint survives in the weights</h2>
<p>The analysis comes from Will DePue, a former OpenAI researcher who worked on Sora and GPT-5 and now publishes through the nonprofit Unemployment Labs. His method starts with a fact about how models are built. Before training begins, software assigns starting values to a model's weights, using a number called a seed to drive a pseudorandom generator. The same seed and the same settings reproduce the same sequence every time. PyTorch, the framework most major models are built with, splits that work across parallel threads on the graphics processor, and the number of threads depends on the chip's number of processing units, called streaming multiprocessors, or SMs.</p>
<p>That division of labor leaves a trace. A chip with more SMs assigns the same job to more workers, so the same seed's numbers land in different positions in the weight files. Recovering both the seed and the arrangement can therefore identify the hardware layout that generated the starting values. Some weights survive training untouched, including values reserved for symbols never encountered during training, and those preserved rows can be compared directly against values regenerated from a candidate seed and thread count. DePue searched roughly six trillion seed-and-layout combinations, then tested the recovered settings on weights held out of the search.</p>
<p>The method was checked against what labs have already disclosed. For 24 specific models, the developer has publicly said what hardware was used, and in every case the method agrees with the announcement. The report is careful about that check: 11 of the 24 involve step-zero checkpoints and related models are not independent, so it calls the result a consistency check rather than a measured accuracy rate.</p>
<h2>The six models, and how certain each match is</h2>
<p>Six models released since April 2026 read as Blackwell, while the same labs' earlier models read as Hopper, Nvidia's previous generation. The split is the story: every lab in the report switched generations in the same year, and the switch is visible in the weights themselves.</p>

<p>The method cannot distinguish chips within a group: a B200 from a B300, or a GB200 from a GB300. What it can do is separate the groups, because Hopper chips have 132 SMs, B200 and B300 chips have 148, and GB200 and GB300 chips have 152. The earlier models from the same four labs all read as Hopper: DeepSeek-V3 in December 2024, Kimi K2 in July 2025 and K2.5 in January 2026, MiniMax-M2.7 in April 2026, and Ling-3.0-flash in August 2026. The newest readable models from five other Chinese labs, Alibaba, Zhipu, Tencent, Xiaomi, and StepFun, all still read as Hopper. The switch is not universal. It is specific to these four labs, and it happened between January and November 2026.</p>

<h2>The numbers behind the claim</h2>
<p>The matches are far stronger than chance would explain. Across the roughly six trillion combinations searched, the strongest chance match would be expected to reach about 8 sigma, a measure of how far a result stands above ordinary random variation. The strongest evidence for each of the six models ranges from 49 to 1,385 sigma. Even the weakest individual test the report gives reaches 20 sigma. The physical checks are the sharpest part: DePue recovered the same seed in DeepSeek-V3 and V4-Flash, then ran unmodified PyTorch on two physical Nvidia chips. An older Hopper H100 reproduced V3's preserved values. A Blackwell B300 reproduced 445 of V4-Flash's 469 selected weight rows bit for bit. Neither chip reproduced the other model's rows, and changing the seed by one broke every match. A rented GB200 reproduced all 13 preserved rows tested for Kimi K3.</p>
<p>There is one alternative the report names, and it matters. A specially configured AMD MI300X, run with only 148 or 152 of its usual 304 compute units active, can produce the same arrangements. DePue tested a physical MI300X in that configuration and reproduced the layouts. For DeepSeek-V4-Flash, a separate arithmetic difference between Nvidia and AMD settled it: among 377 preserved values for which the vendors produce different answers, 363 matched Nvidia and none matched AMD. For the other five models that distinction is unresolved, and the report considers AMD very unlikely because reproducing the pattern would require an unusual, consistent configuration across the initializing cluster. AMD hardware is also restricted from export to China, so the alternative does not change the export-control question. It changes only which vendor's chips were used.</p>
<p>The report also sizes the training runs, and it is unusually honest about the uncertainty. Its best estimates put Kimi K3 at about 4,500 Blackwell GPUs, DeepSeek-V4-Pro at about 3,000, and V4-Flash, MiniMax-M3, and Ling-3.1-flash at about 1,500 each, or about 12,000 across the five runs, with a range of 7,000 to 22,000. The largest cluster would cost roughly 200 to 300 million dollars at about 70,000 dollars per GPU, and the five together more than half a billion. The report says, in its own words, "We are not confident in these estimates," because they rest on assumptions the weights cannot confirm: how efficiently each lab used its GPUs, how many tokens each model was trained on, and how long each run lasted. Two disclosed runs on the same hardware show the spread: Mistral says it trained Mistral Large 4 on 3,800 Grace Blackwell GPUs, while Microsoft says it trained MAI-Base-2, a model of similar size trained on more data, on 16,000 in 38 days.</p>
<h2>What the report does not show</h2>
<p>The limits are stated in the report itself, and they are the most important part of the story. The fingerprints identify how the starting weights were created. They do not establish where the chips were located, who owned them, or how access was obtained. Blackwell systems can be rented at data centers outside China. The report's own words: the findings "provide technical evidence of access to advanced computing hardware, but do not establish where the chips were located, how access was obtained, or whether export controls were violated." DePue's post on X called the evidence "conclusive forensic evidence" that the labs built their newest models on Blackwell chips. That is his claim about the hardware. The report does not extend it to a claim about the law.</p>
<p>Two details keep the picture honest. Kimi K3.1-Pro starts from the same seed as K3 and may be a checkpoint of K3 with further post-training, so six released models need not represent six independent training runs. And the method needs public weights that retain a recognizable pattern: DePue reports recovering no such signature in DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, which means the absence of a fingerprint is not evidence of absence.</p>
<h2>Why this lands now</h2>
<p>Officials and press reports had already alleged that DeepSeek and Moonshot were using Blackwell chips, without publishing technical evidence. The Information reported in December 2025 that DeepSeek acquired about 2,000 Blackwell chips. On July 22, 2026, White House science and technology policy director Michael Kratsios alleged that Moonshot had acquired GB300-equipped servers and accessed GB300s in Thailand, likely to train its models. Nvidia's own filing for the quarter ended July 26, 2026, lists B200 and GB200 NVL72 among Blackwell products requiring export licenses for China, and the report notes that no such licenses have been reported.</p>
<p>The report also arrives in the middle of a fast-moving enforcement story. Democrats in Congress <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/democrats-open-nvidia-inquiry-over-h200.html">opened an inquiry on November 12, 2026, into Nvidia's H200 sales to China</a>, and our <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/the-ai-chip-smuggling-ledger-3-billion.html">ledger of charged AI chip diversion cases</a> counts about 3.02 billion dollars across five cases. The fingerprint report is a different kind of evidence from both: not a charge, not a letter, but a measurement anyone can repeat from the public weights. That is what makes it hard to dismiss and easy to misuse. The measurement says four labs got access to Blackwell hardware. Everything after that, where the chips sat, who paid for them, and whether any rule was broken, still needs evidence the weights cannot give.</p>
