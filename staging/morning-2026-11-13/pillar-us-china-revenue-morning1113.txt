<p>Six leading Chinese AI companies earn about 11 billion dollars a year between them. OpenAI and Anthropic earn about 105 billion dollars. That is the finding of <a href="https://epoch.ai/publications/how-do-chinese-ai-companies-make-money">Epoch AI</a>, a US research nonprofit, in an analysis published October 6, 2026, and the gap has widened since: Anthropic's own stock filing puts its run rate at about 81 billion dollars on October 31, up 10 billion dollars in a single month. This article audits the gap, company by company, and explains why it is a pricing story, not a demand story.</p>

<p>The two American labs total about 105 billion dollars. The six Chinese firms total about 11 billion dollars, which is about 10 percent of the American total, or roughly nine and a half times smaller. ByteDance alone is about 36 percent of the Chinese total. The four model-focused firms, Z.ai, Moonshot, DeepSeek and MiniMax, add up to only 4.6 billion dollars. A second, independent estimate points the same way: Rhodium Group, cited by CNBC on September 17, 2026, put total Chinese AI model revenue at about 10.7 billion dollars, against 40 billion for OpenAI and 65 billion for Anthropic. The <a href="https://www.stateof.ai/State-of-AI-Report-2026.pdf">State of AI Report 2026</a>, published October 8, 2026, by investor Nathan Benaich of Air Street Capital, adds the trajectory: the two American labs' combined run rates rose from about 30 billion dollars at the start of 2026 to 105 billion by late summer.</p>

<h2>The October update widens the gap</h2>
<p>Epoch AI's table is a September snapshot, and October made it staler in one direction. Anthropic's amended stock filing of November 10, 2026, puts its run-rate revenue at about 81 billion dollars as of October 31, up 10 billion dollars from about 71 billion on September 30. The same filing reports 33.2 billion dollars of revenue in the first nine months of 2026, including 17.0 billion in the third quarter, up 47 percent on the second quarter. Two pieces of ByteHerald arithmetic follow, and both are labeled as ours. First, Anthropic alone, at 81 billion dollars, now earns about 7.4 times the six Chinese firms combined. Second, the 10 billion dollars Anthropic added in October alone is about 91 percent of those six firms' combined annual total. The comparison mixes months, September estimates against an October 31 run rate, so it is not like-for-like, but the direction is unambiguous: the gap grew in October, it did not shrink.</p>
<p>OpenAI's side of the ledger is thinner in public data. Epoch AI uses a 40 billion dollar run rate from August 2026, and The Information reported in September 2026 that DeepSeek's annualized revenue run rate had doubled within a few months to reach 1 billion dollars. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/anthropics-644b-ipo-file-every-number.html">Our audit of Anthropic's filing</a> has the full set of numbers behind the 81 billion dollar figure, including the share count and valuation math. The point for this article is narrower: even before October, the American lead was about ten to one, and the only hard new number since then points the same way.</p>
<h2>Why the gap is a pricing story, not a demand story</h2>
<p>Chinese models are widely used. Open models account for more than 70 percent of token usage on OpenRouter, the marketplace where developers buy AI by usage, according to <a href="https://asia.nikkei.com/business/technology/artificial-intelligence/deepseek-other-china-ai-firms-have-just-10-the-revenue-of-openai-anthropic">Nikkei Asia</a>. ByteDance's Doubao has about 380 million monthly active users, DeepSeek about 130 million, and Qwen about 170 million, according to QuestMobile data cited by Epoch AI. The problem is not demand. The problem is that Chinese labs mostly give their model weights away, and once weights are public, anyone can host the model and sell the same thing.</p>
<p>Epoch AI's sharpest evidence is a case study of Z.ai's GLM 5.3 Flash. On August 26, 2026, Z.ai listed the model on OpenRouter at a 50 percent promotional discount and released its weights at the same time. Within a day there were twelve competitors serving the model. By September 15 there were 26, eight of which undercut Z.ai's own price. Z.ai's share of the model's OpenRouter tokens collapsed from 88 percent to 22 percent in 20 days, and its daily token volume fell 70 percent even as the model's total volume grew 17 percent. Its largest rival ended up serving more tokens than Z.ai did. The same pattern shows up across the board: as of September 16, 2026, only about 25 percent of all GLM-5 tokens were served through Z.ai's own API, and only 28.4 percent of DeepSeek V4 Pro tokens were served by DeepSeek. By contrast, a closed-weight model such as Anthropic's Fable is almost always sold at the same list price, 10 dollars per million input tokens, whether it comes from Claude's API platform or Amazon Bedrock, because no rival can serve the same weights more cheaply.</p>

<h2>Five ways Chinese firms make money</h2>
<p>Epoch AI traces five revenue streams, and the numbers show why none of them closes the gap yet. First, consumer apps. They attract huge audiences and thin margins. MiniMax's apps averaged 27.6 million monthly active users but only 1.77 million paying users in the first nine months of 2025, about 6 percent, and the gross margin on those apps was only 4.7 percent, against 69.4 percent for its enterprise platform. Advertisements inside Talkie, MiniMax's companion app, generated 20.9 percent of the company's total revenue in the same period. The low conversion is not unique to China: The Information reports that only 5 percent of ChatGPT's roughly 900 million weekly active users paid in 2025, while OpenAI spent 3.9 billion dollars on inference for nonpaying users. The difference is price: ByteDance's Doubao subscription costs about 9.60 dollars a month, and MiniMax's video package starts at 7.99 dollars, against 20 dollars a month for ChatGPT Plus.</p>
<p>Second, selling model access, which is where the pricing problem bites hardest. Z.ai's gross margins on its API and developer platform were 18.9 percent in 2025 and 24.6 percent in the first half of 2026. For comparison, SemiAnalysis estimates that Opus 4.8 earned about 85 percent API-serving gross margins at first-party list prices by mid-2026, and that 75 to 85 percent of Anthropic's revenue comes from selling API tokens. The one outlier is DeepSeek, whose V4 API gross margin is estimated at 70 to 80 percent, because it spends more than 70 percent of its computing capacity on training rather than inference and keeps inference demand deliberately low. Third, enterprise and government customization, which earns real margins but scales badly. Z.ai's on-premise deployments carried a 48.8 percent gross margin in 2025, but its own interim report says delivery and maintenance costs are rigid, and the on-premise share of its revenue fell from 73.7 percent in 2025 to 13.5 percent in the first half of 2026 as it shifted toward standardized cloud services.</p>
<p>Fourth, licensing fees, which barely exist as a revenue line today. Moonshot released Kimi K3's weights in July 2026 under a modified MIT license that requires a separate agreement if a licensee's model-as-a-service revenue exceeds 20 million dollars in any twelve months. Moonshot is seeking up to 30 percent of K3-related revenue on Microsoft Azure, Amazon Web Services and Google Cloud, according to Reuters, while SemiAnalysis estimates Anthropic's revenue share on Bedrock at about 75 percent, with a gross margin of only about 45 percent after Amazon's infrastructure fee. Z.ai has signed revenue-sharing agreements with cloud providers, including Amazon's integration of GLM-5.3 into Bedrock in October 2026, but the terms are undisclosed. Fifth, indirect revenue, which only the conglomerates can capture. Alibaba's AI cloud and compute revenue was 7.1 billion dollars in the second quarter of 2026, up 45 percent year over year, and its AI-related product revenue reached 1.8 billion dollars in the same quarter, the twelfth straight quarter of triple-digit growth. ByteDance's Volcano Engine handled 49.5 percent of China's public-cloud large-model token calls in 2025, according to IDC. Seedance, ByteDance's video model, made micro-drama production cheaper, and one industry estimate puts AI short-drama advertisers' spending on Douyin at roughly 1.6 billion dollars a year, a figure built on an assumption about average daily spending and labeled here as an estimate, not a measured total.</p>
<h2>What would close the gap</h2>
<p>Epoch AI's own conclusion is that Chinese firms traded short-term monetization for broader model diffusion, releasing weights to spread adoption, and that the trade has a cost. Closing the gap needs one of three things, and the evidence suggests all three are hard. Capability parity would let Chinese labs charge American prices, but Epoch AI notes their models are less capable and their businesses younger. Pricing power would need either closed weights, which would surrender the diffusion advantage, or a differentiated hosting edge, which the GLM case study shows is hard to hold when 26 rivals can serve the same weights. Enterprise scale is the most plausible path: Z.ai's government and bank work, Alibaba's hospital and cloud deals, and ByteDance's financial modeling work all earn real money, but customization is labor-intensive and, as Z.ai's own numbers show, the margins shrink as it scales. The licensing experiments, Kimi K3's 20 million dollar threshold and the revenue-sharing deals with cloud providers, are the first attempts to reconcile diffusion with monetization, and their terms will be the thing to watch. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/ais-money-shot-which-labs-actually-make.html">Our earlier audit of which labs actually make money</a> reached a compatible conclusion from the American side: revenue concentrates where the weights stay closed and the API is the product.</p>
<h2>How to read the next revenue claim</h2>
<p>Four checks separate a solid revenue claim from a misleading one. First, run rate or revenue? A run rate annualizes a recent month, and Anthropic's own filing says it is not a substitute for revenue prepared under accounting rules or a forecast. Second, which months? Epoch AI's figures span June to September 2026, and the October update moved Anthropic's number by 10 billion dollars in a month. Third, model-as-a-service only, or the whole company? ByteDance's 4.0 billion and Alibaba's 2.4 billion cover model services, not their advertising and cloud businesses, which is why Alibaba's 7.1 billion dollar AI cloud quarter looks larger than its 2.4 billion dollar model figure. Fourth, who estimated it? The Chinese figures are estimates assembled from filings, OpenRouter data and Chinese-language sources, not audited financials, and the honest version of the gap carries those caveats in the open. The gap itself is real, and it is about ten to one. The interesting question is no longer whether it exists, but whether the licensing experiments and enterprise deals can narrow it before the next model generation arrives. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/novembers-ai-funding-ranked-179b-and.html">November's funding ledger</a> shows where the money is going while that question is answered: almost all of it to the two American labs. Our method for building audits like this one, including what counts as a run rate versus revenue and how the totals are calculated, is set out on our <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/p/methodology.html">methodology page</a>.</p>
