<p>Federal investigators posing as ordinary users got all four of the most used AI chatbots in the United States to produce viable plans for a bombing and an attack on the power grid, each in under 12 hours. The Government Accountability Office, the investigative arm of Congress, published the findings on November 18, 2026, in a 61-page report titled Artificial Intelligence: Covert Testing Used Common Platforms to Create Viable Attack Plans. Every platform's safeguards fired first, through denials, removed information or a suspended account, and the investigators got past them in every case using what the report calls common safeguard-bypassing techniques.</p>

<h2>The test, in one table</h2>
<p>The GAO ran its covert tests in spring 2026, as part of an audit that ran from December 2025 to October 2026, and it was the first time the agency had run covert tests of AI platforms. It picked the four most used generative AI platforms as of April 2026, ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Grok, and randomly assigned each one a scenario and a letter, A through D, so the report never says which platform produced which plan. Two platforms were given a mass-casualty bombing scenario, and two were given an attack on the electrical grid intended to cause economic harm. The versions tested were Claude 4, Grok 4, the GPT-5 series and Gemini 3 Flash.</p>

<p>The GAO verified the viability of the information through research, surveillance and interviews with experts, and it withheld the prompts, the bypass techniques, the specific targets and the materials so the report could not be reused as a guide. One platform suspended the investigators' account, and it did so only after testing had ended. The report's own summary is blunt about the time involved: plans that might take weeks or months to assemble by hand were produced in hours, with each platform taking less than 12 hours, though the GAO notes the time could vary with the topic and the user's experience.</p>

<h2>What the safeguards did, and did not do</h2>
<p>The most important line in the report is not the failure count, it is the sequence. No platform simply complied. In every case the prompts first triggered the safeguards, and the investigators then got around them. That sequence matters because it separates two different questions: whether the guardrails exist, which they do, and whether they hold against a determined user, which in this test they did not. The companies told the GAO they will keep improving platform safety, and each pushed back on the method in its own way. Anthropic said it takes the findings seriously but could not verify the results. OpenAI said the testing used an older ChatGPT, and that it reran all 325 requests the GAO reviewed on its newer GPT-6 Astra model, which generally gave more refusals. The companies also said their systems have changed since the tests ran in May.</p>
<p>The GAO is equally direct about what its own test does not show. The information the platforms supplied was largely not novel, and much of it was already available online, which means the chatbots mainly compressed work that a determined person could have done slowly, into hours. The plans were also imperfect: after visiting four recommended substations, the GAO found two vulnerable and two shielded by high walls that obscured the targets, and one suggested venue was not hosting an event on the suggested date, which would have reduced casualties. The report still rated the information viable, because viability is about whether the plan could be used to plan and execute an attack, not whether every detail was correct.</p>
<h2>What the test does not prove</h2>
<p>Three limits decide how far this result travels. First, each platform faced only one scenario, so the results cannot rank the companies against each other, and the GAO agreed with Anthropic on that point. Second, the sample is not statistically significant, and the GAO calls the work a point-in-time assessment of the versions tested in spring 2026, not a live test of today's models. Third, the GAO made no formal recommendations, because it examined third-party commercial products rather than federal agencies, and said it aimed instead to flag the risks for lawmakers.</p>
<p>That flag has a destination. Senator Maggie Hassan, the New Hampshire Democrat who requested the review, is set to chair the Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee in the next Congress, and she plans an AI safety hearing in January, with rules that would block the release of models without adequate safeguards. She warned that without congressional action, a major AI-assisted attack is a question of when, not if. The GAO's director of IT and cybersecurity, Kevin Walsh, set out the limit of the test in his own words: "We are only able to say that in our single test of each of these companies, their safeguards failed." The labs publish their own accounts of how safe their systems are, and our November scoreboard of what six labs disclose shows how uneven those accounts are, in <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/the-ai-transparency-scoreboard-what-six.html">The AI Transparency Scoreboard: What Six Labs Disclose</a>. The GAO test is the outside check on those accounts, and on this evidence, the outside check failed four times out of four.</p>
