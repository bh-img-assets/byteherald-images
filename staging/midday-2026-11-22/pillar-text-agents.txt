<h2>What the field looks like</h2>
<p>Most of these agents cost nothing to try. Sixteen of the 24 are free to start, three charge from day one, and five do not publish a price at all. The three that charge immediately are Ask Proxy, Martin, and Tomo, and their prices cluster between 20 and 40 dollars a month. At the other end, Poke's Ultra plan at 199 dollars a month and Iris's Pro plan at 99 dollars a month are the most expensive published tiers, aimed at heavy users who want frontier models for every action. The free tier is the default in this category, not the exception, which means the real cost is not money. It is the access you hand over: your inbox, your calendar, and your messages.</p>
<div class="separator" style="clear: both; text-align: center;"><img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bh-img-assets/byteherald-images/main/img/pillar-text-agents-chart1.png" alt="Chart: price tiers of the 24 text-message agents, 16 free to start, 5 with no published price, 3 paid from day one" /></div>
<p>The channel split is just as telling. Twelve of the 24 live on iMessage, making it the home of this category, followed by plain SMS or text at 10, and WhatsApp, Telegram, and voice calls at 7 each. Most agents offer more than one channel, so the counts overlap, but the pattern is clear: if you live on iPhone, nearly every agent will meet you there, while Android users get SMS, RCS, and WhatsApp instead. Poke covers the widest spread, with iMessage, SMS, WhatsApp, Telegram, and RCS documented, and Wajo's Fo covers the widest overall, with web, iMessage, SMS, RCS, Telegram, Slack, and phone calls, but no WhatsApp. The channel you already live in matters more than the feature list, because an assistant you have to open a new app to reach is an assistant you will stop using.</p>

<h2>The catches</h2>
<p>Five rows in the table deserve a second look before anyone signs up. Underdog is listed among text-message agents, but its own site is clear that it is a desktop-only, invite-only beta with no text channel yet, and phone apps are still coming. It does not currently live in your text messages at all. Skye's homepage is a tagline plus an App Store badge, with no price, channel, or status stated anywhere readable, and Stanley's site returned no readable content, so neither can be judged beyond the name. Iris moved: the domain in TechCrunch's list, iris-agent.co, now shows a different product called Raisi, and the texting assistant lives at irisai.app. Folk's price changed too: TechCrunch listed a Pro plan at 8.33 dollars a month, but the official site now says the service is free with a daily message allowance and lists no Pro tier. Finally, two of the most talked-about agents are the hardest to get: Instinct is invite-only with a waitlist, and Tab is in private beta with no published price. The field is real, but a third of it is either gated, thinly documented, or not actually texting yet.</p>
<h2>How to choose</h2>
<p>Start with the channel, not the brand. If you live on iMessage, Poke and Pally are the two with free plans, full channel coverage, and no waitlist, and Poke is the only one of the 24 that Apple approved for its Messages for Business platform. If you live on WhatsApp, Folk, Pally, Poke, Rene, Tab, and Town all meet you there, with Pally and Poke free to start. If the job is family life, Fambot, Ohai, and Orbits are built for it, with nightly digests, shared calendars, and meal planning. If the job is travel, Miso is the only specialist, though it publishes no price. If the job is work, Town and Comma are the two built for teams, with Slack, email, and routines. If you want the agent in group chats, Tomo and Tab are designed for it, and Pally plans in group chats too. And if you want an agent that spends money for you, check the approval rule before anything else: Maverick, Tab, and Wajo's Fo all ask before they send, book, or pay, which is the difference between an assistant and a liability.</p>
<h2>Before you hand over your inbox</h2>
<p>A texting agent is the most access you will ever give a piece of software. It reads your messages, your calendar, and your email, and in several cases it holds its own phone number, email address, and payment card. That is why identity matters: Wajo gives Fo its own email, phone number, and card so it can deal with businesses without handing over your credentials, and Instinct gives each user a dedicated email address for the same reason, which is the agent-identity question <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/ai-agents-need-id-agent-identity-stack.html">our agent identity comparison mapped across the whole field</a>. Privacy matters too: Underdog runs entirely on your own device, keeping personal data off other companies' servers, while the rest of the field stores your messages in the cloud, and <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/the-zero-data-retention-audit-who-keeps.html">our retention audit shows which providers keep your prompts and for how long</a>. And the shopping question is still open: agents that buy things on your behalf raise the question of who gets paid when the buyer is software, which <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/when-your-ai-agent-buys-something-who.html">our agentic commerce analysis priced layer by layer</a>, and the first real test of that question, the agentic Black Friday, is already showing that shoppers use AI to find things and still buy them themselves, as <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/the-first-agentic-black-friday-is-off.html">our report on the first agentic Black Friday found</a>. The right question before you pick one is not which agent is smartest. It is which one you would trust with your inbox, your calendar, and your card, and what it does with them when you are not watching.</p>
