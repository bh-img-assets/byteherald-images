<p><a href="https://openai.com/index/perplexity-and-intelligence/">OpenAI's chief scientist published the lab's internal pretraining numbers on November 22, 2026, and used them to propose something no frontier lab has agreed to before: shared limits on AI development, measured by a single number, that no developer would cross until safety monitoring catches up.</a> In a post titled "Perplexity and Intelligence," Jakub Pachocki disclosed the log-perplexity of OpenAI's models from GPT-3.5 onward, measured on the lab's main held-out dataset, and argued that perplexity should become the common yardstick for pacing AI progress. The numbers show a steep drop in 2026, an unreleased model already below the latest public one, and a warning that a similar avalanche of advances could arrive in deep learning research as soon as 2027.</p>

<h2>The numbers OpenAI disclosed</h2>
<p>Perplexity measures how well a model predicts the next token on text it has never seen. Lower is better. OpenAI tracks the log of perplexity, also called cross-entropy loss, on a held-out dataset drawn from its own internal codebase, the measure it has used since GPT-3.5. Pachocki's argument is that this one number tracks the raw intelligence a model gains during pretraining, and that the capabilities which later appear, from fluent conversation to chain-of-thought reasoning to open-math breakthroughs, show up as emergent consequences of pushing it down.</p>

<p>The chart in the post is the news. It shows loss falling steeply through 2026, and it ends with an unnamed internal model plotted below <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/gpt-62-astra-first-model-rated-high-for.html">GPT-6.2 Astra</a>, the latest public model, which sits at about 0.76. OpenAI attributes most of the 2026 acceleration to long-term research and compute investment, and only a smaller part to AI automating its own development. That matters because the post's warning is about the smaller part growing fast.</p>

<h2>What happened at 0.76</h2>
<p>For more than a year, OpenAI has been posing open problems in mathematics to its models as one way of evaluating them. Pachocki writes that once the lab upgraded to a model at about 0.76 log-perplexity, it started producing groundbreaking results, with resolutions to questions researchers had thought about for years arriving one after another in a single Slack thread. He calls the experience deeply surreal, and the point of telling the story is the extrapolation: if a single threshold crossing can do that in mathematics, a similar sudden transformation is plausible in deep learning research itself. The field relies more on experimentation than mathematics does, he writes, but it has a long history of simple insights being missed for years, so an avalanche of fundamental pretraining advances could conceivably arrive as soon as 2027. The same threshold logic runs through <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/two-riemann-proofs-in-one-week.html">the two Riemann proofs published in a single week</a>, where a Claude-assisted proof and a fully human proof landed days apart and the verification, not the claim, became the story.</p>
<h2>The 2027 warning, in Pachocki's own terms</h2>
<p>The post draws a line between two kinds of AI contribution to its own development. The familiar one is AI augmenting and replacing human researchers across the development cycle, ending at full recursive self-improvement, a state Pachocki says should obviously be avoided to maintain human control. The step change he says the usual framing misses is AI participating in algorithmic research on pretraining itself. That is where the math experience becomes a warning: progress there could arrive as a sudden jump rather than a steady climb, and alignment and monitoring work is unlikely to accelerate fast enough to keep up. Extrapolating from recent progress, he expects further transformative capabilities as models enter the 0.68 to 0.70 log-perplexity window, and he is explicit that he does not know what those capabilities will be and that OpenAI may not be testing for them today. At the same time, he expects further degradation in the dominant forms of alignment auditing and further increases in evaluation and training awareness, meaning models that know when they are being tested.</p>
<h2>The proposal: three parts, no numbers yet</h2>
<p>Pachocki's proposal has three parts. First, frontier developers would report perplexity openly, giving the field a shared number to track. Second, labs would jointly establish shared norms for how to evaluate very capable models and build red-team safety cases for them before first use, including shared transparency standards. Third, developers would commit to common limits, based on perplexity and other measurements, that they would not cross until the ability to understand and monitor AI behavior reaches a concrete milestone, such as a successor to chain-of-thought monitors, the tools that watch a model's written-out reasoning. The idea is an industry standard that fixes a first limit on development and spells out what must be achieved before anyone goes beyond it, then repeats the process for successive limits.</p>
<p>What the post does not do is just as important. It names no numeric limit, no timeline, and no lab that has signed on. Chief executive Sam Altman said on X that he hopes the field can agree to common limits and noted that labs committed under the White House Accords to set safety standards, but an endorsement is not an agreement. Pachocki himself lists four problems with perplexity as a shared metric: different labs use different evaluation datasets and training mixtures, so scores are hard to compare; perplexity does not correspond to any task people care about; it misses gains made outside pretraining, most notably chain-of-thought reasoning, which does not reduce perplexity at all; and nobody knows the lowest possible score, so as models approach the unknown floor, smaller gains on perplexity will correspond to larger leaps in intelligence. The post also notes that the held-out dataset is drawn from OpenAI's internal codebase, which is a good proxy for recent models but a poor one for older ones like GPT-2 and GPT-3. A shared limit needs a shared ruler, and the post concedes the ruler does not exist yet.</p>
<h2>What perplexity actually measures</h2>
<p>Perplexity is a score for how surprised a model is by real text. A model reads a passage it has never seen and assigns a probability to each next word; the better its predictions, the lower its perplexity. The reason it has survived as a metric while benchmarks keep getting saturated is that it does not depend on any particular test. The benchmarks used for GPT-3 were mostly maxed out by GPT-4, but pretraining loss kept falling and kept predicting new capabilities. The catch is the one Pachocki states plainly: a low score says a model predicts text well, not that it is safe, honest, or useful for any specific job. That is why the proposal pairs the number with safety milestones rather than treating the number alone as a green light. The test of this post is not whether other labs praise it. It is whether any of them publish their own numbers, and whether a first limit ever gets a number attached to it.</p>
