<p>Data centers were on the ballot in roughly 40 American towns on November 3, 2026, and voters reined them in in at least 33 of them. <a href="https://www.usatoday.com/story/news/nation/2026/11/04/data-centers-midterm-elections/92611809007/">About three-quarters of voters say they would oppose a new data center in their own community</a>, and 64 billion dollars of data center projects have now been blocked or delayed by local opposition. The backlash is the rare American political issue that cuts across party lines, it has already changed what gets built and where, and the industry's response so far has been three concessions that its critics call small.</p>

<h2>The backlash, counted</h2>
<p>The numbers come from four separate counts, and they agree with each other. Data Center Watch, which tracks opposition across 28 states, counts 18 billion dollars of projects blocked and 46 billion dollars delayed over the last two years, for 64 billion dollars in total. Centuri's November 2026 briefing counts about 980 active local moratoriums or restrictions, with about 80 percent enacted since April of this year, and 833 activist groups across 49 states. The ballot count is the sharpest: 44 local measures passed restrictions across 12 states, led by Ohio, and in Ohio itself voters in at least 18 communities weighed restrictions and approved all of them. At least 10 municipalities voted to ban large data centers, or those above 25 megawatts, a threshold that effectively prohibits the largest facilities. Two mayors, in Ohio and Oklahoma, were recalled before their terms ended after backlash over proposed data centers.</p>

<h2>Why it is bipartisan</h2>
<p>The opposition does not split along the usual lines. Republican officials tend to raise tax incentives and strain on the energy grid, while Democrats tend to focus on environmental impacts and resource consumption, according to Data Center Watch, and both sides are voting the same way. Florida governor Ron DeSantis and Vermont senator Bernie Sanders are both on record against data centers, a pairing that would have been hard to imagine for any other infrastructure issue. The reason, as journalist Paris Marx told TechCrunch in an interview about his book "Hyperscale," is that a data center is physical in a way a chatbot is not. It shows up in the power bill, in access to water, in the noise from rooftop air conditioning units, and in the quality of life of a specific community. In Ireland, data centers now consume more than 20 percent of the country's electricity. In The Dalles, Oregon, Google was revealed to be using 29 percent of the city's water. A Gallup poll put opposition at 71 percent, with 48 percent strongly opposed, and a Pew survey found 54 percent of Americans say data centers are bad for the environment, up from 39 percent in January 2026. Those are the numbers <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/trump-threatens-tremendous-tax-on-data.html">we reported when the president threatened a tremendous tax on data centers built abroad</a>, and the ballot results since then have matched the polls.</p>
<h2>What the industry conceded</h2>
<p>Tech companies have responded in three ways, and the interview makes clear how limited each one is. First, Microsoft, Amazon and OpenAI have all said they will no longer use non-disclosure agreements in negotiations with local governments, a change Marx calls welcome but not large, since secrecy was already working against the companies once residents learned a data center was coming. Second, companies are offering to pay for more of the electricity infrastructure their data centers require, to take pressure off ratepayers. Third, they are shifting from evaporative cooling systems, which consume large volumes of water, to closed-loop systems that recirculate the same water and use electricity to cool it instead. At the same time, the industry has quietly dropped the argument it made a year or two ago, that renewables and nuclear power would cover the new demand. Marx's reading is that the companies lost that argument and pivoted to water, where national figures about agriculture and golf courses can be used to make a local problem look small. The problem with that pivot is that water stress is local: some communities have water to spare and some do not, and the data centers are being built in both.</p>
<h2>What it means for the buildout</h2>
<p>The backlash has not stopped construction. Projects already under construction are largely unaffected, because moratoriums and governors' pledges target new approvals, for 2026 and 2027 and in some cases beyond. New York enacted the first statewide moratorium on new data centers, and a dozen other states, red, blue and purple, are considering similar bans, according to the Boston Review roundtable. Where governorships flipped in the midterms, the winners had campaigned on future regulation, with pause pledges in Texas, Georgia and Ohio and tax-break rollbacks in Nevada and Iowa. The constraint that matters is the one <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/the-grid-cant-keep-up-so-ai-labs-buy.html">our grid pillar mapped</a>: the power has to come from somewhere, and the communities being asked to host it are now organized, counted, and voting. The buildout continues, but it no longer happens quietly, and the next round of approvals will be fought in public, town by town.</p>
