<p><a href="https://interestingengineering.com/ai-robotics/amateur-astronomers-openai-49-planet-candidates">Signals that could be planets around distant stars have turned up in public Kepler and TESS data, found by two amateurs working with OpenAI's Codex and Astra</a>. OpenAI published the account on November 24, 2026. No planet is confirmed, and that gap is the point, not a flaw. Code can surface a signal. Only patient observation can say whether a planet is behind it.</p>

<h2>Two hunts, one method</h2>
<p>Bond is an engineer at OpenAI. He pointed Codex at Kepler's archive, observations from 2009 to 2018, and asked it to build a program that could hunt for the regular brightness dips a transiting planet leaves behind. The program worked through about 100,000 stars and turned up more than 200 signals. Ten of those matched planets astronomers had already confirmed, which told Bond the pipeline was catching real features instead of inventing them. Byrd runs an email-infrastructure startup, Primitive, and worked on his own with Astra, a second OpenAI tool. He had it survey the published methods for finding planets, then write two search programs, one for candidates in general and one aimed at stars where a planet could plausibly sit in the habitable zone, and run first-pass checks on what looked promising. His searches ran across about 19 million stars from TESS and other public archives. The first pass returned 49 candidates. The strongest is now in the hands of researchers on the TESS mission, and two more are queued to follow.</p>

<h2>The two candidates worth watching</h2>
<p>Two signals from Bond's hunt deserve a closer look. The first sits around KIC 5689121, a cooler star in Lyra roughly half the Sun's size, where the brightness dips on a 13.8-day cycle. The depth of that dip points to a planet about 2.4 times Earth's width, if a planet is what is causing it. The second is KIC 6691030, a dim star whose light fades every 4.8 days in a way that fits a planet smaller than Neptune. Neither is a discovery yet. The dips themselves are real, recorded features. Their cause is not settled, and settling it means follow-up observations that could take months or years.</p>
<h2>How the search actually works</h2>
<p>Both hunts relied on transits. When a planet crosses in front of its star, the star's brightness dips a little, on a regular schedule, and a search program hunts for dips that repeat. That lets a program sift whole archives for a repeating shape, which is also what makes it risky: a dip is not proof of a planet. Two stars circling each other dim one another on the same kind of rhythm, and a telescope's own glitches can leave marks that look like planets. So the tools were never asked to declare a planet. Their job was to build the search: survey the published methods, assemble the program, and test whether each change made the hunt better. Bond's setup split the work between two agents, one scanning light curves for promising signals and another re-checking the strongest candidates. The judgment about what the signals mean stayed human.</p>
<p>The scale is the point. Bond's program covered about 100,000 of the stars Kepler watched over nine years. TESS has swept far more. No amateur could read that much light-curve data by hand, and no professional team has the time to chase every weak signal in it. Anyone with enough technical skill can now write their own search instead of waiting for a professional team to look, and that opens up the faint signals earlier analyses passed over. NASA's Exoplanet Watch and the AAVSO's Exoplanet Observing Program already give volunteers ways to help collect and analyze data. Codex and Astra lower the barrier one step further, from collecting data to building the search itself.</p>
<h2>What would turn a signal into a planet</h2>
<p>SETI Institute gave the findings their first outside test. Bond brought his results to the institute, where associate director Vishal Gajjar searched the Kepler archive independently and turned up two signals that matched Bond's. Finding them twice does not make them planets. It does something narrower and still valuable: it rules out a mistake in one person's code as the cause. Byrd's strongest candidate is now with the TESS mission's researchers for a closer look. For any candidate, the next step is the same: ground-based telescopes watch the star again, checking whether the dip repeats on schedule, whether the star wobbles the way a planet's gravity would make it wobble, and whether some other cause fits better. Until that work is done, the candidates sit on a list alongside more than 6,400 confirmed exoplanets found since 1992, still waiting for a professional check.</p>
<p>The verification discipline here is the same one that applies to every AI-assisted science claim. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/two-riemann-proofs-in-one-week.html">Our November 20 pillar kept a verification ledger for two Riemann proofs in one week, one machine-checked by Lean and one all-human, and neither counted as verified until a human-readable check existed</a>. A transit signal is a claim about data, not a discovery, and it becomes a candidate only when an independent check reproduces it. Bond's signals passed that first check. The planet itself is still unproven.</p>
<h2>The caveats, stated plainly</h2>
<p>Three caveats belong in the story, not in a footnote. First, Bond works at OpenAI, and OpenAI is the one telling the story, so the company has a clear interest in making its tools look good. Second, the headline signal was already known to another volunteer: Kuber Mehta, also an amateur, had independently surfaced the same KIC 5689121 dip, so Bond's program re-found a signal someone else had already flagged. Third, a candidate is not a planet. Exoplanet hunting has a long record of signals that dissolved into binary stars, stellar activity, or instrument noise, and these candidates have not cleared that bar yet. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/openai-claims-three-science.html">Our October 27 pillar applied the same discipline to OpenAI's claimed breakthroughs in mathematics and physics, auditing each claim against what was actually public and verified</a>. The same treatment applies here: a genuine search, genuine signals, a genuine independent check, and no discovery yet.</p>
