<p>Tencent open-sourced DFly2, a speculative decoding drafter for natively sparse language models, with <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34776v1">a paper submitted to arXiv on November 25, 2026</a>. The headline number, 81 percent more throughput on DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, measures how many requests a deployment can serve at matched per-user decode speed on a 64K agentic set. It does not mean one user's answers arrive 81 percent faster. On the same target, DFly2's end-to-end agentic speedup averages 1.50x over the target alone, against 1.44x for the stronger baseline drafter, in Tencent's own tests.</p>

<h2>What DFly2 changes</h2>
<p>Speculative decoding speeds up generation by having a small drafter propose several tokens at once, which the large target model then verifies in one pass. The accepted length, the average number of tokens confirmed per step, sets the speed gain. Block-parallel drafters have traditionally kept a context cache of their own, either over the whole prompt or over a sliding window of recent tokens. Tencent measured the cost of that design on sparse targets: a full drafter cache adds three quarters to the KV cache per token on DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash and a fifth on Hy4 flash, and it also costs prefill time, prefix-cache hits, and decode concurrency.</p>
<p>DFly2 removes the drafter's cache entirely. Each draft layer reads the KV cache its paired target layer already stored, at the positions that layer's sparse indexer selected for the current query. Tencent measured how far one selection carries across a draft block: made one step early, it still covers close to nine tenths of what each later token selects on DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, against less than a fifth for a window of the same size. The drafter therefore reads a fixed number of positions from anywhere in the context, runs no scoring pass of its own, and needs no place on the prefill instances.</p>
<h2>The numbers, separated by what they measure</h2>
<p>Tencent evaluated DFly2 on three targets, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, GLM-5.3-Flash, and Hy4 flash, against DFlash and DSpark trained on the same recipe. The table below is ByteHerald's compilation of the paper's reported averages, with the three different quantities kept apart, because mixing them is how an 81 percent throughput figure turns into a speed claim it never made.</p>

<p>The end-to-end row is where the honest reading lives. On agentic workloads with long prompts, prefill dominates wall-clock time, so even a large decoding gain compresses into a 1.50x end-to-end average. The throughput row answers a different question: at the same per-user speed, the deployment serves 81 percent more work on the SWE 64K set, and at the same throughput each user decodes 15 to 24 percent faster. For a serving team, throughput at matched latency is the number that sets cost per request. For a user waiting on one answer, the end-to-end row is the number that matters, and it is far smaller.</p>
<h2>Where the gain comes from, and what is unverified</h2>
<p>Tencent's ablation isolates the mechanism. Removing the target's top-k selection, leaving only a near window, is the largest loss in the study, two to six times as costly on long-context workloads as on general ones. Sharing the cache by itself changes accepted length only slightly; its value is removing the prefill, prefix-cache, and concurrency costs of a drafter-owned cache. DFly2's accepted length on agentic workloads stays within 4 percent of its general average on every target, while DSpark's falls 11 to 21 percent, because the target's selection reaches anywhere in the context at a fixed size and the drafter inherits that length behavior.</p>
<p>Every figure here is Tencent's own. The baselines were trained by the same team on the same recipe, which controls for training differences but not for benchmark selection, and no independent reproduction exists yet. The code, weights, and technical report are public in <a href="https://github.com/Tencent/AngelSpec">Tencent's AngelSpec repository</a>, so the claim is checkable in a way most vendor benchmarks are not. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/10/who-grades-graders-ai-benchmark.html">ByteHerald's benchmark credibility audit</a> sets out the checks that separate a reproducible result from a marketing number, and DFly2 passes the first of them by shipping the means to rerun it. Until an outside team publishes serving numbers on production traffic, the 81 percent should be read as a throughput result on two 64K test sets, not as a general speed promise.</p>
