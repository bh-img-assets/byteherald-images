<p>Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh laid out three ways the artificial intelligence boom could end on November 16, 2026, speaking at the Federal Reserve's Productivity and Jobs Task Force Conference in San Francisco, according to <a href="https://www.reuters.com/business/feds-warsh-says-emerging-technologies-will-have-significant-impact-risks-need-be-2026-11-16/">Reuters</a>. In the best case, productivity gains are shared broadly and growth runs above its long-run average. In the worst case, the investment disappoints, stock valuations fall, the economy slows, and unemployment rises. The Nasdaq <a href="https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/world/20261117/nasdaq-sp-500-fall-over-1-in-run-up-to-nvidia-results-anthropic-debut">fell 2.30 percent by the close</a> the same day. Warsh stressed that the three paths are illustrative scenarios drawn up by Fed staff, not forecasts, and he gave no signal about interest rates.</p>

<h2>The three scenarios</h2>
<p>Warsh calls the technology "super intelligence," the term the Trump administration now prefers for AI, and describes it as "an entirely new force in economic life and society." Fed staff have begun modeling what it could do to the economy with simplified frameworks, and Warsh presented three broad possibilities.</p>

<p>The third scenario is the one markets heard loudest, because it describes the exact fear hanging over AI stocks: trillions spent on data centers and chips, and returns that never arrive. Warsh's own framing is careful. He called the outcomes "illustrative scenarios, not forecasts," and said the Fed's job is to prepare for whatever comes next, not to predict which path materializes. "Our role in all this is neither advocacy nor opposition," he said. "Our responsibility is to recognize that the widespread adoption of this technology will have significant impact on the economy, with important implications for monetary policy down the road. The big questions are about magnitude, scope, and timing."</p>

<h2>The words that matter</h2>
<p>Warsh's language is doing two jobs at once. He is describing a technology and staking a national claim about it. "What might set super intelligence apart, as an economic influence, is that it could supercharge idea generation," he said, and added that the cost of satisfying human curiosity is "falling fast, perhaps even trending toward zero." He asked the room, "Can you be a superpower without super intelligence?" and said plainly, "I stress that while I'm not here to offer predictions, I'm not exactly impartial about American leadership in a time so filled with both challenge and possibility."</p>
<p>He also drew a line the industry rarely draws for itself. "Machines that can amass or even exceed human knowledge are still machines. They do not take the place of human judgment, morality, or responsibility. Humans are the designers and the builders, and humans must be the deciders," he said. And he named the risk that does not fit in a growth model: "Super intelligence empowers all, including bad actors, so we are thinking especially of critical infrastructures and their potential vulnerabilities." The "super intelligence" wording itself is now official Fed terminology after an October change following a Trump executive order, not a scientific judgment that today's systems have surpassed human intelligence.</p>
<h2>What Warsh did not say</h2>
<p>Nothing in the prepared remarks touches the next interest rate decision. The Fed held its benchmark rate at 3.75 percent to 4 percent on October 28, 2026, the next decision is due December 8 and 9, and minutes of the October meeting are published November 18. Warsh gave no guidance on any of that, and futures markets still price a quarter-point December rise above 90 percent, according to Reuters. The speech is about the next several years, not the next meeting.</p>
<p>The speech also builds on the Fed's own warning from three days earlier. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/the-fed-says-ai-is-top-risk-to.html">The Fed's Financial Stability Report, published November 13, 2026, found AI the top-cited risk to the financial system</a>, named by 87 percent of the contacts surveyed, up from 50 percent in the spring. Warsh's scenarios are the Fed's attempt to put numbers on that worry. The other half of the worry is the money already committed: <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/the-32-trillion-dollar-ai-debt-nobody.html">about 3.2 trillion dollars in off-balance-sheet AI obligations</a> signed by the five biggest builders, which is the spending the bust scenario would strand. A scenario is only a model, but the debt is a contract, and the two now sit on the same page of the Fed's agenda.</p>
<h2>Why the Fed is modeling this now</h2>
<p>A central bank runs scenarios because its tools are slow and the economy is fast. If AI lifts productivity, the Fed can let growth run hotter without raising rates to fight inflation. If AI displaces workers, the Fed faces rising unemployment while growth looks fine on paper, a combination its standard models handle badly. If the investment busts, the Fed faces falling asset prices and a slowdown at the same time, which is when it usually cuts rates. Each scenario points to a different policy, so the Fed is building the models before it needs them, not after.</p>
<p>The conference itself is part of that preparation. It is the only scheduled public event of the five task forces Warsh set up early in his tenure, and the productivity and jobs task force is chaired by Marc Andreessen, the venture capitalist who has become the technology's most prominent advocate. The day's final session was a fireside chat between Warsh and Andreessen, and Warsh moderated two of the three panels and the closing fireside chat. The task force's other members include Chad Jones, a Stanford economics professor now working with Anthropic's internal think tank. The Fed is asking the people building the technology to explain what it will do to the economy, while telling them it will not take their word for it.</p>
