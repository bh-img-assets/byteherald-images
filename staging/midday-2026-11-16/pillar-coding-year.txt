<p>One year ago, in November 2025, Anthropic released Claude Opus 4.5 and a developer named Peter Steinberger launched OpenClaw, and the job of writing software changed in a way that has not reversed since. Today, an AI agent finishes 41 percent of real freelance jobs on a measured benchmark, a frontier lab says its models now lead a quarter of its own research work, and the flagship coding model costs about a quarter of what it cost a year ago. The table below is every dated turning point in that year, November 2025 to November 2026, each with its source. It is the first time the whole year has been laid out in one place.</p>

<h2>The year in one paragraph</h2>
<p>Three things happened at once. The models got cheaper and better: the flagship Opus line fell from 15 dollars per million input tokens to 4 dollars, while its SWE-bench Verified score rose from above 80 percent to 87.6 percent. The work moved out of the chat box and into the loop: a bash loop named after a Simpsons character became an official Anthropic plugin, and by August 2026 Claude was leading 26 percent of Anthropic's own research tasks. And the tools consolidated: Cursor was bought by SpaceX for an implied 60 billion dollars, Windsurf became Devin Desktop, and the guardrails arrived last, in the form of refusals, a shelved model, and a rewritten usage policy. The year did not produce one winner. It produced a new way of working, and then it produced the rules for that way of working.</p>

<p>Three rows in the year deserve a closer look, because they are the ones people quote wrong. In December 2025, the community settled on a claim that models could handle 95 percent of coding tasks. That figure was never measured. It was a mood, stated as a number, and it is included here only as a marker of the hype, not as a fact. In November 2026, The Information reported that GPT-6.2 could ship as early as November 6 in Astra, Sol and Cyber versions. As of November 13, it had not shipped, and OpenAI had not confirmed a name, a price or a date. A reported date is not a release. And the SWE-bench jump from 80.8 percent to 87.6 percent between February and April 2026 is reported by a comparison site, not by Anthropic's own launch post, so it belongs in the table with that caveat attached.</p>

<h2>What the benchmarks actually did</h2>
<p>Two benchmarks tell the year's story better than any launch post, because they measure work rather than promise it. SWE-bench Verified asks models to resolve real GitHub issues. Opus 4.5 crossed 80 percent in November 2025, Opus 4.6 reached 80.8 percent in February 2026, and Opus 4.7 reached 87.6 percent in April 2026. That is a 7 point rise in five months on a test where each point is a real bug fixed. The Remote Labor Index asks models to finish real freelance jobs from Upwork, graded by humans. It started near 2.5 percent, and by November 10, 2026, Fable 5.2 finished 41.30 percent of 240 projects, with Opus 5.5 at 21.25 percent and GPT-6 Astra at 20.83 percent. The two benchmarks do not measure the same thing, and they should never be compared to each other. SWE-bench measures fixing existing code. The Remote Labor Index measures finishing a job a client paid for. The honest reading is that both rose fast, and that the freelance test rose further, because it measures the whole job, not one issue at a time.</p>
<p>The third measurement is the one the labs report about themselves. METR's independent assessment, published November 5, 2026, put the acceleration of AI research and development at 1.9 times, with a wide uncertainty range. Anthropic's own R and D Automation Index, published September 17, 2026, reported that Claude led 26 percent of the company's research work as of August, up from under 1 percent in February, with over 90 percent at the collaborates level or above and nothing fully autonomous. Both numbers are self-reported in different ways, METR by an outside evaluator with redacted evidence, Anthropic by the lab about itself with a model judging models, and both should be read with those limits attached. What they agree on is the direction: the work of building AI is now substantially done by AI.</p>
<h2>Three things the year proved</h2>
<p>First, price collapsed while capability rose. The flagship Opus input price fell from 15 dollars per million tokens in August 2025 to 5 dollars in November 2025 and 4 dollars in November 2026, while SWE-bench Verified rose from above 80 percent to 87.6 percent. The usual trade, better or cheaper, did not apply. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/ai-coding-costs-by-framework-12-builds.html">Our November 1 test of 12 frameworks on the same build</a> found the same pattern at the tool level: the cheapest build cost 2.67 dollars and the most expensive 4.97 dollars, a gap of less than two dollars for the same app.</p>
<p>Second, the loop beat the prompt. The most consequential technique of the year was not a model but a bash loop: run the agent, check the output against a test, and run it again until it passes. Huntley demonstrated it in June 2025, Anthropic shipped it as a plugin in December, and by January 2026 it was the default way serious users ran agents. The models improved all year, but the loop is what turned a good model into an overnight worker.</p>
<p>Third, the guardrails came after the capability. The year opened with agents that would do almost anything and closed with agents that refuse to rebuild a game, a usage policy with a new intellectual property clause, and a frontier model shelved before launch for failing safety tests. <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/the-refusal-ledger-what-ai-coding.html">Our refusal ledger dated that turn across eight cases from October 13 to November 12</a>, and <a href="https://www.byteherald.com/2026/11/the-coding-agent-trust-ledger-8.html">our trust ledger counted eight incidents in the same window</a>, three of them in seven days. The rules were written in response to the incidents, not before them, and that sequence is the year's most important lesson for anyone deploying an agent in production.</p>
<h2>How to judge the next release</h2>
<p>The year taught one practical skill: reading a launch announcement like a skeptic. Five questions cover it. What benchmark is cited, and who measured it, the vendor or an independent evaluator. What the price actually is per million tokens, in and out, and whether the quoted price is a promotion that expires. What changed from the previous version, in numbers, not adjectives. What the model refuses to do, and whether the refusal is written into the usage policy or only implied by a demo. And what the model costs to run for a real job, not a single prompt, because the bill is where the loop meets the budget. A release that answers all five is worth trying. A release that answers none of them is a press release, and the year had plenty of those too.</p>
